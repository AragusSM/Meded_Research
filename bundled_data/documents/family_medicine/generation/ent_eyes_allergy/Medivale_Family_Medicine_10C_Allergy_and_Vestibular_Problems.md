# Medivale Family Medicine

## 10C. Allergy and Vestibular Problems

Localize nasal symptoms, swelling, and dizziness by mechanism, time course, and threatened function. Rhinorrhea does not prove allergy; swelling without hives does not prove hereditary disease; movement-aggravated dizziness does not prove BPPV.

Rhinitis/anaphylaxis parameters, WAO angioedema guidance, US epinephrine labels, and vestibular pathways guide care. Airway procedures, IV rescue infusions, and immunotherapy require supervised protocols.

### Recognize the rhinitis phenotype

Allergen-specific IgE triggers mast-cell mediator release after re-exposure, producing itch, sneezing, and watery discharge; later inflammation sustains congestion. Match symptoms to seasonal, animal, or indoor exposure. Positive testing without matching symptoms indicates sensitization, not necessarily clinical allergy. [AAAAI rhinitis overview](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/hay-fever-rhinitis)

Assess nasal/eye symptoms, sleep, school/work function, and asthma. Review home/work irritants, smoke/vaping, fragrances, temperature, drugs, and decongestants. Allergic/nonallergic mechanisms may coexist. Examine patency, turbinates, septum, discharge, and lesions; unilateral obstruction, bleeding, mass, or unusual watery discharge warrants structural assessment.

Select skin testing or serum allergen-specific IgE when the diagnosis is uncertain, treatment has failed, or identifying an allergen would change avoidance or immunotherapy decisions. Choose exposures that fit the history; a broad panel can create misleading labels. Routine food-allergy testing is not part of an ordinary rhinitis evaluation. Persistent allergic rhinitis generally responds best to an **intranasal corticosteroid**; an intranasal antihistamine can be added when needed. For nonallergic rhinitis, an intranasal antihistamine or corticosteroid may be appropriate. [Rhinitis practice parameter](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Rhinitis-2020-A-practice-parameter-update.pdf)

### Make treatment practical and safe

Target avoidance to relevant exposures: bedroom/pets, bedding, moisture/mold, and pests. Coordinated measures may be needed; a mattress cover alone may fail. For occupational symptoms, assess reproducible improvement away and associated wheeze. Incidental sensitization alone does not justify abandoning employment or removing a pet. [AAAAI indoor-allergen measures](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr)

| Treatment role | Useful approach | Safety or interpretation point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistent nasal inflammation | Regular intranasal corticosteroid with demonstrated technique | Review local irritation, bleeding, product age limits, and adherence |
| Prominent itch/sneezing or incomplete control | Intranasal antihistamine; selected second-generation oral antihistamine | Sedation varies; adding another oral drug is not always the best next step |
| Symptom relief and secretion clearance | Saline irrigation as an adjunct | Use safe water and clean equipment; irrigation does not replace anti-inflammatory treatment |
| Selected refractory allergic rhinitis | Consider montelukast only after inadequate response or intolerance to alternatives | Discuss the boxed warning for serious neuropsychiatric effects |

Demonstrate regular spray use aimed laterally away from the septum, avoiding forceful sniffing. Persistent bleeding warrants mucosal/technique review. Check childhood product eligibility, growth, and cumulative inhaled/topical steroid exposure. Pregnancy choices use product-specific safety, not obsolete FDA letter categories.

**Fluticasone propionate 50 mcg per spray** is an OTC option: from age 12, two sprays per nostril daily for the first week, then one or two daily as needed. At ages 4-11, use one per nostril daily with supervision; do not use below four. Review prolonged child use/growth. Reassess no benefit after seven days, severe/frequent bleeding, or visual change. Check ritonavir/other interactions. Other products have different concentrations/ages. [Fluticasone allergy labeling](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=044a06a1-74fc-43ff-b136-37ad41220941&type=display)

Irrigate with **distilled, sterile, or appropriately boiled and cooled water**, not untreated tap or ordinary drinking water. Clean/dry the device. Substantial immune impairment or recent nasal procedures warrants clinician advice. [FDA irrigation safety](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/rinsing-your-sinuses-neti-pots-safe)

Avoid sedating first-generation antihistamines with driving, machinery, or fall risk. Decongestants do not control inflammation; assess cardiovascular, urinary, sleep, and pregnancy risks. Prolonged topical vasoconstrictors worsen congestion. Oral phenylephrine lacks demonstrated nasal-decongestant efficacy; that finding does not establish removal of every phenylephrine product from sale. [FDA oral phenylephrine review](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-proposes-ending-use-oral-phenylephrine-otc-monograph-nasal-decongestant-active-ingredient-after)

Montelukast is not a routine first choice for mild allergic rhinitis. Discuss possible mood, behavior, and sleep changes before prescribing, including serious mental-health effects. New concerning symptoms warrant stopping the drug and contacting the clinician promptly. Coexisting asthma does not remove the need for a deliberate risk-benefit discussion. Routine oral or depot systemic corticosteroids are also not a sound maintenance strategy for uncomplicated rhinitis. [FDA montelukast boxed warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/fda-requires-boxed-warning-about-serious-mental-health-side-effects-asthma-and-allergy-drug)

**Clinical Case.** A 38-year-old bus driver has springtime nasal/eye symptoms and disrupted sleep. An OTC antihistamine helps sneezing but impairs driving. Address safety, switch to appropriate nasal therapy, demonstrate technique, and review alertness, sleep, and symptom response.

Allergen immunotherapy can reduce symptoms and modify response when avoidance/medicines fail or the patient prefers it. Confirm clinically relevant sensitization and discuss time, cost, route, and systemic-reaction risk. Subcutaneous and sublingual prescriptions differ; durable benefit is possible but not guaranteed. [AAO-HNS immunotherapy overview](https://www.entnet.org/resource/new-clinical-practice-guideline-provides-evidence-based-recommendations-for-immunotherapy-for-inhalant-allergy/)

The 2024 guideline advises **not initiating immunotherapy during pregnancy, with uncontrolled asthma, or when injectable epinephrine cannot be tolerated**. Established maintenance treatment in pregnancy is a separate specialist decision, not permission to start escalation. Assess asthma control before initiation and during injection treatment; do not use immunotherapy to compensate for unstable lower-airway disease. The treating service must be able to recognize and manage anaphylaxis. [Current immunotherapy recommendations](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ohn.648)

### A sting can cause local inflammation without infection

Remove a retained stinger promptly, clean the site, elevate the limb, and use a cold compress and suitable oral antihistamine/analgesia. A large local reaction can enlarge over 24-48 hours and take several days to settle. Warmth and swelling alone do not establish cellulitis; antibiotics are unnecessary without evidence of secondary infection. A short systemic steroid course is sometimes considered for severe local symptoms, but controlled evidence is limited. Respiratory, circulatory, or other systemic involvement changes the decision to the anaphylaxis pathway below. [AAAAI/ACAAI sting-reaction guidance](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Stinging-insect-hypersensitivity-2016.pdf)

After sting anaphylaxis, arrange venom-specific IgE assessment, **venom immunotherapy** evaluation, and epinephrine access. Isolated large local reactions generally do not need immunotherapy; positive venom tests alone are insufficient. Unidentified inflamed lesions do not establish spider bite/MRSA infection (08C). [Venom immunotherapy selection](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Stinging-insect-hypersensitivity-2016.pdf)

### Recognize anaphylaxis before every feature appears

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly evolving systemic hypersensitivity reaction that can threaten breathing or circulation. Look for a plausible exposure followed by combinations of mucosal/skin findings, respiratory compromise, hypotension, collapse, or significant gastrointestinal symptoms. Skin findings may be absent. Sudden bronchospasm, laryngeal symptoms, or circulatory compromise after a known or highly probable allergen can justify treatment without hives or a complete multisystem checklist. A diagnostic definition helps organize evidence; it is not a prerequisite for giving epinephrine when anaphylaxis is suspected. [2023 anaphylaxis practice parameter](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Anaphylaxis-Practice-Paramaters-2023.pdf)

Reassess isolated stable rash for progression. Identify exposure, timing, prior reactions, exercise/cofactors, and asthma without delaying rescue. Epinephrine supports vascular tone through alpha effects and cardiac/bronchodilator function through beta effects; food-associated wheeze with faintness requires more than inhaled bronchodilation.

The **WAO IM pathway uses epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg of 1 mg/mL solution, maximum 0.5 mg per dose**, into the mid-anterolateral thigh. Repeat at 5-15-minute intervals while reassessing and arranging emergency care. Specify mg and mg/mL; concentration changes alter volume. This is not an IV dose. Use an available prescribed device promptly rather than delaying for custom preparation. [WAO anaphylaxis guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7607509/)

**Example: IM epinephrine.** A 16-kg child develops wheeze, lip swelling, and lethargy after food. Dose: 16 x 0.01 = **0.16 mg**, or **0.16 mL of 1 mg/mL** IM solution. Do not give IV or delay an available prescribed 0.15-mg device. Treat immediately, activate emergency help, and reassess.

### Support circulation and avoid treatment substitutions

Call for emergency assistance, stop the suspected exposure when feasible, and assess airway, breathing, and circulation repeatedly. Keep the patient lying down with legs supported when tolerated; a person who suddenly stands or walks can deteriorate despite appearing improved. If breathing is difficult, permit a supported position that facilitates breathing without abrupt upright movement. Use a left-lateral position in pregnancy. Provide oxygen, monitoring, vascular access, and intravenous fluid resuscitation as indicated through the emergency protocol. Pregnancy is not a reason to withhold indicated epinephrine. [ASCIA acute management](https://allergy.org.au/hp/anaphylaxis/acute-management-guidelines)

Persistent compromise after appropriate repeated intramuscular treatment requires experienced resuscitation support, including consideration of a monitored epinephrine infusion. This is not a reason to give an unsupervised intravenous bolus. Inhaled bronchodilators can supplement treatment of residual bronchospasm but do not reverse shock or upper-airway edema. Beta-blocker-associated refractory reactions may require expert use of additional therapies; stopping chronic cardiovascular drugs automatically is not an appropriate substitute for individualized review.

Antihistamines may relieve persistent cutaneous symptoms **after urgent resuscitation needs are addressed**. They do not correct airway obstruction or hypotension. Corticosteroids are not routine first-line treatment and have not reliably prevented biphasic anaphylaxis; using them must not postpone epinephrine. A transient improvement in itching does not establish cardiovascular recovery. Record the timing of symptoms, epinephrine doses, response, vital signs, and recurrence so the receiving team can distinguish a continuing reaction from a new deterioration. [Resuscitation Council UK emergency guidance](https://www.resus.org.uk/sites/default/files/2021-05/Emergency%20Treatment%20of%20Anaphylaxis%20May%202021_0.pdf)

Obtain acute tryptase ideally within two hours and a separate recovery baseline without delaying treatment. A rise above **1.2 x baseline + 2 ng/mL** supports mast-cell activation; normal levels, especially after food reactions, do not exclude anaphylaxis. Severe/recurrent/unexplained episodes warrant possible mast-cell disorder assessment.

Observation should be risk-stratified rather than a universal fixed 24-hour rule. Severe or protracted reactions, repeated epinephrine requirements, significant respiratory compromise, prior biphasic reactions, ongoing allergen absorption, and difficulty reaching emergency care favor longer monitoring. Use the local emergency/discharge pathway; selected short-observation criteria cannot be applied by considering only that the patient now feels well. NICE's final 2026 guidance uses explicit resolution, treatment-response, supervision, and device-readiness conditions rather than one duration for every patient. [NICE current observation framework](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng258/resources/anaphylaxis-assessment-and-referral-after-emergency-treatment-pdf-66144023784133)

### Prescribe a usable rescue plan

Before discharge, provide trigger-avoidance advice, allergy referral, and a written action plan. Demonstrate the actual device/trainer and use teach-back. Ensure two accessible doses, correct storage/expiry, and school/work plans. Recurrent reactions warrant exposure/diagnostic review.

| Current US device example | Labeled weight group | Dose and route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AUVI-Q | 7.5 to less than 15 kg | 0.1 mg epinephrine injection |
| AUVI-Q | 15 to less than 30 kg | 0.15 mg epinephrine injection |
| AUVI-Q | 30 kg or more | 0.3 mg epinephrine injection |
| neffy | 15 to less than 30 kg | 1 mg intranasal epinephrine |
| neffy | 30 kg or more | 2 mg intranasal epinephrine |

These are **product-specific doses**, not milligram-equivalent routes or universal device bands. Inject AUVI-Q into the outer thigh; intramuscular delivery is guideline-preferred. Stabilize a young child's leg and follow the device instructions. Its label permits a second device starting five minutes later if symptoms fail to improve or worsen. Never inject into a finger, buttock, or vein. [AUVI-Q current label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5f40988b-8365-4f7b-84d1-b88e053725cf)

The current US neffy label uses weight eligibility of at least 15 kg. If needed, administer a second new spray into the **same nostril starting five minutes after the first**; do not prime, reuse, or instruct the patient to sniff the dose. Nasal structural abnormalities can affect absorption, so an alternative route may be appropriate. Carry two devices and obtain emergency assistance according to the action plan; multiple doses do not make severe or persistent symptoms safe to manage alone. [neffy current label](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a1758142-a905-401d-8961-05829f51023a)

### Angioedema: assess the airway, then the mediator

Angioedema is deeper tissue swelling, often involving lips, tongue, face, extremities, genital tissue, or bowel. A changing voice, tongue/floor-of-mouth swelling, stridor, drooling, or trouble swallowing requires urgent airway-capable assessment. The amount visible outside the mouth does not reliably show the extent inside. Anticipate progression and seek experienced airway help rather than waiting until oxygen saturation falls. When anaphylaxis remains plausible, give epinephrine while arranging airway care; uncertainty about the mediator must not delay lifesaving treatment.

| Pattern | Mechanism to consider | Useful next distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rapid swelling with itch/hives or a systemic reaction | Often mast-cell mediated | Look for respiratory/circulatory involvement and a relevant exposure |
| Nonpruritic tongue/lip swelling during ACE-inhibitor treatment | Bradykinin-mediated medication reaction | It can begin after years of previously tolerated therapy |
| Recurrent nonurticarial swelling, painful abdominal attacks, or suggestive family history | Hereditary or acquired bradykinin disorder | Absence of a family history does not exclude hereditary disease |

These patterns overlap: mast-cell angioedema can lack wheals, and incidental rash does not establish the swelling mechanism. Review ACE inhibitors, neprilysin-related therapy, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, estrogen, and nonprescription drugs; mechanisms differ. Record duration, sites, abdominal symptoms, response, and safe photographs. [International angioedema consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11889046/)

ACE-inhibitor angioedema is principally an airway-management problem. Stop the ACE inhibitor and avoid the **entire ACE-inhibitor class permanently**; another brand is not a safe retry. There is no laboratory test that confirms an acute ACE-inhibitor episode. Routine antihistamines, steroids, or epinephrine have insufficient evidence for a known isolated bradykinin reaction, and evidence does not establish a routine preferred drug rescue such as icatibant, tranexamic acid, or plasma for every case. This limitation does not apply to withholding epinephrine when anaphylaxis is possible. [AAEM ACE-inhibitor angioedema statement](https://www.aaem.org/statements/ed-patients-angioedema-secondary/)

**Clinical Case.** After eight years of lisinopril, a 67-year-old develops progressive tongue swelling without itch, hives, wheeze, or new food exposure. Arrange emergency airway care, stop lisinopril, document permanent class avoidance, and select another BP regimen after stabilization. Prior tolerance does not exclude ACE-inhibitor angioedema.

### Recurrent swelling needs a specific diagnosis

C1-inhibitor deficiency/dysfunction permits excess bradykinin and vascular permeability, causing airway or severe abdominal attacks without external swelling. Consider acquired deficiency with later onset, absent family history, or hematologic/autoimmune disease. Recurrent abdominal attacks warrant assessment beyond anxiety or an isolated surgical diagnosis.

The current WAO pathway favors a **functional C1-inhibitor assay** as an initial test where a reliable specialized laboratory is available; pragmatic evaluation can combine C4, C1-inhibitor antigen, and function. C4 alone is not an exclusion test. Type I disease has reduced antigen and function; type II has impaired function despite normal or increased antigen. Abnormal or discordant results need specialist interpretation and confirmation. Add C1q and selected acquired-disease testing when appropriate. Normal C1-inhibitor testing does not exclude all hereditary forms; specialist genetic evaluation may be indicated. [Current WAO hereditary angioedema guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13184495/)

Care separates early **on-demand attack treatment** from long-term prevention. Patients need an individualized rescue plan and ready access to effective therapy, including instructions for airway attacks despite use of rescue medication. C1-inhibitor products and other pathway-specific treatments have different routes, ages, and regional approvals; one product's eligibility cannot be applied to the entire class. Procedure planning, pregnancy, estrogen exposure, attack burden, and patient preferences influence prevention. Plasma-derived C1 inhibitor is an important preferred option during pregnancy within specialist care.

Sebetralstat is FDA-approved for acute hereditary angioedema attacks from **12 years and older**, with CYP3A-interaction and hepatic restrictions. This oral option does not treat all facial swelling or replace emergency airway care. [FDA sebetralstat prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/219301s000lbl.pdf)

### Dizziness: establish timing and triggers

Establish continuous versus episodic dizziness, duration, onset triggers, and symptom-free intervals. Distinguish a **trigger starting an attack** from movement aggravating continuous symptoms. Review gait/falls, hearing, headache, focal symptoms, drugs, orthostasis, palpitations, glucose, dehydration, and intoxication; vestibular disease, presyncope, and disequilibrium can coexist. [Timing-and-triggers diagnostic framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4522574/)

| Timing pattern | Important possibilities | Targeted assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brief episodes started by a particular head position | BPPV; central positional mimics | Positional examination when safe, with attention to the nystagmus pattern |
| Spontaneous recurrent episodes | Vestibular migraine, Meniere disease, TIA, arrhythmia | Neurologic/hearing/cardiovascular history and examination matched to the episode |
| New continuous vertigo with nausea, imbalance, and movement intolerance | Acute unilateral vestibulopathy or posterior-circulation stroke | Acute vestibular assessment by an appropriately trained clinician |

Balance integrates vestibular, visual, and somatosensory input; poor vision, neuropathy, and sedatives compound fall risk. Examine vitals/orthostasis, eye movements, hearing, cranial nerves, coordination, and gait when safe. Inability to walk independently is a major finding. [NIDCD balance disorders](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/balance-disorders)

### Do not use a normal CT as a stroke exclusion test

The GRACE-3 pathway addresses adults with acute dizziness in emergency care. In an acute vestibular syndrome **with nystagmus**, a clinician trained in HINTS can use the head impulse, nystagmus, and test-of-skew examination to guide central-versus-peripheral assessment. HINTS is not a universal screening test for every dizzy patient, an asymptomatic patient between attacks, or typical brief positional episodes. Without nystagmus, gait severity and the broader neurologic assessment matter; do not manufacture a reassuring HINTS result.

Head impulse tests the vestibulo-ocular reflex; corrective saccades suggest dysfunction but do not alone exclude stroke. Direction-changing gaze nystagmus or vertical realignment on alternate cover testing raises central concern. Any central component or new hearing asymmetry is concerning; interpretation requires the appropriate syndrome and training. [Barany Society vascular vertigo consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9249306/)

A central or equivocal examination requires stroke-capable evaluation and appropriate MRI/MRA. Do not routinely use noncontrast CT to distinguish peripheral vertigo from posterior-circulation ischemia. Suspected TIA may require CTA or MRA. A focal deficit, new severe headache/neck pain, marked truncal instability, or acute hearing change should increase concern. If the setting lacks the necessary examination expertise or urgent imaging pathway, escalate care rather than relying on a partially learned bedside maneuver. [SAEM GRACE-3 recommendations](https://www.saem.org/publications/grace/grace-3)

**Clinical Case.** A 61-year-old has ten hours of continuous vertigo, vomiting, severe gait impairment, and new unilateral hearing difficulty. Movement worsens symptoms, but rest does not end them. A normal CT does not establish BPPV; arrange urgent central/vascular reassessment rather than repeated positional maneuvers.

### BPPV is a mechanical positional disorder

In BPPV, displaced otoconia allow head position to inappropriately stimulate a semicircular canal. The typical history is brief attacks when rolling in bed, lying back, or looking upward, with relative relief between attacks. Posterior-canal disease produces characteristic **torsional, upbeating nystagmus** during the Dix-Hallpike test; a compatible history alone is not equivalent to a documented positive examination. Persistent or atypical findings require reconsideration rather than repeated reassurance. [AAO-HNS BPPV guideline](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1177/0194599816689667)

When the history fits but positional examination shows horizontal nystagmus or no typical posterior-canal finding, consider a supine roll test or referral for lateral-canal evaluation. Treatment must match the affected canal. Assess cervical mobility, instability, vascular/neurologic concerns, and the patient's ability to tolerate positioning before testing or treatment; modified examination or specialist help may be necessary. Routine imaging is not needed for a characteristic presentation without inconsistent findings. [AAO-HNS BPPV clinical pathway](https://www.entnet.org/quality-practice/quality-products/clinical-practice-guidelines/bppv/)

Treat confirmed posterior-canal BPPV with a canalith repositioning procedure such as the Epley maneuver, performed or taught appropriately. A repositioning procedure moves debris; a sedating vestibular suppressant does not. Routine chronic antihistamine or benzodiazepine treatment and routine postprocedure positional restrictions are not recommended. Reassess within one month and evaluate persistent symptoms for unresolved BPPV or another peripheral/central disorder. Recurrence warrants reassessment, not an assumption that every future dizzy spell has the same cause. [AAO-HNS BPPV action-statement summary](https://www.entnet.org/resource/aao-hnsf-updated-cpg-bppv-press-release-fact-sheet/)

**Clinical Case.** A 54-year-old has 20-second attacks when rolling right, with comfort at rest. Safe examination confirms the posterior-canal pattern; perform repositioning and arrange response review/fall advice. Future continuous symptoms, hearing loss, or neurologic signs require a new assessment.

### Vestibular neuritis requires exclusion of competing causes

Acute unilateral vestibulopathy, often called vestibular neuritis, produces sustained vertigo from unilateral vestibular dysfunction. The Barany Society criteria include a compatible acute course, direction-fixed peripheral-pattern nystagmus, a unilateral vestibulo-ocular reflex deficit, and absence of acute central neurologic or auditory signs, after other causes are excluded. Established acute symptoms last at least 24 hours; an evolving syndrome may be assessed earlier. New hearing loss should not be casually folded into the same label. A preceding viral illness is possible but does not prove a specific viral cause. [Barany Society diagnostic consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9661346/)

Provide hydration and limited symptom control when necessary, then prioritize safe mobilization and recovery of function. Prolonged vestibular suppression can interfere with compensation and increases sedation/fall concerns. Early corticosteroids are a shared-decision option in selected patients within the first three days under GRACE-3, with **very low-certainty evidence**, not a mandatory treatment for every presumed case. Diagnostic uncertainty should be resolved before discussing marginal treatment benefits.

For persistent peripheral vestibular hypofunction, offer individualized gaze-stability, balance, and functional rehabilitation. Track tolerance, adherence, confidence, falls, and daily tasks. Coexisting later positional symptoms may need separate BPPV assessment. [Updated vestibular rehabilitation guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8920012/)

### Meniere disease, vestibular migraine, and longitudinal care

Definite Meniere disease requires at least two spontaneous vertigo episodes lasting **20 minutes to 12 hours**, audiometrically documented low- to mid-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, fluctuating affected-ear symptoms, and exclusion of another cause. Probable disease allows episodes lasting 20 minutes to 24 hours with fluctuating aural symptoms without the same documented-hearing requirement. Obtain an audiogram and assess for vestibular migraine; fullness alone is not diagnostic. Selected asymmetric sensorineural loss may warrant MRI rather than routine vestibular testing or electrocochleography. [AAO-HNS Meniere guideline](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1177/0194599820909438)

Discuss dietary/lifestyle measures, a limited course of vestibular suppressants during attacks, and the uncertain benefit of maintenance medicines. Diuretics or betahistine are options in the AAO-HNS pathway, not obligatory proven cures. Persistent active disease despite noninvasive care may lead to specialist intratympanic treatment; treatment choices can trade vertigo control against hearing or balance risks. Rehabilitation helps chronic imbalance rather than terminating an acute attack. [AAO-HNS management framework](https://www.entnet.org/quality-practice/quality-products/clinical-practice-guidelines/menieres-disease/)

Track hearing and balance, communication, driving, falls, work, mood, and support. Symptoms alone do not establish one certain mechanism of inner-ear fluid dysfunction. New sudden hearing loss follows the urgent ear pathway, not presumed routine fluctuation. [NIDCD Meniere disease overview](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/menieres-disease)

Vestibular migraine is another episodic syndrome. The ICHD-3 framework requires at least five moderate/severe vestibular episodes lasting **5 minutes to 72 hours**, a migraine history, migraine features during at least half the episodes, and exclusion of a better explanation. Headache need not accompany every attack. Migraine features during a dizzy spell do not independently rule out another vestibular disorder; examine the entire pattern, particularly documented hearing change. [IHS vestibular migraine criteria](https://ichd-3.org/appendix/a1-migraine/a1-6-episodic-syndromes-that-may-be-associated-with-migraine/a1-6-6-vestibular-migraine/)

**Clinical Case.** A 43-year-old has three spontaneous two-hour attacks, unilateral tinnitus/fluctuating hearing, and compatible sensorineural loss. Arrange Meniere evaluation rather than repeated Epley maneuvers. Assess migraine history and monitor hearing/treatment harms. New focal deficits require emergency reassessment despite established ear disease.

Follow-up should confirm diagnostic fit, usable treatment, and improving function. Provide clear instructions for symptoms that require renewed assessment.
