# Medivale Family Medicine

## 10A. Ear, Nose, and Throat Complaints

Localize ear pain, congestion, or sore throat by compartment, time course, and threats to hearing, vision, swallowing, or airway. Red membranes, tonsillar exudate, and colored mucus alone establish neither AOM, GAS, nor bacterial sinusitis. Explain treatment versus observation and expected recovery.

CDC, IDSA, AAO-HNSF, and ADA pathways guide condition-specific decisions; pediatric doses below use the named UCSF regimen. Adjust for age, organ function, formulation, pregnancy, allergy, and resistance. See 10B-10C for eyes, allergy, and vestibular disorders.

### Examine the ear before naming the infection

Ask about onset, respiratory illness, swimming, instrumentation, hearing, drainage, pressure, surgery, and tubes. Examine pinna, tragus, canal, membrane, and mastoid. Clear debris only when safe and within capability. Pneumatic otoscopy/tympanometry assesses effusion; improve a poor view before diagnosing from redness.

Eustachian-tube dysfunction after infection can leave fluid without acute bacterial disease. Water or mechanical injury instead disrupts the external-canal barrier; treatment routes differ. With unexplained otalgia, consider dental, TMJ, or pharyngeal referral.

| Finding | Likely compartment/problem | Treatment implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulging tympanic membrane with middle-ear effusion and acute symptoms | Acute otitis media (AOM) | Analgesia for all; antibiotic or eligible observation according to age, severity, and follow-up |
| Middle-ear fluid without acute inflammatory illness | Otitis media with effusion (OME) | Follow hearing and persistence; do not prescribe another antibiotic merely for residual fluid |
| Inflamed canal with pain on tragal/pinna manipulation | Acute otitis externa (AOE) | Usually topical treatment; membrane integrity and host factors modify selection |

For AOM, CDC requires substantial bulging or new otorrhea not caused by externa, or mild bulging with recent ear pain or marked membrane erythema. **Do not diagnose AOM without middle-ear effusion.** Crying, fever, and a viral illness can redden the membrane without creating this syndrome. Amoxicillin is generally first-line when a child needs antibiotics and has not received it recently; recent amoxicillin, purulent conjunctivitis, or recurrent amoxicillin-unresponsive disease favors additional beta-lactamase coverage. Do not use prophylactic antibiotics for recurrent AOM. [CDC pediatric AOM diagnosis and selection](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/pediatric-outpatient.html)

### Observation is an active AOM plan

For children six through 23 months, non-severe unilateral AOM without otorrhea can be observed when reliable reassessment and treatment are available. At age two or older, selected non-severe unilateral or bilateral disease can be observed. Bilateral disease below age two, otorrhea, significant illness, or severe symptoms generally favors antibiotics. Severe features include moderate/severe ear pain, pain lasting at least 48 hours, or temperature at least 39 C. Infants below six months, immune compromise, craniofacial abnormalities, and complications need a separate assessment rather than automatic application of these observation criteria.

Provide analgesia and arrange review or a safety-net prescription for worsening/no improvement over **48-72 hours**. Observation requires clear instructions, treatment access, and responsibility for follow-up; resolve access barriers at the initial visit. [AAP AOM observation framework](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/131/3/e964/30912)

For a suitable child requiring treatment, the selected **UCSF regimen** is **amoxicillin 45 mg/kg per oral dose twice daily, maximum 1,000 mg per dose**. This is 90 mg/kg/day before the dose ceiling, not 90 mg/kg per dose. In that pathway, beta-lactam duration is ten days below age two or at any age with severe illness, seven days at ages two to five with non-severe disease, and five days above age five with non-severe disease. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is calculated by the amoxicillin component; products with different clavulanate ratios are not interchangeable volume for volume. Verify kidney function, reaction history, recent therapy, and local dosing/formulation policy. [UCSF pediatric AOM treatment and duration](https://idmp.ucsf.edu/content/acute-otitis-media)

**Example: AOM dosing.** A six-year-old weighing 24 kg has AOM, temperature 39.2 C, and severe persistent pain. Amoxicillin 24 x 45 = 1,080 mg, capped by UCSF at **1,000 mg per dose twice daily**. At 400 mg per 5 mL (80 mg/mL), 1,000/80 = **12.5 mL per dose**. Severe illness supports ten days despite age. Confirm bottle concentration, measuring device, and instructions.

New postauricular swelling, a protruding auricle, persistent marked illness, neurologic signs, or severe headache raises concern for mastoid or intracranial complications. Reassess treatment failure rather than simply replacing one oral antibiotic repeatedly. Acute mastoiditis needs urgent ENT assessment, usually parenteral therapy and evaluation for drainage or other source control. [UCSF complicated ear/head-neck infection care](https://idmp.ucsf.edu/guidelines-empiric-therapy-categories/respiratory-head-and-neck-infections)

### Effusion, recurrent infection, and hearing follow-up

OME is fluid without the acute infection syndrome. Hearing may be muffled even when pain and fever have resolved. In an otherwise low-risk child, observe for **three months** from onset, or from diagnosis if onset is unknown. Obtain an age-appropriate hearing test if fluid persists at least three months, or sooner for an at-risk child. Baseline hearing, developmental, sensory, cognitive, or craniofacial problems can make even a shorter period important. Reevaluate chronic OME at three-to-six-month intervals until resolution or a hearing/structural concern changes management. Antibiotics, systemic or nasal steroids, antihistamines, and decongestants are not routine OME treatment. Treat a coexisting nasal disorder on its own merits. [AAO-HNSF OME pathway](https://www.entnet.org/resource/aao-hnsf-updated-cpg-ome-press-release-fact-sheet/)

Recurrent AOM is at least three documented episodes in six months, or four in twelve months including one in the last six months. The 2022 pathway recommends against tubes without effusion at assessment and supports offering them with effusion. Bilateral OME for at least three months with hearing difficulty and developmental risk also informs candidacy. Discuss benefits, procedural/anesthetic risks, drainage, and follow-up; later symptoms remain possible. [AAO-HNSF 2022 tube recommendations](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1177/01945998211065661)

Persistent unilateral/unexplained adult effusion warrants assessment for obstruction, especially with neck mass, nasal symptoms, or asymmetric hearing loss. Persistent drainage, perforation, or abnormal membrane also requires referral despite treatment of apparent infection. [NICE adult hearing-loss referral criteria](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng98/chapter/recommendations)

### Otitis externa: use the canal route and check the membrane

Diffuse AOE typically causes canal pain, itch, swelling, discharge, and tenderness when the tragus or pinna is moved. Distinguish dermatitis, a focal furuncle, fungal disease, foreign body, perforated middle-ear infection, and referred pain. For uncomplicated diffuse disease, use appropriate topical therapy and analgesia rather than routine oral antibiotics. Explain administration, keeping the treated ear upward long enough for delivery, and avoiding further instrumentation or water exposure. Obstruction can require trained aural cleaning or a wick. **Known or suspected perforation or a tube requires a non-ototoxic preparation.** Reassess lack of response over 48-72 hours, including diagnosis and whether drops reach the target. [AAO-HNSF AOE recommendations](https://www.entnet.org/resource/aao-hnsf-updated-cpg-acute-otitis-externa-press-release-fact-sheet/)

One labeled option for uncomplicated AOE is **ofloxacin otic 0.3%: five drops in the affected ear daily from six months to under 13 years, or ten drops daily from age 13, for seven days**. Keep the ear upward for five minutes after administration and avoid touching the dropper tip. Quinolone hypersensitivity precludes this product; pregnancy/lactation need individual review. Its middle-ear indications have different age limits and schedules: do not apply the once-daily externa regimen to otorrhea through a tube or chronic perforation. A non-ototoxic choice does not make unassessed ear irrigation safe. [Current ofloxacin otic label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=b95114f6-6753-4217-b621-44fa34afc298)

Severe nocturnal pain, persistent purulence, granulation, or cranial neuropathy in an older, diabetic, or immunosuppressed patient suggests **necrotizing otitis externa** with possible skull-base osteomyelitis. Arrange urgent specialist assessment, culture, imaging, and directed systemic therapy; biopsy may distinguish malignancy. Neither routine drops nor a fixed organism/duration assumption is adequate. [Current expert review of invasive externa](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/external-ear-disorders/malignant-external-otitis)

Remove cerumen when it causes symptoms or obstructs needed examination, choosing the method by membrane status, surgery, anatomy, host factors, and cooperation. Avoid cotton swabs, candles, and unassessed irrigation. Persistent loss after removal requires evaluation. [AAO-HNSF cerumen safety guidance](https://www.entnet.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Cerumen_Dos-Donts_CoBranded-v2.pdf)

### Sudden hearing loss is not a routine congestion visit

Determine whether loss developed abruptly or gradually, is unilateral or bilateral, and accompanies tinnitus, vertigo, trauma, focal neurologic symptoms, or an ototoxic exposure. Inspect for wax, perforation, or middle-ear fluid, but remember that inner-ear disease can have a normal otoscopic examination. New neurologic deficits require emergency assessment for a central cause, not only an outpatient ear referral.

**Sudden sensorineural hearing loss needs urgent evaluation.** A commonly used audiometric definition is a drop of at least 30 dB across three contiguous frequencies within 72 hours, but an apparently smaller subjective loss still deserves prompt investigation. Patients often describe fullness and attribute it to wax or allergies. Do not require them to establish the audiometric threshold before making an urgent referral. [NIDCD sudden-deafness assessment](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness)

| Bedside pattern | Weber, if reliable | Rinne in the affected ear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unilateral conductive loss | Toward the ear with reduced hearing | Bone conduction may equal or exceed air conduction |
| Unilateral sensorineural loss | Toward the better-hearing ear | Air conduction usually remains better than bone conduction |
| Normal hearing or symmetric sensorineural loss | Often central | A positive Rinne does not distinguish normal hearing from sensorineural loss |

A 512-Hz fork helps triage but does not replace audiometry. Technique, mixed disease, symmetric deficits, and profound unilateral loss can mislead. Do not use an equivocal bedside result to postpone assessment. Pure-tone air/bone thresholds and speech testing clarify the type and functional effect. [NHS clinical-skills hearing examination](https://uhmeded.uhcw.nhs.uk/Portals/0/AssetUploads/CHESS%20Book%20Clinical%20Skills%20Workbook%202022-23.pdf?ver=ELcRsPPRSWHk3baBgJ2INQ%3D%3D)

AAO-HNSF recommends audiometry as soon as possible and within **14 days** of onset. This is an outer confirmation window, not advice to wait two weeks. Corticosteroids may be offered within two weeks after individualized discussion; incomplete recovery can warrant intratympanic salvage at two to six weeks. Evaluate retrocochlear disease with MRI or auditory brainstem response as appropriate. Routine head CT, indiscriminate blood panels, and routine antivirals are not the default idiopathic-SSNHL workup. Document follow-up hearing and rehabilitation needs. [AAO-HNSF sudden-hearing-loss pathway](https://www.entnet.org/resource/aao-hnsf-updated-cpg-shl-press-release-fact-sheet/)

**Clinical Case.** A 62-year-old has six days of sudden unilateral muffling/tinnitus after a cold despite normal canal/membrane findings. Arrange urgent ENT/audiometry, not delayed congestion follow-up. Facial weakness, ataxia, or other neurologic signs requires emergency care. Neither spontaneous recovery nor steroid benefit is guaranteed.

For gradual loss, assess communication, drugs, noise, work safety, and isolation. Use face-to-face speech, reduced background noise, and accessible instructions. Asymmetry, unilateral/pulsatile tinnitus, persistent vertigo, or adult effusion warrants diagnostic referral; rehabilitation does not replace investigation.

### Sore throat: choose testing, not antibiotics, from the score

Cough, rhinorrhea, hoarseness, conjunctivitis, and oral ulcers support a viral syndrome; clear viral illness generally does not need GAS testing. Fever, tender anterior cervical nodes, and tonsillar inflammation increase suspicion but cannot prove GAS. EBV, acute HIV, exposure-site gonococcal infection, and other diagnoses can overlap; exposure and systemic findings determine additional evaluation (09B-09C). A positive result in a person tested indiscriminately during a viral illness can reflect carriage rather than the cause of symptoms.

The **2025 IDSA update** conditionally supports clinical scores for selecting children/adults for testing, with very low-certainty evidence. A high Centor/McIsaac score is not proof or an automatic antibiotic indication. Household exposure, prior rheumatic fever, or complications can justify testing despite low scores. Treatment/follow-up recommendations remain separate. [IDSA current strep risk-assessment update](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/streptococcal-pharyngitis2/)

For appropriate patients, RADT or culture establishes the usual diagnostic pathway. **Back up a negative RADT with culture in symptomatic children age three or older**; routine backup is not necessary in adults. Know whether the actual local test is antigen-based or a validated molecular assay rather than applying an antigen rule to every platform. Routine testing below age three is uncommon and depends on exposure/clinical circumstances. Ensure someone reviews a pending culture and contacts the family. Do not use a remote antibody titer to diagnose today's sore throat.

Treat confirmed uncomplicated GAS with a suitable narrow-spectrum regimen. CDC options include **penicillin V 500 mg orally twice daily for ten days in adolescents/adults**, or **amoxicillin 50 mg/kg orally once daily, maximum 1,000 mg, for ten days**; the alternative is 25 mg/kg per dose twice daily, maximum 500 mg per dose, for ten days. Allergy phenotype and local macrolide/clindamycin resistance affect alternatives; cephalexin and cefadroxil are avoided with immediate-type penicillin allergy. Review kidney function and formulation. Stay home until afebrile and at least 12-24 hours after appropriate antibiotics. [CDC strep testing and treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/strep-throat.html)

Track backup cultures and treat positive results; see 13C for the pediatric case. Provide analgesia/hydration and urgent reassessment for drooling, dyspnea, dehydration, neck swelling, or unilateral progression before results return.

Recurrent/persistent pharyngitis requires review of adherence, reinfection, carriage, alternatives, and focal complications. Avoid routine antibiotics for well contacts, cure testing, or serial broadening without diagnosis. Treatment does not prevent every immune sequela; see renal/cardiac chapters.

### Deep-neck infection: airway safety comes before imaging

Drooling, stridor, inability to handle secretions, tripod positioning, progressive swelling, muffled voice, or toxic appearance changes the visit immediately. Keep a threatened-airway patient in a comfortable position, summon emergency/airway expertise, and avoid agitation or procedures that could precipitate obstruction. Do not send an unstable patient to an imaging department or insist on an office throat swab before transfer.

| Syndrome | Clues that change concern | Important next decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peritonsillar abscess | Unilateral severe pain, trismus, palatal swelling, muffled voice, displaced uvula | Urgent assessment for drainage, hydration, antibiotics, and airway risk |
| Retropharyngeal/parapharyngeal infection | Fever with restricted neck movement, neck swelling, swallowing difficulty, or systemic illness | Hospital evaluation; severity determines airway planning and imaging |
| Epiglottitis/supraglottitis | Severe pain or drooling, sometimes with little visible pharyngeal inflammation; stridor is concerning | Controlled expert airway assessment; a normal-looking throat is not reassuring |
| Spreading dental/floor-of-mouth infection | Induration, tongue displacement, progressive submandibular swelling | Emergency airway and dental/ENT source-control assessment |

Peritonsillar cellulitis and abscess can look similar; ultrasound or imaging can help when diagnosis or extent is uncertain. An abscess usually requires drainage by a trained clinician, plus aerobic/anaerobic coverage and supportive care. CT is useful when a deeper process is suspected or examination is difficult, but it is not a prerequisite for every clinically evident uncomplicated collection. Ability to drink, airway status, comorbidity, and reliable follow-up determine disposition. [Current expert peritonsillar assessment](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/peritonsillar-abscess-and-cellulitis)

In children, neck infection can be less obvious after prior antibiotics. CHOP's March 2026 pathway separates stable non-severe disease, often managed medically as an inpatient without routine initial CT, from possible airway compromise/sepsis/complications needing immediate ENT involvement, critical-care assessment, and urgent contrast CT with a coordinated sedation/airway plan. This is a pediatric institutional pathway, not a rule to scan every sore throat or an adult protocol. [CHOP pediatric deep-neck pathway](https://www.chop.edu/clinical-pathway/neck-infection-clinical-pathway)

Epiglottitis remains possible despite Hib vaccination and occurs in adults as well as children. In a child with suspected obstruction, forceful pharyngeal examination or attempted visualization without advanced airway support can cause complete collapse. Diagnosis and airway management belong in a controlled expert setting; antimicrobials accompany stabilization rather than replace it. Adults may deteriorate less abruptly but still need hospital monitoring and airway planning. [Current expert epiglottitis care](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/epiglottitis)

Recurrent rigors, unilateral neck pain/swelling, or pleuritic respiratory symptoms after pharyngitis should raise concern for septic complications, including Lemierre syndrome, rather than an uncomplicated prolonged viral illness. Severe systemic or suppurative sore-throat complications warrant hospital care. [NICE severe-complication referral](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng84/chapter/Recommendations)

**Clinical Case.** A recently treated 20-year-old returns with unilateral throat pain, trismus, muffled voice, and inability to drink. Arrange urgent deep-neck/peritonsillar assessment with airway and hydration support. Prior antibiotics do not exclude an abscess requiring source control.

### Rhinosinusitis: the trajectory matters more than mucus color

Viral sinonasal inflammation impairs drainage and causes pressure. Assess duration, double worsening, unilateral dental pain, severe headache, orbital symptoms, and immune compromise. Facial pain may instead be dental or migraine-related. Persistent unilateral obstruction, blood, or a mass requires further investigation.

For **uncomplicated adult ABRS**, the 2025 AAO-HNSF pathway uses compatible nasal drainage with obstruction and/or facial pressure that **fails to improve for at least ten days**, or worsens after initial improvement. Offer watchful waiting with assured follow-up. If an antibiotic is chosen, amoxicillin with or without clavulanate for **five to seven days** is first-line for most adults. Reassess failure to improve or worsening after **three to five days of appropriate antibiotics**, checking the diagnosis and complications before changing therapy. Routine imaging is not indicated for uncomplicated acute illness. [AAO-HNSF 2025 adult sinusitis recommendations](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ohn.1342)

For an otherwise healthy adult with uncomplicated mild-to-moderate ABRS who needs antibiotics, an example is **amoxicillin 500 mg PO three times daily or 875 mg twice daily for five to seven days**. Amoxicillin-clavulanate **875/125 mg twice daily** is an option when beta-lactamase resistance risk warrants it. Check allergy, recent antibiotic use, kidney function, and the selected product before prescribing; these are not pediatric or complicated-infection regimens. Azithromycin and TMP-SMX are not recommended for initial empiric ABRS treatment. [AAO-HNSF full guideline: antibiotic selection and dosing](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ohn.1344)

| Population | Compatible bacterial-course criteria | Initial-management distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adults, named 2025 AAO-HNSF pathway | At least ten days without improvement or double worsening | Offer observation for uncomplicated illness with follow-up; selected antibiotics usually five to seven days |
| Children, CDC/AAP pathway | More than ten days of persistent discharge/daytime cough, worsening after improvement, or fever at least 39 C with purulent discharge for at least three days | Up to three days' observation for persistent-only illness; treat severe or worsening disease |

Pediatric age, severe onset, and treatment duration are not imported from the adult pathway. Amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate can be used when indicated; check the child's dose, allergy, recent therapy, and actual formulation. Eye swelling with painful/restricted movement, diplopia, visual change, proptosis, neurologic signs, or severe systemic illness needs urgent complication assessment. [CDC pediatric sinusitis distinctions](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/pediatric-outpatient.html)

Offer analgesics, suitable intranasal treatment, and saline. During observation, reassess absent improvement over three to five days or worsening sooner. Reserve antihistamines for coexisting allergy; systemic steroids are not routine ABRS therapy. Explain antibiotic harms and expected recovery. [AAO-HNSF adult observation and symptom-care handout](https://www.entnet.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/CPG-AdultSinusitisUpdate_TreatingABRS_V5-1.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 29-year-old has eleven days of unimproved drainage/pressure, normal vision, no orbital/toxic findings, and reliable follow-up. Offer adult observation, symptom relief, and dated reassessment. Day ten supports bacterial diagnosis but does not mandate antibiotics; diplopia or severe headache requires urgent reassessment.

Chronic symptoms require a different question: is there objective sinonasal inflammation, rather than another acute infection? CRS generally lasts at least 12 weeks; endoscopy, examination, or appropriate CT establishes objective evidence. Assess polyps, asthma, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, structural disease, and immune problems when relevant. Saline and intranasal corticosteroids are core therapies; specialist-selected surgery or biologics depend on phenotype and prior treatment. Repeated antibiotics are not a routine default for stable CRS without an acute exacerbation. [AAO-HNSF chronic-sinusitis recommendations](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ohn.1342)

### Rhinitis can be allergic, irritant, or medication-related

Sneezing, itch, watery drainage, and reproducible seasonal/exposure symptoms support allergy. Irritants, temperature change, medicines, and excessive topical decongestant use can produce nonallergic symptoms. Intranasal corticosteroids are preferred monotherapy for persistent allergic rhinitis; an intranasal antihistamine can add benefit in selected patients. Do not automatically escalate every case to an oral decongestant or montelukast. Review hypertension, rhythm disease, urinary retention, glaucoma risk, pregnancy, and sedating/anticholinergic medicines before choosing symptomatic drugs. Aim sprays away from the septum and review technique if bleeding or poor response develops. The fuller allergy discussion follows in 10C. [AAAAI/ACAAI rhinitis practice parameter](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Rhinitis-2020-A-practice-parameter-update.pdf)

Use **distilled, sterile, or previously boiled and cooled water** for irrigation and clean the device. Drinking-water safety does not establish nasal safety. Prolonged topical vasoconstrictor use can worsen congestion. [CDC safe sinus rinsing](https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/prevention/sinus-rinsing.html)

### Epistaxis: local control and medication context

First assess bleeding severity, airway, perfusion, and whether blood is being swallowed or aspirated. Ask about trauma, dryness, instrumentation, intranasal drugs, previous treatment, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, and personal/family bleeding history. Sit a stable patient forward and apply sustained pressure to the **soft lower third of the nose**, not the bony bridge. AAO-HNSF specifies at least five minutes; a continuous 10-15-minute attempt is practical without repeatedly releasing pressure to check. Heavy bleeding, instability, airway concern, or failure of initial measures requires urgent escalation.

After clot removal and examination when safe, an identified anterior site may receive a topical vasoconstrictor or directed cautery. Do not cauterize blindly across broad areas. Persistent bleeding can need packing; **resorbable packing is preferred with a bleeding disorder or anticoagulant/antiplatelet use**. Recurrent unilateral bleeding or recurrence despite prior packing/cautery needs endoscopic assessment. In the absence of life-threatening hemorrhage, use local first-line treatment before automatically reversing or stopping anticoagulation. Uncontrolled posterior or refractory bleeding needs specialist care, potentially arterial control or embolization. [AAO-HNSF epistaxis action statements](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31910122/)

Packing requires explicit instructions about type, removal if needed, follow-up, and urgent symptoms. Moisturizing measures and avoiding further nasal trauma can reduce recurrence. Recurrent bilateral bleeding with mucocutaneous telangiectasias or a family history warrants assessment for hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, not attribution to dryness alone. [AAO-HNSF nosebleed prevention and patient instructions](https://bulletin.entnet.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Bulletin_February20.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 74-year-old taking apixaban has a controlled anterior nosebleed and stable vital signs. Review recurrence, indication/dose, other bleeding, renal function, and prescriber follow-up rather than automatically stopping anticoagulation. Life-threatening hemorrhage requires resuscitation and separate reversal decisions.

### Oral lesions: distinguish a familiar pattern from an unhealed abnormality

Inspect oral mucosa, lips, tongue, floor, palate, gingiva, teeth, and neck; palpate for induration. Document site/size and assess pain, duration, recurrence, trauma, dentures, drugs, tobacco/alcohol/betel, systemic symptoms, and immunity. Dysphagia, spreading swelling, dehydration, or airway symptoms requires escalation.

| Pattern | Useful distinction | Next clinical question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recurrent small painful aphthae | Commonly on nonkeratinized oral mucosa; not contagious | Typical limited episodes or severe/persistent disease suggesting another cause? |
| Grouped recurrent labial vesicles | HSV reactivation; can transmit | Is early antiviral treatment useful, and is there eye/immune-host risk? |
| White plaques with soreness, often removable | Possible candidiasis | What medicine, denture, metabolic, or immune factor predisposed to it? |
| Persistent ulcer, induration, or unexplained red/white lesion | Not safely classified by appearance alone | Is biopsy or specialist referral needed now? |

Minor aphthae and herpes labialis usually heal within one to two weeks. Aphthae are not HSV and do not routinely need antivirals. Frequent, extensive, or persistent ulcers warrant medication, nutritional, GI/systemic, and immune assessment, with pain/intake support. [NIDCR common oral sores](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/fever-blisters-canker-sores)

Thrush is associated with factors such as antibiotics, steroids, dentures, diabetes, and impaired immunity. Address the precipitant and use an appropriate antifungal by site and severity. New dysphagia or odynophagia can signal deeper involvement rather than simple surface disease. A fixed non-wipeable white lesion is not automatically candidiasis; persistent or atypical findings need reassessment instead of repeated empiric antifungals. [CDC candidiasis assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/candidiasis/about/index.html)

For mild adult oropharyngeal candidiasis, the active IDSA pathway includes **clotrimazole troches 10 mg dissolved in the mouth five times daily for 7-14 days**; another option is nystatin suspension 100,000 units/mL, 4-6 mL four times daily for 7-14 days. These are local oral-cavity treatments, not esophageal therapy or neonatal doses. Clean dentures and correct the predisposing problem. Moderate/severe or refractory disease, immune suppression, or painful swallowing requires reassessment and often systemic treatment, with renal, pregnancy, and interaction review. [IDSA oral-candidiasis recommendations](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/candidiasis/)

The **2026 ADA pathway** retains clinical examination and biopsy/referral as the diagnostic foundation. If an unexplained mucosal abnormality is not resolving **10-14 days after initial evaluation** without a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan, recommend biopsy or referral. A suspicious lesion warrants prompt assessment rather than a mandatory waiting period. Commercial light, dye, saliva, or cytology adjuncts do not routinely replace this decision; cytology has a limited fallback role when biopsy is not possible/advisable/indicated, and persistence despite a negative result still requires action. [ADA current oral-cancer pathway](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/science/evidence-based-dental-research/oral-cancer-guideline)

Absence of smoking does not exclude cancer. Persistent throat pain, unilateral referred otalgia, a neck mass, dysphagia, bleeding, or voice change can accompany head/neck malignancy. HPV-associated oropharyngeal disease and tobacco/alcohol-related oral disease are related but not identical entities. Arrange appropriate dental, oral-medicine, or ENT assessment and track completion. [NCI concerning head/neck symptoms](https://www.cancer.gov/types/head-and-neck/head-neck-fact-sheet)

**Clinical Case.** A 51-year-old nonsmoker has an indurated tongue ulcer three weeks after smoothing a rough tooth. Arrange biopsy-capable referral; presumed trauma and a negative commercial adjunct do not establish healing or remove the need for tissue diagnosis.

Assign a recovery/review date, escalation symptoms, and responsibility for results/referrals, especially across primary care, dentistry, audiology, and ENT.
