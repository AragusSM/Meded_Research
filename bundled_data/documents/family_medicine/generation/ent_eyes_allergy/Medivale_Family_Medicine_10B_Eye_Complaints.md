# Medivale Family Medicine

## 10B. Eye Complaints

First establish visual change and threats to the eye or its blood supply. Retinal ischemia may feel like dimming, angle closure like headache, and keratitis like lens discomfort. Redness does not localize disease; painless white eyes can have emergencies. Establish onset, examine each eye, and determine urgency.

CDC infection pathways, ADA diabetic surveillance, and AAO condition-specific guidance inform this section. Specialist procedures and multidrug rescue protocols require supervised care. Screening recommendations apply to asymptomatic populations and never justify delaying evaluation of new symptoms.

Document findings, communicate urgency and confirm access to the arranged care. Give explicit emergency precautions for sudden loss, a curtain, new major floaters/flashes, severe pain, worsening photophobia or new neurologic symptoms.

### Measure vision and localize the problem

Establish onset and test each eye separately; apparent monocular symptoms may represent a homonymous field defect. Assess pain, itch, photophobia, discharge, flashes/floaters, distortion, diplopia, headache, and neurology. Record trauma, chemicals, lenses, surgery/injections, diabetes, immunity, inflammatory disease, and drops. Report new postprocedural symptoms immediately.

Measure distance acuity in **each eye separately**, using the patient's usual distance correction and the chart's specified distance. Document the actual result, not simply that the patient can see. Use age-appropriate matching symbols or fixation behavior in young children; an uncooperative examination is not a normal examination. If acuity is too poor for the chart, document counting fingers, hand motion, or light perception as appropriate. Assess pupils, direct and consensual responses, visual fields, alignment, eye movements, lids, conjunctiva, corneal clarity, and red reflex. Fluorescein and slit-lamp examination help when a surface defect is possible. [RCH examination framework](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Eye_Examination/)

Pinhole improvement suggests refractive contribution but does not exclude disease. Myopia impairs distance focus, astigmatism distorts focus, and presbyopia reduces near focus. Arrange refraction for stable low-risk symptoms; sudden change requires evaluation beyond glasses or presumed cataract. [NEI refractive errors](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/refractive-errors)

| Examination pattern | What it helps localize | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discharge with mild irritation, clear cornea, and preserved acuity | Conjunctival surface | Marked pain, photophobia, or persistent visual loss requires another explanation |
| Corneal staining or an infiltrate | Epithelial injury or corneal infection | A white infiltrate is not merely a scratch |
| Abnormal pupil, corneal haze, or inflammation around the limbus | Anterior chamber/pressure-related disease | A primary-care inspection cannot exclude subtle uveitis or angle closure |
| New afferent pupillary defect or field loss | Asymmetric retinal/optic-nerve dysfunction or visual pathway disease | Preserved central chart vision does not exclude a peripheral retinal problem |

**Clinical Case.** A 46-year-old requests drops for two days of unilateral red-eye blur. Reduced acuity and photophobia require ophthalmic corneal/anterior-segment assessment rather than a conjunctivitis prescription.

### A red eye is a symptom, not a diagnosis

Conjunctivitis usually causes irritation, grittiness, or itch rather than deep pain. Severe pain, substantial photophobia, reduced acuity, corneal opacity, abnormal pupil, or possible penetrating injury requires urgent specialist assessment. Purulent discharge can accompany keratitis or intraocular infection, so it does not establish benign conjunctivitis. Examine the cornea before accepting that diagnosis, especially in a unilateral or contact-lens-associated presentation. [RCH red-eye danger signs](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Acute_red_eye/)

Viral disease commonly follows respiratory exposure and produces watery discharge; allergic disease usually features bilateral itch and an exposure pattern. Bacterial disease can produce repeatedly accumulating mucopurulent discharge and matted lids. These findings overlap, and mixed or atypical presentations occur. Most acute infectious conjunctivitis in adults is viral and does not need an antibiotic. Provide lubricants, cool compresses, hand hygiene, and separate towels; do not share drops. A contact lens wearer with presumed bacterial conjunctivitis needs lens removal, topical antibiotic treatment, and prompt ophthalmologic evaluation because bacterial keratitis is more likely. [CDC clinical overview](https://www.cdc.gov/conjunctivitis/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

Avoid **indiscriminate antibiotic or corticosteroid drops**. Mild bacterial conjunctivitis may resolve untreated; antibiotics can shorten it. Choose by severity, host, allergy, age, and practice. Culture severe, recurrent, unusual, or unresponsive infection. Steroid combinations are inappropriate for undifferentiated red eye. [AAO highlighted recommendations](https://aao.org/education/preferred-practice-pattern/conjunctivitis-ppp-2023), [College of Optometrists bacterial conjunctivitis scope](https://www.college-optometrists.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-management-guidelines/conjunctivitis_bacterial)

When treating a suitable uncomplicated bacterial conjunctivitis without lens-related or corneal danger signs, one labeled option is **polymyxin B 10,000 units/mL plus trimethoprim 1 mg/mL ophthalmic solution: one drop in each affected eye every three hours, maximum six doses daily, for 7-10 days**. Do not use with component hypersensitivity; stop and reassess an increasing allergic/irritative reaction. Safety below two months is unestablished, and the product is not for neonatal ophthalmia. Pregnancy/lactation require benefit-risk review. This surface-infection schedule is not treatment for keratitis, gonococcal disease, or intraocular infection. [Current ophthalmic product label](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=87999254-1857-4d3c-817a-cb8f6a5b0cf7)

For allergy, reduce exposure and consider topical antihistamine/mast-cell stabilizers plus lubricants. Oral antihistamines may worsen dryness. Significant photophobia/visual loss requires another assessment. Review persistent/worsening symptoms according to severity and access. Attendance depends on illness, close-contact risk, and local policy, not mandatory antibiotics.

**Hyperacute copious purulence**, marked swelling, and rapid progression raise concern for gonococcal disease and corneal damage. Obtain appropriate specimens and arrange urgent ophthalmology and infectious-disease/STI care without delaying treatment. CDC's adolescent/adult conjunctivitis regimen is **ceftriaxone 1 g intramuscularly once**, with consideration of a one-time saline lavage. This is not the usual uncomplicated genital-site dose. Address other STI testing, partner care, allergy, and pregnancy-specific coinfection treatment. Persistent chlamydial conjunctivitis also requires systemic treatment and partner management rather than drops alone. [CDC ocular gonorrhea regimen](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-adults.htm)

A newborn with conjunctivitis needs prompt assessment for gonococcus, chlamydia, HSV, and systemic infection, not the routine older-child pathway. Gonococcal ophthalmia requires systemic treatment; topical therapy alone is inadequate. Evaluate dissemination and maternal/partner infection, and account for neonatal ceftriaxone cautions, including bilirubin and intravenous calcium. Birth prophylaxis does not remove the need to evaluate a symptomatic infant or prevent all causes of neonatal conjunctivitis. [CDC neonatal infection guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-neonates.htm)

### Dryness, lid disease, and localized redness

Dry eye causes burning, grittiness, fluctuating blur, and reflex watering from deficient production or excess evaporation. Review screens, environment, lenses, drugs, lid closure, and autoimmunity. Start lubricants, exposure modification, and visual-task breaks. Persistent/severe disease warrants surface assessment and possible specialist therapy; fixed loss or severe pain requires another explanation. [NEI dry eye](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/dry-eye)

Blepharitis causes lid irritation/crusting; meibomian dysfunction destabilizes the oily tear layer. Use regular gentle hygiene/warm compresses for ongoing control. A painful focal lump suggests hordeolum; a persistent painless nodule suggests chalazion. Avoid squeezing; recurrent, atypical, nonhealing, or lash-distorting lesions warrant referral. [NEI lid disease](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/blepharitis)

A sharply demarcated subconjunctival hemorrhage can look dramatic while leaving vision and comfort intact. Ask about trauma, straining, recurrent bleeding, anticoagulants, and other bleeding symptoms; check blood pressure and investigate when indicated. It usually clears without an antibiotic. Trauma-associated extensive hemorrhage, abnormal globe contour, reduced vision, or an uncertain posterior extent needs injury assessment. Do not independently stop an indicated anticoagulant for an isolated uncomplicated surface bleed. Mild focal episcleral inflammation differs from the deep pain of scleritis; diagnostic uncertainty should lower the referral threshold.

### Corneal disease and injury require different decisions

A corneal abrasion is an epithelial defect from trauma or foreign material. Fluorescein identifies the defect; vertical scratches prompt safe upper-lid eversion after confirming globe integrity. Assess acuity, location, contamination, lenses, and infection. Provide oral analgesia, appropriate topical treatment, and early review for high-risk or persistent defects. The College of Optometrists advises **against dispensing topical anesthetic for unsupervised pain relief**, although it facilitates examination. Do not pressure-patch contact lens wearers. [Corneal abrasion management](https://www.college-optometrists.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-management-guidelines/cornealabrasion)

An **infiltrate or ulcer**, worsening pain, photophobia, or loss of acuity suggests microbial keratitis rather than a simple abrasion. Contact lenses, corneal injury, immune suppression, and abnormal ocular surfaces increase risk. Remove lenses and arrange same-day ophthalmic assessment; sampling and intensive organism-appropriate therapy may be needed. Do not manage a corneal ulcer with a routine conjunctivitis prescription and delayed follow-up. Pseudomonas is important in lens-associated disease, but bacteria are not the only possibility: water exposure raises concern for Acanthamoeba, and vegetative trauma can introduce fungi. [CDC bacterial keratitis](https://www.cdc.gov/contact-lenses/causes/what-causes-contact-lens-related-bacterial-keratitis.html)

HSV epithelial keratitis can produce a branching dendritic lesion and may recur after latent infection reactivates. Arrange prompt specialist-directed antiviral care. Steroids can worsen active epithelial infection; stromal or intraocular inflammatory disease is a different situation in which an ophthalmologist may use steroids with appropriate antiviral coverage. A forehead/upper-lid zoster eruption, especially with ocular symptoms, also needs prompt assessment and systemic antiviral consideration; the absence of a nasal lesion does not exclude eye involvement. [HSV corneal disease](https://www.college-optometrists.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-management-guidelines/herpessimplexkeratitis_hsk)

**Clinical Case.** A 23-year-old develops pain, photophobia, reduced acuity, and a small pale corneal lesion after sleeping/swimming in soft lenses. Remove lenses and arrange urgent ophthalmology. The exposure informs microbiology but does not prove the organism.

Keep lenses away from shower/swimming water; never rinse/store them in tap water. Follow prescribed care, replacement/disinfection advice, and eye-team clearance before restarting wear. [CDC water and contact lenses](https://www.cdc.gov/contact-lenses/about/healthy-habits-keeping-water-away-from-contact-lenses.html)

Chemical exposure requires **immediate copious irrigation**; do not postpone irrigation to finish the history or locate an ideal solution. Remove retained particulate matter safely, reassess ocular pH according to the emergency protocol, and obtain urgent ophthalmic care. Suspected open globe is different: stop manipulation, avoid pressure or tonometry, apply a rigid shield rather than a pressure dressing, keep the patient fasting, and arrange emergency care. Do not remove an embedded object. High-velocity metal work can cause penetration despite an unimpressive surface lesion. Severe post-traumatic proptosis with reduced vision or an afferent defect may indicate orbital compartment syndrome requiring immediate expert decompression. [RCH eye-injury emergency framework](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Acute_eye_injury/)

### Pressure, intraocular inflammation, and orbital infection

In **acute angle closure**, iris obstruction of aqueous outflow rapidly raises pressure. Severe unilateral pain, halos/blur, headache, vomiting, hazy cornea, and a poorly reactive mid-dilated pupil need emergency ophthalmology. Pressure-lowering treatment and laser iridotomy address appropriate pupillary-block mechanisms; secondary mechanisms require different care under the 2026 AAO pathway. [Current AAO primary angle-closure guideline](https://www.aaojournal.org/article/S0161-6420%2825%2900816-4/abstract)

Open-angle glaucoma is progressive optic neuropathy with initially silent field loss. Pressure alone neither diagnoses nor excludes it: damage can occur at normal pressure and hypertension without damage. Assess nerve structure, fields, pressure, and angle. Drops/laser/surgery prevent further injury but do not restore lost nerve tissue; reinforce adherence, technique, and follow-up. [NEI glaucoma](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/glaucoma)

Include eye drops in medication reconciliation. **Ophthalmic timolol can cause systemic beta-blockade** and is contraindicated with asthma/history of asthma, severe COPD, sinus bradycardia, second/third-degree AV block, overt heart failure, or cardiogenic shock. New wheeze, syncope, or a slow pulse after starting drops warrants prompt review with the treating clinician. Concurrent systemic beta-blockers and selected conduction-slowing drugs can increase harm. A small topical dose is not proof of negligible systemic exposure. [Timolol ophthalmic contraindications and interactions](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=b686bbad-1ef7-4b94-a316-4d182a40b89a&type=display)

The USPSTF finds insufficient evidence to determine the benefit-harm balance of population screening for primary open-angle glaucoma in **asymptomatic adults 40 years or older**. This is not a recommendation to ignore symptoms, suspicious examination findings, family risk, or established glaucoma. Risk-based eye-care decisions and treatment of diagnosed disease are separate from the preventive screening evidence question. [USPSTF final screening statement](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/primary-open-angle-glaucoma-screening)

Anterior uveitis involves inflammation of the iris/ciliary region and may cause pain, photophobia, blurred vision, and a small or irregular pupil. Slit-lamp examination demonstrates anterior-chamber inflammation; not every case is obvious on penlight inspection. Arrange urgent ophthalmic assessment before starting empiric steroid drops. Causes include immune-mediated disease and infection; a history of inflammatory back pain, bowel disease, psoriasis, recurrent episodes, or immune compromise guides further investigation. Treatment and testing depend on location and cause, not a universal autoimmune panel. Monitor pressure and cataract risk during steroid therapy. [NEI uveitis](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/uveitis)

Scleritis causes deep, severe, sometimes radiating pain, tenderness, and possible visual impairment. Immune disease or infection requires urgent ophthalmic assessment. Known autoimmunity does not exclude infection, and no autoimmune history does not make deep pain benign. [Scleritis recognition and referral scope](https://www.college-optometrists.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-management-guidelines/scleritis)

| Presentation | Main concern | Initial disposition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain, halos, vomiting, hazy cornea, abnormal pupil | Acute angle closure | Emergency ophthalmic care |
| Photophobia with anterior-chamber inflammation | Uveitis | Urgent specialist-directed evaluation and treatment |
| Deep severe pain and scleral inflammation | Scleritis | Urgent ophthalmology and directed systemic evaluation |
| Painful red eye and visual decline after surgery/injection | Endophthalmitis | Immediate contact with surgical/retinal service or emergency care |
| Lid swelling plus painful/restricted movements, proptosis, or impaired vision | Orbital cellulitis | Emergency hospital evaluation, imaging, and specialist care |

Preseptal cellulitis involves tissue anterior to the orbital septum. In a well patient, vision and movements remain normal and proptosis is absent; selected cases can receive oral treatment with reliable review. Orbital disease threatens vision and can extend intracranially. Painful or limited movements, diplopia, an afferent defect, proptosis, severe headache, or an examination too limited to exclude orbital involvement warrants urgent hospital assessment. Coordinate ophthalmology/ENT, imaging, and intravenous therapy. Age, immunization, sinus disease, local resistance, and systemic illness modify the regimen. [RCH orbital versus preseptal pathway](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Periorbital_and_orbital_cellulitis/)

**Clinical Case.** After sinus symptoms, a seven-year-old has fever, lid swelling, painful upward gaze, and diplopia. Arrange emergency assessment for postseptal disease even if conjunctiva appears normal.

### Flashes, floaters, and sudden loss of sight

Age-related vitreous separation can cause new moving opacities and flashes as fibers pull on the retina. An uncomplicated posterior vitreous detachment often needs no treatment, but its symptoms overlap with a retinal tear. **New flashes or a sudden increase in floaters requires prompt dilated retinal examination**, not reassurance based on age alone. A tear can occur without an initial curtain, and an initial negative examination does not eliminate the need for instructed follow-up and reassessment if symptoms change. [NEI vitreous detachment](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/vitreous-detachment)

Retinal detachment separates the neurosensory retina from supporting tissue. A new shadow or curtain, peripheral field defect, or reduced vision after flashes/floaters needs immediate ophthalmic or emergency evaluation. Myopia, trauma, previous detachment, and ocular surgery increase concern, but no risk factor is required. Direct ophthalmoscopy in an undilated primary-care examination cannot reliably exclude a peripheral tear or detachment. The retinal team determines whether laser, cryotherapy, or surgery is needed and how urgently; do not wait for central acuity to deteriorate. [NEI retinal detachment](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/retinal-detachment)

Sudden painless monocular visual loss can be **retinal arterial ischemia**, an ischemic stroke of the eye. Record the last known normal time and arrange immediate emergency/stroke-capable evaluation with ophthalmology, even when there are no limb symptoms. A relative afferent defect and retinal hypoperfusion support the diagnosis, but do not delay transfer while trying to demonstrate a classic fundus sign. Acute reperfusion decisions belong to the stroke/ophthalmology team under current protocols; do not promise thrombolysis or perform ocular massage as a proven remedy. Carotid/cardiac evaluation and secondary vascular prevention remain important. Transient monocular loss can be a warning ischemic event even after vision returns. [AHA retinal artery occlusion statement summary](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/management-of-central-retinal-artery-occlusion/top-things-to-know)

Retinal vein occlusion impairs venous drainage, producing hemorrhages, edema, and sometimes retinal ischemia. It commonly causes unilateral blur and requires prompt retinal evaluation; edema and neovascular complications may require injections or laser. Check vascular risk factors and coordinate systemic evaluation rather than treating the retinal appearance as an incidental finding. Broad thrombophilia testing is not automatic for every typical unilateral case; unusual age, bilateral disease, or other clinical clues changes that assessment. Sudden undifferentiated visual loss still follows an emergency pathway until the cause is established. [ASRS venous occlusion](https://www.asrs.org/patients/retinal-diseases/22/central-retinal-vein-occlusion)

In an older adult, visual symptoms with a new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, or polymyalgia symptoms raise concern for **giant cell arteritis**. Obtain inflammatory markers and blood counts promptly, but do not delay glucocorticoid treatment for biopsy when suspicion is high and vision is threatened. Arrange emergency ophthalmic/rheumatologic care; the ACR/Vasculitis Foundation guideline conditionally favors intravenous pulse glucocorticoids over high-dose oral treatment for threatened vision. Diagnostic confirmation and longer-term steroid-sparing care follow without sacrificing the remaining vision. [ACR/VF GCA guideline](https://www.vasculitisfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2021-ACR-VF-Guideline-for-Management-of-Giant-Cell-Arteritis-and-Takayasu-Artheritis.pdf)

Optic neuritis is another important cause of visual decline: pain with eye movement, impaired color vision, an afferent defect, and subacute loss suggest optic-nerve dysfunction. A normal-looking disc does not exclude a lesion behind the globe. Arrange urgent ophthalmic/neurologic evaluation, with imaging and treatment tailored to the cause; not all optic neuropathy is multiple sclerosis. New binocular field loss, diplopia with neurologic findings, or visual symptoms with a severe new headache can instead reflect intracranial disease and require emergency assessment. [NIH optic-neuritis clinical features](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/68009902)

**Clinical Case.** A 71-year-old has a sudden monocular dark curtain that resolves after four minutes. Normal office acuity and absent redness/weakness do not exclude retinal ischemia; arrange emergency vascular/ocular evaluation.

### Chronic visual loss: identify the tissue and functional effect

Cataract causes gradual painless blur, glare, reduced color clarity, and night-driving difficulty. Assess retinal/coexisting disease. Correction/lighting may help; surgery depends on expected function, preferences, and comorbidity rather than one acuity cutoff. Arrange transport/support. Sudden postoperative loss, pain, marked redness, or flashes requires reassessment. [NEI cataracts](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/cataracts)

AMD affects the macula, producing central distortion or a central scotoma rather than the initially peripheral loss typical of glaucoma. Drusen and retinal pigment changes help define the phenotype; neovascular disease can progress quickly. New wavy lines or central blur needs prompt retinal review, often including OCT. Intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment is central to neovascular AMD management. Encourage smoking cessation and follow-up; home monitoring of each eye can identify change but does not replace examination or prove that new symptoms are harmless. [NEI AMD recognition](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/age-related-macular-degeneration)

AREDS2 supplementation is for selected patients, especially **intermediate AMD or late disease in one eye**, after eye-care review. It is not a general vitamin prescription for every older adult or a way to prevent early AMD from appearing. Check the exact formulation, other supplements, and interactions. Avoid beta-carotene-containing eye supplements in current or former smokers because of lung-cancer risk; AREDS2 uses lutein/zeaxanthin instead. Do not let supplement discussion delay assessment of new distortion. [NEI AREDS2 indications and safety](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/age-related-macular-degeneration/nutritional-supplements-age-related-macular-degeneration)

Advanced dry AMD with geographic atrophy is not simply an untreatable category. Selected patients may be offered **intravitreal complement inhibition**, including avacincaptad pegol or pegcetacoplan, to slow lesion enlargement; this is not restoration of already lost vision. Balance likely benefit, injection burden, ocular inflammation/infection risks, and risk of neovascular disease with a retinal specialist. Pegcetacoplan labeling additionally warns about retinal vasculitis/vascular occlusion. Do not substitute these agents for anti-VEGF treatment of neovascular AMD or promise that all atrophic disease warrants injections. [Current avacincaptad pegol labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=1642fe6a-dc26-4d20-ae6e-654af744e3bd&type=display), [Current pegcetacoplan labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=b4434e61-9ec9-4e0e-bd60-45aaa0a87e64)

| Predominant complaint | Possible localization | Useful next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gradual glare and hazy vision | Lens/cataract | Full eye examination and function-based surgical discussion |
| Central distortion or missing detail | Macula | Prompt retinal assessment for new change; OCT as indicated |
| Progressive field loss with little early awareness | Optic nerve/glaucoma | Structural/field assessment and pressure-directed care |
| Fluctuating blur with burning | Tear film/surface | Surface/lid assessment while checking for danger signs |

**Clinical Case.** A 76-year-old with cataracts sees a newly bent door frame through the right eye. Arrange retinal assessment for macular disease; cataracts do not explain new distortion, and vitamins/refraction do not replace evaluation.

Offer **vision rehabilitation** for persistent impairment: magnification, contrast, accessible devices, medication identification, lighting, falls/driving safety, and home support. Prioritize daily tasks and coordinate low-vision/occupational services, addressing isolation/depression. Rehabilitation complements disease treatment despite irreversible damage. [NEI low-vision care](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/low-vision)

### Diabetic surveillance is preventive care, not symptom triage

Diabetic microvascular injury can remain asymptomatic while progressing. Leakage causes edema; ischemia stimulates fragile new vessels that can bleed or create traction. Sudden floaters, a curtain, or new visual loss needs urgent assessment regardless of the date of the last screening visit. Optimize diabetes, blood pressure, and lipid care while ensuring that retinal treatment actually occurs. A good recent A1C does not erase accumulated risk. [NEI diabetic eye disease](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/diabetic-retinopathy)

Use the **2026 ADA** surveillance pathway below. Annual review is usual; selected patients without retinopathy after normal annual examinations and with glycemia at goal may extend to one to two years. Existing disease requires at least annual, and progressing or sight-threatening disease more frequent, ophthalmic care. Refer promptly for diabetic macular edema, moderate-or-worse nonproliferative retinopathy, or proliferative disease. Validated photography/authorized automated screening can improve access but needs a clear referral route; ungradable images are not a normal result. [ADA adult retinopathy recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690177/)

| Population | Initial assessment | Important exception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult type 1 diabetes | Comprehensive dilated examination five years after onset | Symptoms require assessment now |
| Type 2 diabetes | At diagnosis | Do not wait for visual symptoms |
| Preexisting type 1/type 2 diabetes and pregnancy | Before pregnancy and in the first trimester | May need each-trimester monitoring and follow-up for one year postpartum, according to retinal status |
| Gestational diabetes alone | No additional diabetic-retinopathy examination solely for GDM | New symptoms or suspected preexisting diabetes change the evaluation |

Youth use their own pathway: in type 1 diabetes, begin a comprehensive diabetes eye examination after three to five years of diabetes once age 11 years or puberty has begun, whichever is earlier. Type 2 diabetes in children/adolescents warrants screening at or soon after diagnosis and annually thereafter. Do not mechanically transfer the adult five-year rule to every child; subsequent intervals should follow the pediatric diabetes/eye team. [ADA 2026 pediatric recommendations](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-s014)

**Anti-VEGF injections** reduce leakage and neovascular growth, often requiring repeated visits. **Panretinal photocoagulation** reduces the neovascular stimulus in proliferative disease; hemorrhage/traction may need vitrectomy. OCT tracks edema/response. Improving systemic diabetes does not prevent ongoing retinal damage; arrange transport/reminders for attendance-dependent treatment. [ASRS diabetic retinopathy mechanisms and treatment](https://www.asrs.org/patients/retinal-diseases/3/diabetic-retinopathy)

**Clinical Case.** An asymptomatic 32-year-old with type 1 diabetes plans pregnancy. Arrange preconception assessment and a first-trimester plan rather than waiting until next year's screen; coordinate monitoring alongside appropriate glycemic care.

### Children need developmental surveillance as well as symptom care

In childhood, a correctable visual problem can impair development of the visual pathway. Amblyopia can arise when one eye is chronically disadvantaged by unequal refraction, misalignment, or an obstructed visual axis. The USPSTF recommends **at least one screen from ages three to five years** for amblyopia or its risk factors; its insufficient-evidence statement under age three is not a ban on infant eye examination. Failed screening, parental concern, abnormal alignment, or an abnormal eye examination warrants referral rather than waiting for the next routine age window. [USPSTF pediatric vision statement](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/vision-in-children-ages-6-months-to-5-years-screening)

An absent/asymmetric red reflex or **white pupil (leukocoria)** needs prompt pediatric ophthalmic assessment. Causes include cataract, retinoblastoma, retinal disease, and other obstructing lesions; a photograph can reveal a concern but cannot establish or dismiss the cause. Do not reassure a family that an infant's opaque lens can be managed on the same timetable as an older adult's elective cataract. Children may compensate with the better eye and never report blur, so appearance, alignment, monocular testing, and follow-through after referral all matter. [AAPOS leukocoria](https://www.aapos.org/glossary/leukocoria)
