# Medivale Family Medicine

## 14B. Gynecologic and Breast Complaints

For bleeding, discharge, pelvic pain, or breast change, first assess instability and time-sensitive disease, then localize the cause and select tests. Normal screening or one negative test cannot clear a new symptom. Match treatment to comfort, sexual function, privacy, and pregnancy goals.

CDC infection guidance supplies the main antimicrobial pathways, with ACOG's recurrent-BV partner pathway distinguished where it changes care. ACOG's 2026 postmenopausal-bleeding and endometriosis updates inform those diagnostic decisions. Endocrine, menopause, breast-imaging and pelvic-floor frameworks are identified below. Routine cervical screening and abnormal-result algorithms are consolidated in 01B; a new symptom or visible lesion instead needs diagnostic assessment.

### Begin with urgency, anatomy and consent

Assess vital signs and the amount and trajectory of bleeding. Syncope, hypotension, persistent tachycardia, severe pain, peritoneal findings or pregnancy-associated symptoms may require emergency evaluation. Establish whether blood comes from the uterus, cervix, vulva, urinary tract or rectum. Ask about the last normal period, pregnancy possibility, contraception, medicines, anticoagulation, prior procedures, infection exposure and relevant cancer or bleeding history. Heavy bleeding with instability is a resuscitation problem before it is a cycle-management problem. [ACOG acute bleeding assessment](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2013/04/management-of-acute-abnormal-uterine-bleeding-in-nonpregnant-reproductive-aged-women)

Explain examinations/specimens, obtain consent, and offer a chaperone. Adapt position, instruments, and pace to pain, disability, trauma, and preference; pause or stop on request. Discuss exposure and coercion privately without assumptions. Document examination limits and agree on alternatives when declined. Symptom-directed assessment differs from routine contraceptive prerequisites.

### Distinguish vaginal disease from cervical infection

Ask about odor, itch, burning, discharge, dyspareunia, postcoital bleeding, and pain; review antibiotics, diabetes, pregnancy, immunosuppression, and irritants. Inspect vulva/discharge; add speculum/bimanual assessment for possible cervical disease or PID. pH and microscopy help, but negative wet mounts do not reliably exclude infection. Use indicated molecular tests or cultures. [CDC cervicitis assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/urethritis-and-cervicitis.htm)

| Working possibility | Useful finding, not a standalone diagnosis | Next diagnostic distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bacterial vaginosis | Thin discharge, elevated pH, clue cells or amine odor | Dysbiosis criteria or a validated symptomatic-patient test |
| Vulvovaginal candidiasis | Itch, vulvar inflammation, yeast; pH often below 4.5 | Symptomatic infection versus colonization; complicated disease |
| Trichomoniasis | Inflammation/discharge; microscopy may miss organisms | Sensitive testing and a partner/reinfection plan |
| Cervicitis or upper-tract infection | Cervical discharge/friability or pelvic tenderness | Gonorrhea/chlamydia testing, pregnancy assessment and PID evaluation |

For symptomatic BV, use clinical criteria or an appropriate laboratory method. Amsel diagnosis requires three of four findings: characteristic discharge, pH above 4.5, clue cells and an amine odor with KOH. One CDC oral regimen is metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for seven days; recommended vaginal regimens are alternatives, selected for tolerance and product precautions. Do not diagnose BV from a cervical Pap result or a nonspecific Gardnerella culture. Return if symptoms recur; recurrence does not establish nonadherence or infidelity. [CDC BV diagnosis and treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/bv.htm)

For recurrent symptomatic BV in adults, ACOG's October 2025 update supports considering concurrent oral plus topical treatment of male sexual partners; shared decision making also applies to same-sex partners and a first symptomatic episode. [ACOG partner update](https://www.acog.org/news/news-releases/2025/10/acog-recommends-concurrent-sexual-partner-treatment-recurrent-bacterial-vaginosis-first-time) The named NYSDOH 2026 implementation offers an ongoing partner with a penis metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily plus clindamycin 2% cream to the penile head and shaft twice daily, both for seven days. Penile cream use is off-label; do not substitute vaginal gel or 1% skin preparations. Arrange partner assessment and abstain until both finish treatment; cream can weaken latex barriers. This is not automatic treatment of every past partner or every vaginal complaint. [NYSDOH partner regimen and limitations](https://www.hivguidelines.org/guideline/sti-bv/)

Treat candidiasis when compatible symptoms accompany yeast evidence; a positive culture without symptoms can reflect colonization. Uncomplicated disease in an eligible nonpregnant patient can receive a topical azole or fluconazole 150 mg orally once. During pregnancy, CDC recommends a seven-day topical azole rather than oral fluconazole. Persistent symptoms, non-albicans species, severe inflammation or at least three episodes in a year require confirmation and a longer, individualized induction/maintenance plan. Oral interactions and liver disease matter; repeated unsupervised single doses are not a diagnostic strategy. [CDC candidiasis guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/candidiasis.htm)

For trichomoniasis in women, CDC recommends oral metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for seven days. Treat current partners, avoid intercourse until treatment is completed and symptoms have resolved, and test for other STIs. Retest sexually active women at about three months because reinfection is common; this is not an immediate test of cure. A negative wet mount should lead to sensitive testing when suspicion remains. For persistent/recurrent symptoms, reassess exposure, adherence and resistance; if using NAAT, wait at least three weeks after treatment completion to avoid residual-nucleic-acid detection. [CDC trichomoniasis pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/trichomoniasis.htm)

### Recognize PID without waiting for every classic feature

In an at-risk patient with otherwise unexplained pelvic pain, cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness can justify presumptive PID treatment; all three are not required. Fever and positive cervical NAAT are not mandatory. Exclude pregnancy-related emergencies. Pregnancy, abscess, severe illness, inability to tolerate/follow therapy, an unexcluded surgical emergency or failed outpatient care favors hospitalization. [CDC PID diagnostic and treatment framework](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/pid.htm)

For eligible mild-to-moderate outpatient PID, give ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once plus doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily and metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily, both for 14 days. With documented gonorrhea and weight over 150 kg, the CDC PID footnote specifies ceftriaxone 1 g. Reassess within 72 hours; nonresponse requires escalation. Avoid intercourse until therapy is completed, symptoms resolve and partners are treated. An existing IUD need not be removed immediately; reconsider with nonresponse.

**Clinical Case: PID nonresponse.** Persistent pain three days into treatment requires reassessment for abscess, surgical disease, and escalation despite negative cervical NAAT.

Cervicitis warrants gonorrhea/chlamydia NAAT, BV/trichomoniasis assessment and evaluation for PID. When empiric treatment is indicated by exposure risk or unreliable testing/follow-up, the CDC nonpregnant regimen is doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for seven days; add gonorrhea coverage when indicated. Pregnancy changes treatment. See 09B for organism-specific regimens, pregnancy alternatives, partner treatment and retesting. Persistent cervicitis requires reassessment for reinfection, other organisms or noninfectious lesions, not indefinite antibiotics. A suspicious visible lesion needs diagnostic assessment even when screening is current. [CDC cervicitis pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/urethritis-and-cervicitis.htm)

### Investigate noninfectious vulvar symptoms

Persistent vulvar symptoms suggest dermatitis, lichen sclerosus/planus, hypoestrogenism, focal lesions, or pain disorders. Pale fragile skin, fissures, and architectural change suggest lichen sclerosus, requiring anti-inflammatory treatment and surveillance. Biopsy suspicious, changing, ulcerated, or resistant lesions. Assess triggers and pelvic-floor involvement; unexplained pain is not automatically psychological. [ACOG vulvar-disorder overview](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/disorders-of-the-vulva-common-causes-of-vulvar-pain-burning-and-itching)

For adult vulvar lichen sclerosus, the BSSVD pathway uses clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment to affected skin nightly for four weeks, alternate nights for four weeks, then twice weekly for four weeks. Demonstrate the amount and site, add an emollient and review response and application technique; many patients need twice-weekly maintenance. Continue at least annual surveillance and promptly assess a persistent ulcer, induration or new lump for biopsy rather than simply increasing steroid. [BSSVD treatment and follow-up](https://bssvd.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/CRIB-SHEET-FOR-CLINIC-LICHEN-SCLEROSUS-vs-8.pdf)

### Describe abnormal bleeding before naming its cause

Describe cycle frequency, regularity, duration, intermenstrual/postcoital bleeding, and functional burden. Consider pregnancy testing, CBC, iron deficiency, and bleeding disorders. Heavy flow since menarche, bruising, or surgical/dental bleeding suggests systemic disease. Medicines and contraception can contribute without fully explaining persistent symptoms. [ACOG abnormal bleeding overview](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/abnormal-uterine-bleeding)

PALM-COEIN separates polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma, and malignancy/hyperplasia from coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, endometrial, iatrogenic, and unclassified causes. Mechanisms coexist. Chronic anovulation causes irregular shedding and unopposed-estrogen hyperplasia risk; predictable heavy cycles still warrant structural/hematologic assessment when indicated. [FIGO bleeding terminology and classification](https://obgyn.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ijgo.12666)

| Pattern or finding | Possibilities to investigate | What should not be assumed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistent intermenstrual bleeding | Polyp, cervical lesion, endometrial disease | That a normal screening Pap excludes the cause |
| Heavy flow with pelvic pressure | Fibroids; other structural disease | That every incidental fibroid explains all symptoms |
| Painful heavy periods and bulky tender uterus | Adenomyosis, with other pain causes considered | That ultrasound alone explains every pelvic symptom |
| Irregular prolonged gaps followed by heavy flow | Anovulation and endometrial effects | That young age eliminates hyperplasia risk |
| Lifelong heavy flow with mucosal bleeding | Coagulopathy | That menstrual history is unrelated to systemic bleeding |

Under the named ACOG reproductive-age pathway, endometrial sampling is a first-line consideration for AUB in patients older than 45 and in younger patients with unopposed-estrogen exposure, persistent bleeding or failed treatment. Ultrasound assesses structural disease; suspected focal pathology or persistent symptoms after nondiagnostic/benign sampling may need hysteroscopy and directed assessment. A premenopausal endometrial measurement is not interchangeable with a postmenopausal triage rule. [ACOG age- and risk-based assessment](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2013/04/management-of-acute-abnormal-uterine-bleeding-in-nonpregnant-reproductive-aged-women)

After pregnancy and dangerous pathology have been addressed, therapy can reduce bleeding while evaluation continues. Depending on eligibility and cause, options include an appropriate levonorgestrel IUD, combined contraception, progestin, tranexamic acid or NSAIDs; treat iron deficiency too. NICE's HMB treatment framework favors an LNG-IUS for selected patients without relevant cavity distortion or other concerning pathology. Choice changes with thrombosis risk, renal/GI disease, pregnancy goals and local labeling. Large/cavity-distorting fibroids, refractory symptoms or suspected cancer require specialist planning. Ablation is not contraception and is unsuitable when future pregnancy is desired; hysterectomy and uterine-preserving procedures have different consequences. [NICE symptom- and cause-directed treatment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Recommendations)

### Evaluate postmenopausal bleeding under the updated pathway

Uterine bleeding at least 12 months after the final period requires evaluation, however light. ACOG's April 2026 pathway recommends both transvaginal ultrasound and tissue sampling initially for most patients; thickness of 4 mm or less is not a universal sampling exemption. Explain limitations and resolve inadequate samples or persistent/recurrent bleeding. [ACOG 2026 postmenopausal-bleeding update](https://www.acog.org/news/news-releases/2026/04/acog-publishes-updated-guidance-evaluation-postmenopausal-bleeding)

**Clinical Case: Postmenopausal spotting.** At 63, new spotting with a 3-mm endometrium still merits discussion of tissue sampling. Confirm the source and pursue inadequate sampling or recurrent bleeding.

### Approach amenorrhea through the reproductive axis

First exclude pregnancy, using serum testing when a negative urine result does not resolve clinical concern. ASRM recommends investigation after more than three months without menses after previously regular cycles, or six months with previously irregular cycles. History should cover weight/energy change, exercise, stress, medicines, galactorrhea, headaches/vision change, androgen symptoms and prior uterine procedures. TSH, prolactin, FSH and estradiol help separate thyroid/prolactin, ovarian and hypothalamic-pituitary causes; targeted examination and pelvic ultrasound add anatomic information. Primary amenorrhea has a developmental/anatomic pathway, not simply a longer wait for adult testing. [ASRM 2024 amenorrhea evaluation](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/current-evaluation-of-amenorrhea/)

High FSH/low estradiol suggests ovarian dysfunction; low/normal gonadotropins with low estradiol suggests central or functional disease. Interpret with medicines, timing, and illness. Confirm persistent prolactin elevation without abruptly stopping essential antipsychotics. A progestin-withdrawal response supports anovulation; absent bleeding may instead reflect low estrogen or outflow/endometrial disease.

Investigate absent breast development by age 13 or absent menarche by age 15 despite otherwise normal pubertal development. Establish breast development and whether a uterus/outflow tract is present. With developed breasts but no uterus, testosterone and karyotype help distinguish Mullerian agenesis from complete androgen insensitivity; absent breasts with a uterus directs attention to ovarian versus central causes using FSH/estradiol. Cyclic pain without bleeding can indicate outflow obstruction. Refer for developmental assessment rather than ordering an adult anovulation panel alone. [ASRM developmental pathway](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/current-evaluation-of-amenorrhea/)

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is a diagnosis of exclusion linked to low energy, exercise, or stress. Restore energy with nutritional/psychological support and assess bones and stability; normal BMI does not ensure adequacy. Do not prescribe contraception solely for periods or bone density while leaving the deficit untreated. Severe bradycardia, hypotension, orthostasis, or electrolyte abnormalities may require admission. Investigate severe headache, visual change, or other CNS signs. [Endocrine Society functional amenorrhea guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/hypothalamic-amenorrhea)

Premature ovarian insufficiency occurs before 40. The current joint guideline uses at least four months of disordered cycles with FSH above 25 IU/L, repeating FSH when diagnostic uncertainty remains rather than mandating two tests in every case. Coordinate etiologic assessment, fertility counseling, bone/cardiovascular health and appropriate hormone replacement, usually to the usual menopausal age when eligible. Intermittent ovarian activity may occur: hormone replacement is not reliable contraception, and the diagnosis is not a promise that spontaneous conception is impossible. [Joint POI guidance](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/evidence-based-guideline-premature-ovarian-insufficiency--2024/)

### Recognize the syndrome historically called PCOS

The 2023 international clinical framework remains in use after the May 2026 renaming of PCOS to polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS). Both names remain relevant during transition. Neither ovarian cysts nor ovarian appearance alone defines the disorder. [ASRM terminology update](https://www.asrm.org/news-and-events/asrm-news/latest-news/may-27-2026-pcos-is-now-pmos-understanding-the-name-change/)

In adults, diagnosis requires two of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction and characteristic ovarian morphology, after excluding other causes; appropriately interpreted AMH can substitute for ultrasound in adults, not serve as a standalone test. When irregular cycles and hyperandrogenism coexist, imaging/AMH is not required. Adolescents require both ovulatory dysfunction and hyperandrogenism; ultrasound and AMH lack sufficient specificity. Rapid virilization or marked androgen change requires evaluation beyond this syndrome. [International diagnostic and management recommendations](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/recommendations-from-the-2023-international-evidence-based-guideline-for-the-assessment-and-management-of-polyendocrine-metabolic-ovarian-syndrome-2023)

Address menstrual/endometrial protection, androgen symptoms, glucose/lipid/BP risk, sleep symptoms and mental health. Eligible combined contraception or progestin can manage cycles; metformin has a metabolic role, not a universal cure for every symptom. Support activity and nutrition without stigma. For anovulatory infertility without other infertility factors, letrozole is the guideline's first pharmacologic ovulation-induction option; exclude preexisting pregnancy and discuss local off-label use. Contraceptive suppression and fertility treatment serve different goals.

Assess glycemia at diagnosis and every one to three years according to risk; a 75-g OGTT is the guideline's most accurate test regardless of BMI. Obtain a lipid profile at diagnosis and measure BP annually and before pregnancy. Routine insulin assays do not improve clinical classification. These checks are not replaced by prescribing a pill for cycle control. [International metabolic follow-up recommendations](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/recommendations-from-the-2023-international-evidence-based-guideline-for-the-assessment-and-management-of-polyendocrine-metabolic-ovarian-syndrome-2023)

**Clinical Case: Beyond cycle control.** For infrequent cycles and hirsutism without pregnancy goals, exclude alternatives and provide endometrial protection plus metabolic, sleep, and mental-health care. Follicle-rich ultrasound is neither universally required nor sufficient. Revisit treatment when pregnancy goals change.

### Separate primary cramps from secondary pelvic pain

Primary dysmenorrhea reflects menstrual prostaglandin effects without an underlying pelvic lesion. Appropriate NSAID use and, when desired and eligible, hormonal suppression can help; check allergy, renal/GI disease and bleeding risks rather than assuming an over-the-counter medicine is harmless. Progressive pain, dyspareunia, pain between periods, infertility, abnormal bleeding, a mass or poor response warrants assessment for a secondary cause. Endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, infection and nongynecologic bowel, urinary or musculoskeletal disease can overlap. [ACOG painful-period overview](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/dysmenorrhea-painful-periods)

Endometriosis is inflammatory endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus. ACOG's February 2026 guideline supports clinical diagnosis and empiric treatment while imaging proceeds, without mandatory surgery first. Normal examination or routine imaging cannot exclude all disease; imaging helps identify ovarian/deep disease and alternatives. [ACOG 2026 diagnostic update](https://www.acog.org/news/news-releases/2026/02/acog-publishes-new-endometriosis-clinical-guidance-aiming-shorten-time-diagnosis-improve-access-care)

For pain, discuss analgesia and eligible combined hormonal contraception or progestin treatment, matched to preferences, side effects and pregnancy plans. Under the ESHRE management framework, persistent symptoms, unsuitable empiric treatment or complex disease may require surgical/specialist assessment; GnRH therapies need product-specific risks and bone/add-back planning. Hormonal suppression is not a treatment to improve natural fertility while actively trying to conceive. Chronic pelvic pain may also need pelvic-floor therapy and coordinated bowel/bladder and psychological support; these do not imply that the pain is imagined. [ESHRE endometriosis management recommendations](https://www.eshre.eu/-/media/sitecore-files/Guidelines/Endometriosis/ESHRE-GUIDELINE-ENDOMETRIOSIS-2022_2.pdf)

Sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain with vomiting raises concern for adnexal torsion. Obtain urgent gynecologic/surgical evaluation when suspected; preserved Doppler flow does not exclude torsion. Consider ectopic pregnancy whenever biologically possible, as well as ruptured/hemorrhagic cyst, infection, appendicitis and urinary disease. Do not wait for a scheduled outpatient pain visit when the pattern changes acutely. An adolescent torsion pathway emphasizes timely assessment and ovarian preservation when feasible rather than assuming a dark-appearing ovary must be removed. [ACOG adolescent torsion guidance](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2019/08/adnexal-torsion-in-adolescents)

**Clinical Case: Persistent cyclic pain.** Worsening cyclic pain and deep dyspareunia despite correctly used NSAIDs merit clinical endometriosis assessment, eligible empiric treatment, and referral despite normal ultrasound. New acute unilateral pain requires urgent reassessment.

### Match menopause treatment to the symptom and risk

Menopause is recognized after 12 months without menses when no other cause explains it. Perimenopause does not exclude pregnancy or remove the need to assess abnormal bleeding. Separate vasomotor from genitourinary symptoms; consider thyroid, medication, sleep, and mood alternatives. Treat the symptom that impairs function.

The Menopause Society's currently listed 2022 statement supports individualized systemic hormone therapy for bothersome symptoms, with a generally favorable benefit-risk profile for eligible healthy patients younger than 60 and within ten years of menopause onset. Age/timing is not the only assessment: discuss route, dose, personal risks and periodic reassessment. Starting later has different absolute risks; no single birthday automatically decides every continuation question. Do not prescribe menopausal hormone therapy solely to prevent cardiovascular disease or dementia. [Menopause Society hormone-therapy statement summary](https://menopause.org/wp-content/uploads/press-release/ht-position-statement-release.pdf)

For systemic estrogen in a patient with a uterus, provide adequate endometrial protection with an appropriate progestogen or a specifically indicated alternative regimen. Unexplained bleeding needs assessment first. Prior hormone-sensitive cancer, VTE, stroke, myocardial infarction or significant liver disease generally changes systemic treatment selection and may warrant specialist input. Low-dose local vaginal therapy has a different exposure profile; do not transfer all systemic rules without examining the actual product. FDA-approved formulations are preferred over assuming compounded hormones are safer. [ACOG hormone selection and contraindications](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/hormone-therapy-for-menopause)

In February 2026, FDA removed selected cardiovascular, breast-cancer, and probable-dementia boxed-warning statements from six products. This product-specific change does not eliminate risk or make formulations interchangeable. Use individual labels and clinical assessment; systemic estrogen-alone still requires attention to endometrial protection. Requested class changes differ from approved product changes. [FDA approved labeling update](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-labeling-changes-menopausal-hormone-therapy-products)

Vasomotor options include selected SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, oxybutynin, and supported behavioral therapies. Consider sedation, anticholinergic effects, comorbidity, and cancer-treatment interactions. The 2023 Menopause Society statement does not recommend supplements or clonidine as routine preferred therapy. [Menopause Society nonhormone recommendations](https://www.menopause.org/docs/default-source/professional/2023-nonhormone-therapy-position-statement.pdf)

Neurokinin drugs have different labels. Fezolinetant is 45 mg orally daily, with liver tests before treatment, monthly for months one through three, then at months six and nine. Cirrhosis, severe renal impairment/end-stage renal disease and CYP1A2 inhibitors are contraindications. Do not start if ALT/AST or total bilirubin is at least twice the upper limit of normal. Stop for transaminases above five times normal, or above three times with bilirubin above twice normal; new liver-injury symptoms require immediate stopping and assessment. [Current fezolinetant label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cae9f798-24f9-4580-a4fc-e6c710cbda3c)

Elinzanetant is 120 mg orally at bedtime; its August 2026 label requires liver tests before treatment and at three months, pregnancy exclusion and contraception through two weeks after treatment. Reduce to 60 mg with a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor; avoid strong inhibitors and strong/moderate inducers. Use caution with a seizure history or other lowered seizure threshold; the current warning includes postmarketing seizures. Avoid driving while somnolence or other CNS effects persist. End-stage renal disease and moderate/severe hepatic impairment are not recommended settings. Do not substitute fezolinetant's monitoring schedule. [Current elinzanetant label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=f42884ff-7dff-419c-8a0c-affe2ed73818)

### Treat genitourinary symptoms without overlooking other disease

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause can cause dryness, burning, painful sex and urinary symptoms. Examine and consider infection, dermatoses and malignancy when appropriate; not every dysuria episode is bacterial cystitis. Offer moisturizers/lubricants and discuss low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA or oral ospemifene when suitable. Local therapy can still be useful when systemic treatment does not resolve local symptoms. A history of breast cancer calls for multidisciplinary shared decision making rather than an unsupervised systemic prescription or a blanket refusal of all symptom care. Evidence does not support routine vaginal laser/radiofrequency as established GSM treatment. [AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 GSM recommendations](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/GSM%20Unabridged%202025.pdf)

**Clinical Case: Isolated vaginal symptoms.** A 56-year-old with dryness and painful sex but no hot flashes needs examination and local-treatment discussion, not automatic systemic estrogen. Investigate any postmenopausal bleeding first.

### Evaluate a breast lump diagnostically, not as routine screening

Document location, duration, growth, cyclicity, tenderness, skin/nipple change, nodes and relevant family/personal history. Cysts, fibroadenomas, fat necrosis and lactational lesions can resemble concerning processes on palpation. Neither tenderness nor mobility reliably excludes malignancy. Select diagnostic imaging, correlate it with the palpable site and resolve discordance; a recent normal screening mammogram is not a complete workup for a new lump. [ACR palpable-mass guidance](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69495/Narrative)

| Patient with a new palpable mass | Initial imaging direction in the ACR framework | Follow-through |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Younger than 30 | Targeted ultrasound | Add appropriate imaging/biopsy if suspicious |
| Age 30-39 | Ultrasound and/or diagnostic mammography/DBT, guided by context | Correlate complementary findings with the examination |
| Age 40 or older | Diagnostic mammography/DBT, with targeted ultrasound as indicated | Complete diagnostic assessment rather than screening alone |

Suspicious or discordant palpable findings may need core biopsy despite reassuring imaging. Probably benign findings require scheduled surveillance; concordant simple cysts differ from complex cystic/solid lesions. Assign result communication and follow up missed appointments. [ACR/RSNA diagnostic follow-up overview](https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/acs-palpable-breast-masses)

Breast pain without a lump or other suspicious finding is triaged differently: diffuse or cyclic pain generally needs no diagnostic imaging beyond age/risk-appropriate screening. Focal noncyclic pain warrants targeted ultrasound below age 30 and diagnostic mammography/DBT and/or ultrasound from age 30. Re-examine if a lump, skin change or pathologic discharge develops. [ACR/RSNA breast-pain pathway](https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/acs-breast-pain)

Spontaneous, unilateral, single-duct, clear/serous or bloody nipple discharge warrants diagnostic assessment. Discharge expressed from multiple ducts on both sides is more often physiologic, but associated findings can change the plan. Use age-appropriate ultrasound and diagnostic mammography/DBT rather than assuming all discharge requires immediate duct surgery. [ACR/RSNA discharge guidance](https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/acs-eval-nipple-discharge) Milky discharge outside expected lactation warrants pregnancy, medication, prolactin and thyroid assessment as indicated; do not abruptly discontinue an essential psychiatric medicine. [NIDDK prolactin evaluation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/prolactinoma)

Rapid diffuse swelling, skin thickening, peau d'orange, nipple inversion or nodes can reflect inflammatory breast cancer, sometimes without a discrete mass. Nonresolving apparent infection needs reassessment rather than repeated antibiotics without a diagnostic endpoint. Prompt assessment may include imaging and breast/skin biopsy. [NCI inflammatory breast cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/types/breast/breast-cancer-types/inflammatory) Persistent nipple eczema can represent Paget disease and also needs directed evaluation. Do not let a normal screening interval or absent family history delay assessment. [NCI Paget disease](https://www.cancer.gov/types/breast/breast-cancer-types/paget-disease-breast)

Lactational inflammation is a spectrum; fever/redness does not mean every case needs antibiotics. Support physiologic feeding, cold/appropriate analgesia and lactation assessment; avoid deep massage and excessive pumping to empty the breast. Suspected bacterial disease needs appropriate antibiotics and follow-up. If not improving after about 48 hours of first-line therapy, consider milk culture and resistance; a persistent focal collection may need ultrasound and drainage. Breastfeeding can often continue with bacterial mastitis. Severe illness, inability to take fluids or concern for sepsis requires escalation. [ABM mastitis-spectrum protocol](https://www.bfmed.org/assets/ABM%20Protocol%20%2336.pdf)

**Clinical Case: A new lump after screening.** A 44-year-old develops a firm lump two months after normal screening. Obtain diagnostic evaluation of that site; persistent clinical-imaging discordance needs specialist review and tissue assessment when indicated, not annual-screening recall.

### Ask about pelvic-floor function, not only a visible bulge

Prolapse may involve vaginal walls, uterus, or apex. Ask about bulge, leakage, emptying, bowel/sexual function, discomfort, and goals. Anterior prolapse can obstruct urine flow and affect the upper tract. Examine with consent and check residual urine when indicated. Retention, renal consequences, unexplained bleeding, or suspicious lesions require further assessment. [NIDDK cystocele assessment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/cystocele)

| Functional complaint | Distinction to make | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leakage with cough/exertion | Stress versus urgency/mixed symptoms | Treatment should match the dominant mechanism |
| Urgency with leakage | Bladder symptoms versus infection, retention or other disease | A drug is not the answer to every urinary symptom |
| Bulge with slow stream/incomplete emptying | Prolapse with possible obstruction | Assess emptying and complications |
| Pain with attempted contraction or penetration | Overactive/tender versus weak pelvic-floor muscles | More strengthening may not be the appropriate first step |

Using the named NICE conservative-care framework, offer supervised pelvic-floor muscle training for at least three months for stress/mixed incontinence; urgency/mixed symptoms can benefit from bladder training for at least six weeks. Selected symptomatic stage 1-2 prolapse can receive at least 16 weeks of supervised muscle training. A pessary is an option alone or with therapy, with fitting, removal and review arrangements. Discuss discharge, bleeding, discomfort and urinary effects. Support constipation management and realistic activity adjustments; surgery is individualized when symptoms remain bothersome or complications require it. Different mesh procedures and their risks must not be conflated. [NICE pelvic-floor and prolapse management](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations)

**Clinical Case: Pessary follow-up.** A caregiver prefers a pessary for symptomatic prolapse. Arrange fitting, emptying assessment, removal/review, and prompt contact for bleeding, pain, or difficulty voiding.

### Close the loop on symptoms and results

Explain supported and uncertain diagnoses and warning signs. Assign cultures, pathology, imaging, partner care, and referrals; reassess response, adverse effects, and function. Persistent symptoms require follow-through despite reassuring results.
