# Medivale Family Medicine

## 14D. Postpartum and Reproductive Follow-Up

Hemorrhage, infection, hypertension, thrombosis, and psychiatric illness may first appear after discharge. Ask directly about recovery, feeding, sleep, pelvic function, relationships, and home support; attending a visit does not establish uncomplicated recovery.

ACOG supplies the main postpartum-care framework; CDC U.S. MEC/SPR 2024 governs contraception. WHO's 2025 immediate-hemorrhage pathway is distinct from outpatient warning signs. ADA 2026 and ASRM 2026 guide metabolic follow-up and recurrent loss, respectively. Hospital rescue protocols do not replace early recognition and urgent transfer from primary care.

### Arrange continuing care before the patient leaves

Review gestation, delivery, blood loss/transfusion, lacerations, anesthesia, infection, pressure/glucose, and unresolved results. Invite discussion or debriefing of difficult birth experiences. Assign each unresolved problem, including newborn prevention, to a clinician.

ACOG recommends contact with a maternal-care provider within three weeks, continuing assessment as needed and a comprehensive postpartum visit no later than 12 weeks. These are outer limits for routine planning, not permission to postpone acute concerns. A hypertensive disorder requires blood-pressure evaluation no later than 7-10 days; severe hypertension requires assessment within 72 hours. Schedule earlier problem-specific reviews for wounds, feeding, anemia or mood when indicated. Remote checks require usable equipment, clear reporting thresholds and a route to in-person assessment. [ACOG postpartum-care framework](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2018/05/optimizing-postpartum-care)

Review physical/emotional recovery, sleep, feeding, sexuality, contraception, disease, and prevention. Identify practical caregiving and sleep support, medication access, safe private communication, and language/disability needs. Arrange follow-up rather than only supplying phone numbers. [ACOG postpartum checklist](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/health-tools/my-postpartum-care-checklist)

### Distinguish expected recovery from a dangerous trajectory

Uterine contraction controls placental-bed bleeding and reduces uterine size; breastfeeding-related oxytocin can intensify cramps. Lochia should generally diminish with healing. Increasing bleeding, large clots, faintness, fever, or worsening pain requires assessment regardless of days since birth.

Assess vital signs, bleeding, abdominal/perineal or incisional pain, urination, bowel function and ability to move and care for oneself. Persistent or worsening perineal pain can reflect hematoma, infection, wound breakdown or pelvic-floor injury. Ask about urinary retention, dysuria, leakage, stool control and wound drainage. Examination should be symptom-directed, explained and consented to; do not defer a concerning finding until a routine six-week visit. [NICE postnatal physical assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194/chapter/Recommendations)

| Finding after discharge | Why it matters | Appropriate response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rapidly heavy bleeding, faintness or collapse | Hemorrhage and impaired circulation | Emergency evaluation; do not wait to count more pads |
| Fever with uterine, wound or breast symptoms | Infection, potentially sepsis | Prompt examination; instability needs emergency care |
| Persistent severe headache, visual change or upper-abdominal pain | Hypertensive or neurological emergency | Check pressure and arrange urgent obstetric assessment |
| Chest pain, breathlessness, orthopnea or one-sided leg swelling | PE, heart failure or other cardiopulmonary disease | Urgent diagnostic evaluation, not presumed exhaustion |
| Delusions, confusion, mania or imminent harm risk | Psychiatric/medical emergency | Maintain safety and obtain emergency assessment |

Pad counts are warning aids, not minimum thresholds for help; syncope or dyspnea can signal danger without visible bleeding. Patients should mention recent pregnancy when seeking care, even beyond early postpartum weeks. [CDC urgent maternal warning signs](https://www.cdc.gov/hearher/maternal-warning-signs/index.html)

### Recognize hemorrhage and treat circulation and cause together

Primary hemorrhage occurs within the first 24 hours. Use the **four Ts** to assess overlapping causes: tone (atony), trauma, tissue (retained placenta/products) and thrombin (coagulation disorder). Quantify blood loss, reassess vital signs and examine for the source. A firm uterus does not exclude laceration or concealed bleeding. Substantial loss can precede a late fall in pressure or a meaningful change in hemoglobin.

Activate the obstetric hemorrhage response, secure large-bore IV access and initiate monitored resuscitation and blood-product preparation as needed while assessing the source. For the immediate postpartum setting, the WHO 2025 framework promotes early action with measured loss of at least 500 mL, or at least 300 mL with abnormal vital signs, rather than waiting for shock. Its first-response bundle combines uterine massage, a uterotonic, tranexamic acid, IV fluids, genital-tract assessment and escalation. These are coordinated interventions, not a prolonged sequence in which every earlier step must fail. [WHO 2025 immediate-hemorrhage recognition and response](https://www.who.int/news/item/05-10-2025-global-health-agencies-issue-new-recommendations-to-help-end-deaths-from-postpartum-haemorrhage)

For diagnosed acute PPH, WHO recommends early IV tranexamic acid within three hours of birth alongside standard care. The studied regimen is 1 g IV over 10 minutes, with another 1 g if bleeding continues after 30 minutes or restarts within 24 hours. Check contraindications; this recommendation is not routine prophylaxis or a rule for starting TXA days after birth. Uterotonics do not repair a laceration, remove retained tissue or correct a coagulopathy. Ongoing bleeding may require tamponade, procedural/surgical control and transfusion. [WHO consolidated treatment recommendations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK619233/)

IV oxytocin is WHO's first-line uterotonic for treatment, even after prophylactic oxytocin. Its 2025 remarks describe an initial 10 IU diluted and given slowly over 1-2 minutes, or infused over 5-10 minutes, with maintenance titrated through the monitored protocol; never give a rapid undiluted IV injection. [WHO treatment recommendation 24](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK619233/table/ch3.tab28/) Review alternative-drug contraindications: [methylergonovine](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a7ec8dfe-9b58-4363-a709-c1b72ee67855) is unsuitable in hypertension, and [carboprost](https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=12134) is generally avoided in asthma. This is monitored emergency care, not an outpatient drug trial.

Persistent bleeding after the first day warrants assessment for retained products, infection, impaired placental-site involution and less common vascular lesions. Interpret ultrasound with symptoms, examination and hemodynamics; an echogenic finding alone does not establish retained tissue. [ACOG postpartum hemorrhage scope](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-bulletin/articles/2017/10/postpartum-hemorrhage)

After hemorrhage, reassess anemia and iron needs despite transfusion. Choose oral/IV iron by severity, tolerance, response, and urgency, distinguishing stable recovery from active bleeding. Record future-pregnancy implications and assess trauma-related distress.

### Evaluate infection without dismissing fever as normal recovery

Ascending polymicrobial endometritis causes fever, uterine tenderness, pain, or malodorous discharge; not all are required. Cesarean birth and prolonged rupture increase risk. Consider retained tissue, urinary/wound infection, mastitis, and other sources. Severe pain, tachycardia, hypotension, or altered mentation requires urgent sepsis assessment and treatment.

Significant suspected endometritis generally requires inpatient IV antibiotics and obstetric assessment. WHO recommends clindamycin/gentamicin as one option, adjusted for allergy, renal function, resistance, and course. Obtain specimens without delaying therapy and assess source control. Nonresponse warrants investigation for resistance, abscess, retained tissue, or septic pelvic thrombophlebitis. [WHO maternal peripartum infection recommendations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK327075/)

An incisional infection needs assessment of wound depth, drainage and fascial integrity; a superficial-looking opening can conceal a deeper problem. Fluctuance, necrotic tissue or severe disproportionate pain changes urgency and the need for source control. Dysuria may represent cystitis, but fever/flank pain suggests upper-tract disease. Use breastfeeding-appropriate medication without allowing lactation concerns to delay treatment of serious illness.

**Clinical Case: Infection beyond the incision.** Nine days after cesarean birth, fever, lower-abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, and persistent tachycardia require urgent obstetric assessment and monitored treatment despite clean skin.

### Do not let delivery end hypertension surveillance

Preeclampsia can first present postpartum, including after a normotensive pregnancy, and may occur up to six weeks after birth. Persistent headache, visual disturbance, epigastric/right-upper-quadrant pain or breathlessness requires assessment; absence of proteinuria does not exclude severe disease. Consider other causes of postpartum headache, including postdural-puncture headache, venous thrombosis, hemorrhage, infection and migraine. An apparent positional feature does not excuse ignoring severe pressure or focal findings. [ACOG postpartum preeclampsia recognition](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/experts-and-stories/the-latest/3-conditions-to-watch-for-after-childbirth)

Persistent severe pressure, at least 160 systolic or 110 diastolic mm Hg, is an emergency. Confirm promptly and activate the severe-hypertension pathway rather than waiting for a routine appointment or a complete urine study. ACOG calls for treatment within 30-60 minutes of confirmed acute severe hypertension. Protocol-selected IV labetalol, IV hydralazine or immediate-release oral nifedipine are options; magnesium sulfate addresses seizure prevention/treatment when indicated and does not replace pressure control. [ACOG acute severe-hypertension guidance, primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30575639/)

For nonsevere postpartum hypertension, arrange medication titration, home readings and timely reassessment. Pregnancy and lactation restrictions differ: NICE's named postnatal pathway offers enalapril with maternal creatinine/potassium monitoring and includes nifedipine among options. Choose for the individual patient and infant, especially with prematurity; monitor poor feeding, lethargy or other possible infant hypotension. This does not make ACE inhibitors suitable during pregnancy. Do not stop all treatment after one normal reading. [NICE postnatal medication and lactation recommendations 1.9](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng133/chapter/recommendations)

**Clinical Case: New postpartum preeclampsia.** Eight days after uncomplicated birth, headache, flashing lights, and repeated pressure 168/112 mm Hg require emergency assessment and acute treatment despite normal prenatal pressures or absent edema.

### Investigate thrombosis and heart failure promptly

Hypercoagulability, stasis, and vascular injury persist after birth; cesarean, immobility, prior VTE, and thrombophilia raise risk. Assess unilateral painful swelling for DVT, generally with compression ultrasound. Chest pain, acute dyspnea, hypoxemia, or syncope requires emergency PE/alternative assessment. Expected changes or nonspecific laboratory results cannot replace indicated imaging.

Treat confirmed VTE with an individualized anticoagulation plan, accounting for bleeding, renal function, recent neuraxial procedures and feeding. ASH considers UFH, LMWH and warfarin among compatible breastfeeding options and recommends against direct oral anticoagulants during breastfeeding. Warfarin's pregnancy limitations should not be automatically transferred to lactation. [ASH pregnancy-associated VTE recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/30482767/)

Prevention depends on risk, not delivery mode alone. Under SMFM's cesarean framework, use perioperative sequential compression until fully ambulatory; patients with prior DVT/PE generally need both mechanical and six weeks of pharmacologic prophylaxis after cesarean delivery. This does not mean every postpartum patient needs six weeks of injections. Follow the institution's risk protocol and coordinate timing with bleeding and anesthesia. [SMFM cesarean thromboprophylaxis](https://cdn.smfm.org.s3.amazonaws.com/attachments/768/b7811ef0e3b6a66c4e186872a32e39fc.pdf)

Progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, edema with respiratory symptoms, or unexplained tachycardia may indicate peripartum cardiomyopathy or other heart failure. Arrange urgent ECG, imaging, and echocardiography as indicated rather than assuming anxiety. Coordinate treatment and future-pregnancy counseling. [AHA 2026 perinatal heart-failure statement overview](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/heart-failure-occurring-in-the-perinatal-period)

### Support feeding with observation and an infant safety check

Support the chosen feeding approach without coercion. Observe latch, positioning, swallowing, pain, and transfer; frequent feeding alone does not prove adequate intake. Coordinate infant weight, hydration, output, and jaundice assessment. Sleepiness with poor intake needs evaluation. [ACOG support for informed feeding decisions](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2018/10/optimizing-support-for-breastfeeding-as-part-of-obstetric-practice)

For engorgement, support effective feeding and express only enough for relief when appropriate; excessive emptying drives oversupply. Assess persistent feeding pain, latch, and other causes. Help with pump fit/cleaning, work, and supply during separation. [ACOG breastfeeding challenges](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2021/02/breastfeeding-challenges)

**Clinical Case: Pump-driven oversupply.** After every comfortable feed, a patient pumps to empty both breasts; fullness rapidly returns without redness or systemic illness. Assess transfer and oversupply, reduce unnecessary stimulation with lactation support, and provide symptom relief.

Inflammatory mastitis is not automatically bacterial. ABM's spectrum approach favors physiological feeding, cold and appropriate analgesia; avoid deep massage and forced emptying. When bacterial mastitis is diagnosed, one first-line oral option is dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily for 10-14 days in an eligible patient. Reassess nonresponse; lack of improvement after 48 hours supports milk culture and review for resistance or abscess. A drainable collection needs source control. Breastfeeding can generally continue, with individualized guidance when a wound might contact the infant. [ABM mastitis protocol](https://www.bfmed.org/assets/ABM%20Protocol%20%2336.pdf)

Breast milk alone does not supply sufficient vitamin D for most infants. Breastfed or partially breastfed infants generally need 400 IU/day beginning soon after birth; coordinate product and dose with pediatric care. Supplementation, when medically needed for inadequate intake, should protect hydration and nutrition while the feeding problem is addressed. [CDC infant vitamin D guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/diet-micronutrients/vitamin-d.html)

### Make lactation decisions specific to the exposure

Assess the exact drug, dose, route, milk exposure, infant age/prematurity, and vulnerability using current lactation references. Balance untreated maternal disease and better-studied alternatives. Pumping once does not remove long-lived drugs; imaging does not universally require discarded milk. [CDC medication and temporary-interruption principles](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/contraindications/)

| Situation | Important distinction | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classic galactosemia in the infant | Human milk contains the relevant sugar substrate | Use the prescribed alternative feeding plan |
| Active breast HSV lesions | Direct lesion exposure; affected-side milk can be contaminated | Protect the infant from that side; unaffected-side feeding may continue with precautions |
| Untreated contagious pulmonary TB | Airborne exposure, not a blanket ban on expressed milk | Coordinate temporary contact precautions and safe milk provision |
| Stable methadone/buprenorphine treatment | Treatment differs from ongoing nonprescribed high-risk drug use | Support feeding within an individualized addiction/pediatric plan |
| HIV with sustained suppression on ART | Low transmission risk is not zero | Use specialist shared decision making and a monitoring plan |

NIH's June 2026 guidance supports patient-centered feeding counseling. Formula or banked pasteurized donor milk eliminates breastfeeding-associated HIV transmission; sustained ART suppression reduces risk below 1%, not to zero. With consistent ART and HIV RNA below 50 copies/mL for at least three months before delivery, discuss the options and support an informed choice. Breastfeeding requires coordinated maternal monitoring and infant prophylaxis/testing. If RNA becomes detectable at 50 copies/mL or higher, temporarily stop or discontinue breastfeeding, start replacement feeding and urgently reassess with the HIV team; most experts recommend permanent discontinuation at 200 copies/mL or higher. Sexual-transmission U=U does not establish zero milk-transmission risk. [NIH current infant-feeding guidance](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/perinatal/preventing-transmission-infant-feeding?view=full)

Hepatitis B or C alone does not automatically prohibit breastfeeding. Ensure the HBV-exposed newborn has received the indicated immunoprophylaxis and follow-up; temporarily interrupt affected feeding when nipples are cracked and bleeding, with expression/discard and lactation support until healing. [CDC hepatitis and breastfeeding](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/illnesses-conditions/hepatitis-b-c.html)

For pain, acetaminophen and ibuprofen are usual first-line postpartum choices when suitable for the patient's renal, gastrointestinal and other conditions. An NSAID restriction late in pregnancy is not a universal breastfeeding prohibition. If stronger analgesia is necessary, use the shortest appropriate monitored plan, consider other sedating medicines and explain infant toxicity signs. [ACOG multimodal postpartum pain care](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2021/09/pharmacologic-stepwise-multimodal-approach-for-postpartum-pain-management) FDA recommends against codeine and tramadol during breastfeeding because of potential infant sedation and respiratory depression. Marked infant sleepiness, poor feeding or breathing difficulty requires immediate assessment. [FDA opioid breastfeeding warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/food-and-drug-administration-overdose-prevention-framework/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-substance-use-and-overdose)

### Distinguish transient mood changes from illness requiring treatment

Assess mood, anxiety, available sleep, intrusive thoughts, function, and safety. Depression can begin antenatally or beyond the first four weeks postpartum. ACOG recommends validated screening such as EPDS/PHQ-9 with assessment/treatment systems; positive scores require diagnostic evaluation. Screen for bipolar disorder before pharmacotherapy if not already done. Self-harm responses require immediate risk assessment. [ACOG perinatal mental-health screening](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/patient-screening)

| Presentation | Distinguishing concern | Management direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild, fluctuating early "blues" | Short-lived symptoms with preserved safety/function | Practical support and reassessment; worsening is not reassurance |
| Depressive illness | Persistent low mood/anhedonia, impairment or severe symptoms | Diagnostic evaluation, treatment and active follow-up |
| Anxiety/OCD-type intrusive thoughts | Distressing unwanted thoughts with insight may differ from intent | Assess meaning, compulsions, intent and safety; arrange treatment |
| Mania, psychosis or severe confusion | Impaired reality testing and potentially rapid deterioration | Emergency medical/psychiatric evaluation and supervised safety |

Mild early mood lability often resolves within two weeks, whereas persistent or severe symptoms require evaluation. Do not wait two weeks when safety, reality testing or basic functioning is impaired. Consider anemia, thyroid disease, medication effects and substance use without using a medical differential to dismiss depression. Postpartum psychosis, including delusions or hallucinations, is an emergency requiring hospitalization. [NIMH perinatal depression and psychosis overview](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/perinatal-depression)

[Unwanted intrusive thoughts](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/anxiety-and-pregnancy) are not automatically an intention to harm, but neither can their risk be inferred from a screening label. Ask about insight, desire, plans, command experiences, access and ability to maintain safety. Psychosis, intent or inability to care safely for oneself or the infant requires emergency intervention. Arrange a trusted caregiver and supervision while obtaining help; do not leave the infant solely with an acutely psychotic parent.

Choose psychotherapy, antidepressants, or both by severity, prior response, and preference. Arrange sleep/caregiving support, monitor symptoms, and confirm access to referred care. [ACOG integrated perinatal mental-health care](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/integrating-mental-health-care-into-ob-practice-guide/aims)

Sertraline is often preferred during breastfeeding because exposure is low, but continuing another previously effective agent may be better than switching. Review premature/fragile infants closely; SSRIs are not risk-free. Monitor maternal response, activation, adverse effects, and infant feeding/growth. [LactMed sertraline monograph](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

Zuranolone's April 2026 label specifies 50 mg orally each evening with fat-containing food for 14 days. Reduce to 30 mg for eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, Child-Pugh C hepatic impairment or a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor; 40 mg may be used for CNS intolerance. Avoid CYP3A4 inducers and review additive sedatives. No driving or hazardous activity for at least 12 hours after each dose, even if the patient feels unimpaired; arrange capable infant-care support. Use effective contraception during treatment and one week afterward. Low milk levels do not establish infant safety; discuss feeding individually. [Current zuranolone prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=f18e53b0-d0bb-422d-8de7-ab64b7292b29)

ACOG's focused pathway considers zuranolone within 12 months after delivery for severe depression beginning in the third trimester or within four weeks postpartum; FDA's adult-PPD indication is broader. Neither a short course nor rapid improvement replaces continuing care or emergency treatment for psychosis. Section 12B develops psychiatric diagnosis and treatment selection. [ACOG focused update, clinical-scope text](https://pnqinma.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/zuranolone_and_brexanolone_for_the_treatment_of.1381.pdf)

**Clinical Case: Early severe depression.** Ten days after birth, hopelessness, impaired basic care, and self-harm thoughts require immediate assessment of intent, plans, access, and support. Do not wait two weeks: arrange supervision, safe infant care, and urgent risk-matched treatment.

### Restore pelvic function and comfortable activity

Ask about pain, leakage, emptying, pressure, and sexual concerns. Assess persistent symptoms for infection, retention, sphincter injury, prolapse, or pelvic-floor dysfunction; acute retention or neurologic deficits needs urgent evaluation. Tailor therapy to contraction, relaxation, pain, and function rather than prescribing strengthening for every painful pelvic floor. [NICE postpartum pelvic-floor assessment and training](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng210/chapter/Recommendations)

Resume activity according to delivery, healing, and symptoms; intercourse depends on consent, comfort, healing, and contraception, not a deadline. Evaluate dryness before lubricants or selected local treatment. Persistent dyspareunia, bleeding, wounds, incontinence, or function-limiting pain needs follow-up. [ACOG postpartum pain and recovery counseling](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/postpartum-pain-management)

### Plan contraception before ovulation surprises the patient

Ovulation can precede the first period. Lactational amenorrhea is a defined contraceptive method only when all three conditions hold: the infant is younger than six months, menses have not returned and breastfeeding is full or nearly full. Longer feeding gaps and substantial supplementation reduce reliability; arrange another method before those criteria cease to apply. Discuss effectiveness, bleeding effects, reversibility, access and the patient's priorities without pressuring a permanent or long-acting choice. [CDC postpartum method initiation and backup](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html)

Use U.S. MEC 2024 for method safety, not a universal "wait 30 days" rule. Combined estrogen-containing methods are category 4 before 21 days postpartum. Breastfeeding and VTE risks continue to shape eligibility through day 42; after that, breastfeeding alone remains category 2 rather than category 1. Nonbreastfeeding patients have a different 21-42-day risk assessment. Review the actual table and other conditions, including migraine with aura, hypertension and thrombosis. [CDC U.S. MEC 2024 postpartum categories](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/rr7304a1_appendix.htm)

Progestin-only methods and IUDs can provide earlier options when medically eligible, but product-specific initiation and backup rules differ. Immediate postpartum IUD insertion has a greater expulsion risk and requires counseling; postpartum sepsis precludes placement. Confirm pregnancy status appropriately when starting later. An uncertain recent exposure may need emergency contraception or a bridge/retest plan rather than reliance on a negative test obtained too soon. The contraception volume provides detailed method-specific pathways. [CDC selected practice recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/)

**Clinical Case: Incomplete lactational-amenorrhea criteria.** At eight weeks, a patient without menses uses several formula feeds and no overnight breastfeeding. Assess pregnancy risk and initiate a preferred eligible method with backup; amenorrhea alone does not provide protection.

ACOG/SMFM advises avoiding a birth-to-next-conception interval shorter than six months and discussing risks/benefits of pregnancy sooner than 18 months. Individualize for age, fertility, previous cesarean, medical disease and preferences; do not present spacing as a judgment about family choices. Birth-to-conception and birth-to-birth intervals are different quantities. Optimize medicines, nutrition, folate, vaccination and chronic illness before another pregnancy. [ACOG/SMFM interpregnancy care](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/obstetric-care-consensus/articles/2019/01/interpregnancy-care)

### Carry pregnancy complications into the long-term record

After GDM, ADA recommends a fasting 75-g OGTT at 4-12 weeks postpartum using nonpregnancy criteria. A1C alone is less suitable then because pregnancy-related red-cell turnover, blood loss and the preceding glucose profile may distort it. A normal result still requires lifelong screening every 1-3 years. Arrange the actual order, fasting instructions and result review rather than recording "test later." [ADA 2026 postpartum diabetes surveillance](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards)

**Clinical Case: Postpartum glucose intolerance.** Seven weeks after GDM and blood loss, A1C is 5.3%, but 75-g OGTT gives fasting glucose 97 mg/dL and two-hour glucose 162 mg/dL. This establishes nonpregnancy impaired glucose tolerance despite A1C; discuss prevention and follow-up. [ADA nonpregnancy diagnostic criteria](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690183/)

Hypertensive and other adverse pregnancy outcomes identify future cardiovascular risk, not inevitable disease. Record obstetric history and reassess pressure, weight, lipids, glucose, tobacco, and activity. Assign ongoing prevention and future-pregnancy planning. [AHA postpartum cardiovascular-risk statement](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001212)

Postpartum fatigue, palpitations, anxiety, constipation or low mood can also reflect thyroid dysfunction. Check TSH/free T4 and distinguish destructive postpartum thyroiditis from Graves disease. Not every patient has both thyrotoxic and hypothyroid phases; antithyroid drugs do not treat release of stored hormone. Section 04C develops the current ATA pregnancy/postpartum pathway, phase-specific treatment and follow-up. [NIDDK postpartum thyroid-disease distinction](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/pregnancy-thyroid-disease)

### Provide care after loss without demanding a particular response

Loss may bring grief, relief, numbness, or mixed feelings. Ask about preferred terms and willingness to discuss it. Explain findings, uncertainty, choices, and follow-up without blame or replacement-pregnancy assurances. Include support people with permission and assess mood, trauma, and practical needs.

Confirm location and nonviability before irreversible early-loss treatment when the diagnosis is uncertain. A stable patient with confirmed early intrauterine loss can choose expectant, medical or aspiration management when eligible. Infection, instability or major hemorrhage changes the priority. One ACOG medical approach uses mifepristone 200 mg orally before misoprostol 800 micrograms vaginally about 24 hours later; eligibility, access, repeat dosing and confirmation of completion need the full supervised protocol. Provide analgesia, expected-course information and explicit bleeding/infection return precautions. [ACOG early pregnancy loss management](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-bulletin/articles/2018/11/early-pregnancy-loss)

In that protocol, one repeat 800-microgram vaginal misoprostol dose may be needed, no earlier than three hours after the first and typically within seven days. Confirm completion, commonly by ultrasound at 7-14 days or serial hCG when ultrasound is unavailable, with symptoms considered too. Persistent bleeding, infection or diagnostic uncertainty changes follow-up; a thick endometrial stripe alone in an asymptomatic patient does not mandate surgery. [ACOG dosing and completion assessment](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-bulletin/articles/2018/11/early-pregnancy-loss)

For abortion or pregnancy loss before 12 weeks, ACOG's 2024 update suggests forgoing routine Rh testing/immune globulin, with individualized shared decisions. This does not change routine antenatal/postpartum prevention or every ectopic/trauma pathway. Other active frameworks differ; identify the chosen protocol. [ACOG early-loss Rh update, primary supplement](https://cdn-links.lww.com/permalink/aog/d/aog_144_6_2024_08_26_cpggynecology_24-1499_sdc1.pdf)

Review completion, unresolved bleeding, emotional recovery and pregnancy intentions. Earlier birth-spacing advice should not automatically become a mandatory waiting interval after every uncomplicated early miscarriage. Ectopic pregnancy, molar disease, a particular treatment or major complication can require a different surveillance/conception plan. Offer contraception if desired and preconception care when trying again. [ACOG care across pregnancy outcomes](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/obstetric-care-consensus/articles/2019/01/interpregnancy-care)

ASRM's 2026 recurrent-pregnancy-loss definition includes two or more spontaneous losses, excluding confirmed ectopic and molar pregnancies; hCG confirmation is sufficient, so ultrasound documentation is not required for every loss. Refer for a stepwise evaluation rather than insisting on three losses or dismissing biochemical pregnancies. Genetic evaluation of miscarriage tissue, uterine assessment and selected endocrine/antiphospholipid testing depend on the history and results. Avoid indiscriminate inherited-thrombophilia/immune panels or empirical anticoagulation for unexplained loss. [ASRM 2026 recurrent-loss recommendations](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/recurrent-pregnancy-loss-a-committee-opinion-2026)

**Clinical Case: hCG-confirmed recurrent loss.** Two pregnancies confirmed by positive then declining hCG ended before ultrasound, without confirmed ectopic or molar disease. ASRM 2026 includes these losses. Review records, provide support, and arrange evaluation.

Distinguish infertility from recurrent loss. See 14A for age/risk-based referral and parallel ovulatory, tubal, and semen testing, and 14B for amenorrhea/anovulation. Donor-assisted care need not await unsuccessful intercourse. Discuss options, cost, burden, and goals without promising outcomes.
