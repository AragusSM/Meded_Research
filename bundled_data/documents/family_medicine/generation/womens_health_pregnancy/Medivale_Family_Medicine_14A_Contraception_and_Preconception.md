# Medivale Family Medicine

## 14A. Contraception and Preconception

Begin with the patient's reproductive goals. Balance effectiveness with bleeding, privacy, control, cost, side effects, fertility, and access to treatment or removal. Pregnancy preparation also requires medication review, disease control, and time for safe transitions.

CDC's 2024 U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC) and Selected Practice Recommendations (SPR) are the main U.S. contraceptive frameworks; product labeling supplies device-specific durations and restrictions. Preconception and fertility decisions use the named CDC, ACOG, ADA and ASRM pathways below. Procedural consent and access rules remain jurisdiction-specific.

### Start with preferences, not a method hierarchy

Ask about pregnancy goals, prior methods, menstrual symptoms, sexual practices, pregnancy possibility, health, medicines, and access. Explain reversible and permanent options without assumptions about age, parity, disability, income, or relationships. Patient control or avoiding procedures may outweigh maximal effectiveness. Respect declining, switching, and removal. [CDC person-centered prescribing framework](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/rr7303a1.htm)

Privately assess pressure to conceive, contraceptive sabotage, and fear of discovery. Establish safe communication and explain confidentiality limits. Eligible adolescents can use highly effective reversible methods without prior pregnancy; jurisdictional consent rules still apply. If the preferred method is unavailable, offer a suitable bridge and realistic referral. [AAP adolescent-centered contraceptive counseling](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2025-072217/202144/Contraception-for-Adolescents-Policy-Statement)

### Explain effectiveness in terms of actual use

Typical-use failure describes pregnancies over a year of real-world use, not failure per sexual act. The gap from perfect use is larger when a method depends on daily dosing or use with every exposure. CDC estimates roughly seven pregnancies per 100 users annually for pills, patches and rings, four for injectable contraception and 13 for external condoms; IUDs and the implant have rates below one per 100. These are counseling estimates, not a prediction for one patient. [CDC effectiveness overview](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/about/)

| Method family | Practical advantage | Counseling issue that changes selection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levonorgestrel IUD | Long-acting; often reduces bleeding | Placement, initial spotting and product-specific duration |
| Copper IUD | Long-acting without hormones | May increase bleeding/cramps; device-specific duration |
| Etonogestrel implant | Long-acting without a uterine procedure | Unpredictable bleeding, interactions and insertion/removal planning |
| Combined pill, patch or ring | User-controlled with cycle benefits | Estrogen eligibility, adherence and formulation-specific instructions |
| Progestin-only pill or injection | Avoids estrogen | Pill-specific timing or reinjection; injection has distinct risks |
| Barrier, fertility-awareness or permanent method | Different preferences and degrees of permanence | Exposure-by-exposure use, training or irreversible intent |

Hormonal methods and IUDs do not prevent STIs; add condoms when appropriate, avoiding natural-membrane products for STI protection. Teach correct use and compatible lubrication, not routine spermicide for STI prevention. Fertility awareness requires validated training; apps alone cannot establish infertile days, especially with irregular cycles. Discuss emergency contraception early. [FDA method overview](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/womens-health-topics/birth-control)

### Separate medical eligibility from prescribing logistics

MEC categorizes method-condition safety: 1, no restriction; 2, benefits generally outweigh risks; 3, risks usually favor alternatives; 4, unacceptable risk. Categories are neither failure rates nor additive scores. Review clarifications for complex disease, anticoagulation, postpartum timing, and interactions. Eligibility does not override product contraindications or patient preference. [CDC MEC classification framework](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/index.html)

Measure blood pressure before combined hormonal contraception and review migraine aura, smoking, thromboembolism, cardiovascular disease, breast cancer, severe liver disease and pregnancy-related risks. A pelvic examination or cervical cancer screening is not routinely required before a pill, patch or ring. Avoid turning preventive tests into a barrier to timely contraception. IUD placement does require an appropriate pelvic assessment, and symptoms may independently warrant investigation. [CDC combined-method initiation](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html)

### Recognize when estrogen changes the risk

Combined methods suppress ovulation through the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and also alter cervical mucus. They may improve cycle control and dysmenorrhea, but estrogen-associated vascular risk makes some otherwise convenient options unsuitable. A patch or vaginal ring still contains estrogen and is not a workaround for an estrogen contraindication. Review new focal neurologic symptoms, chest pain, dyspnea or unilateral leg swelling urgently rather than treating every symptom as an expected hormonal effect.

| Selected finding | Combined hormonal MEC category | Practical interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Migraine with aura | 4 | Choose an appropriate nonestrogen method |
| Confirmed BP 140-159 systolic or 90-99 diastolic | 3 | Usually choose an alternative; address hypertension |
| Confirmed BP at least 160 systolic or 100 diastolic | 4 | Avoid combined methods and manage the hypertension |
| Smoking at age 35 or older | 3 if fewer than 15 cigarettes/day; 4 at 15 or more | Smoking amount and vascular risk matter |

Apply the relevant MEC clarifications. A single high reading does not diagnose hypertension; aura means focal neurologic symptoms, not any headache. VTE eligibility varies with recurrence risk and anticoagulation. Thrombophilia and major surgery with prolonged immobilization can preclude combined methods, but routine population thrombophilia screening is unnecessary. [CDC combined-method risk classifications](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html)

Current breast cancer makes hormonal contraception, including a levonorgestrel IUD, unsuitable under MEC; a copper IUD may remain an option when otherwise eligible. Conversely, a family history of breast cancer alone is not the same condition. Obesity alone is not a universal contraceptive contraindication, but multiple vascular risks and the chosen patch's BMI/weight labeling require review. Severe renal, hepatic, cardiac or hematologic disease needs condition-specific selection, not the shortcut that every progestin is safe whenever estrogen is avoided. [CDC cross-method safety comparison](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/classifications-mec-contraception.html)

For example, sickle cell disease is MEC category 4 for combined hormonal contraception; DMPA is category 2 or 3 according to disease severity and thrombosis risk, not an automatically safe substitute. Nephrotic syndrome or dialysis also makes combined methods category 4. Match the alternative to the actual condition and its full clarification, not merely to the absence of estrogen. [CDC condition-specific classifications](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/classifications-mec-contraception.html)

**Clinical Case: Estrogen across routes.** A 29-year-old requesting a ring has established migraine with visual aura. A combined ring retains estrogen-associated vascular risk; offer an eligible nonestrogen method. Ordinary nonmigraine headaches require a different assessment.

### Start promptly while assessing pregnancy correctly

A negative urine test cannot exclude conception after very recent intercourse. With no pregnancy signs or symptoms, CDC's checklist permits reasonable exclusion when any one applies: within seven days after normal menses began; no intercourse since that menses began; correct, consistent reliable contraception; within seven days after abortion; within four weeks postpartum; or amenorrheic, fully/nearly fully breastfeeding and under six months postpartum. If no criterion applies, a negative urine test alone is insufficient. Many non-IUD methods can be quick-started with counseling and repeat testing in two to four weeks; use a bridge rather than inserting an IUD into an uncertain pregnancy. Recent unprotected exposure may need emergency contraception first. [CDC pregnancy-exclusion and quick-start guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/not-pregnant.html)

For a menstruating patient starting combined hormonal contraception, initiation within five days of bleeding onset needs no backup; later initiation needs seven days. Norethindrone/norgestrel pills use the same five-day start boundary but only two days of backup when started later. Drospirenone pills need seven days of backup unless begun on day one of bleeding. Postpartum, postabortion and switching situations have additional rules. Plan overlap before IUD removal after recent intercourse, and identify active versus placebo tablets before missed-dose advice. [CDC combined-method initiation](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html), [CDC formulation-specific pill initiation](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/progestin-only-pills.html)

### Match missed-pill advice to the formulation

For a combined oral contraceptive, one late or missed active pill with less than 48 hours elapsed generally means taking it promptly and continuing without extra protection. After at least two consecutive missed active pills, take the most recent missed pill, discard earlier missed pills and use backup until seven consecutive active pills have been taken. If the lapse is in the last active week, omit the hormone-free interval and start the next pack after finishing active pills. First-week lapses with unprotected intercourse in the previous five days warrant emergency-contraception assessment, ordinarily excluding ulipristal in this immediate-restart pathway. These are oral-pill instructions, not automatic patch or ring instructions. [CDC combined-pill missed-dose algorithm](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html)

| Progestin-only pill | Important lapse | Usual CDC response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Norethindrone or norgestrel | More than three hours late | Take promptly, continue daily and use backup until two consecutive days are taken correctly |
| Drospirenone | Two or more consecutive active pills missed, at least 48 hours since a pill was due | Take the last missed pill, continue and use backup for seven consecutive active-pill days |

One late or missed drospirenone active pill with less than 48 hours elapsed generally does not need backup. Evaluate recent intercourse and emergency contraception when indicated; the effect of progestin on ulipristal matters. Frequent lapses are a reason to revisit feasibility and preferences, not to shame the patient. [CDC progestin-only pill instructions](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/progestin-only-pills.html)

**Clinical Case: Formulation-specific timing.** A night-shift worker takes norethindrone five hours late. Take it promptly and use backup through two consecutive days of correct dosing; assess recent exposure for emergency contraception. A friend's drospirenone window does not apply. Reconsider dosing feasibility and preferences.

### Distinguish progestin products and injection risks

Progestin products differ in mechanism, bleeding, interactions, and safety. Norethindrone/norgestrel rely substantially on cervical mucus; drospirenone suppresses ovulation more consistently and has antimineralocorticoid effects. Its label contraindicates renal impairment, adrenal insufficiency, and specified hepatic disease. With long-term daily potassium-raising drugs, check potassium before starting and during the first cycle; review strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. [Drospirenone product precautions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=db32bc55-f295-4d87-9dbb-0a2f45573dcf)

DMPA suppresses ovulation and may produce early irregular bleeding followed by amenorrhea. Under U.S. SPR, reinjection is ordinarily every 13 weeks, with a two-week grace period; beyond 15 weeks, assess pregnancy, use seven days of backup after an appropriate reinjection and consider emergency contraception other than ulipristal. CDC supports trained subcutaneous self-administration as an additional option, but this remains off-label under U.S. labeling that specifies clinician administration. Keep provider-administered care available. [CDC injectable prescribing pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/injectables.html)

The products are not volume-for-volume substitutes: Depo-Provera CI uses 150 mg by deep IM injection, whereas depo-subQ provera 104 uses 104 mg subcutaneously. The latter product label specifies a 12-14-week interval; the 13-week appointment above is the named SPR pathway. Confirm the formulation, route and agreed reinjection plan. [DMPA product-specific administration](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/020246s074lbl021583s045lbl.pdf)

Before DMPA, discuss weight change, bone mineral density and delayed fertility return. FDA discourages use beyond two years unless alternatives are inadequate; reassess without creating a contraceptive gap. The December 2025 label adds a meningioma warning, especially with long exposure, and directs discontinuation if diagnosed. An injection cannot be removed. Delayed fertility return is not permanent infertility but matters when pregnancy is desired soon. [Current DMPA labeling](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/020246s074lbl021583s045lbl.pdf)

### Use long-acting methods without outdated duration rules

The etonogestrel implant suppresses ovulation and thickens mucus. Nexplanon is labeled for up to five years, with certified insertion/removal through its REMS. Confirm placement; localize a nonpalpable rod before removal. Discuss unpredictable bleeding and enzyme-inducing interactions. Apply product contraindications as well as MEC eligibility. [FDA Nexplanon label, revised May 2026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/021529s028lbl.pdf)

Copper devices interfere with sperm function without a hormone; levonorgestrel devices primarily create a local cervical and endometrial environment that impairs sperm passage and reduces bleeding. Products differ: Mirena is labeled for eight years of contraception but five years for treatment of heavy menstrual bleeding. Lower-dose levonorgestrel devices have shorter contraceptive durations. Record the exact product, insertion date, indication and replacement/removal plan rather than assigning every hormonal IUD the same lifespan. [Mirena indication-specific labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=dcbd6aa2-b3fa-479a-a676-56ea742962fc)

Paragard T380A is labeled for up to ten years, whereas Miudella is labeled for three and requires its own REMS insertion training. Miudella's label contraindicates emergency-contraception use; it is not an equivalent substitute for T380A's guideline protocol. Copper-associated bleeding/cramps matter with heavy menses or iron deficiency; an eligible levonorgestrel device may better fit that preference. CDC eligibility does not impose previous childbirth or a mutually monogamous relationship as a blanket access condition. [Paragard label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=3c420b53-c95e-46af-aebd-73741be58391), [Miudella label, revised May 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=4a75ebac-a765-460d-bb6a-9fbabb0b927d)

### Make IUD placement and follow-up person-centered

Assess pregnancy, uterine anatomy and current infection. Indicated gonorrhea/chlamydia screening can usually occur at placement without waiting for results in an asymptomatic patient, but known current infection or purulent cervicitis precludes insertion. A remote treated STI or prior ectopic pregnancy is not a blanket exclusion. Distortion of the uterine cavity, current pelvic infection, pregnancy and selected malignancy require a different plan. Copper and hormonal devices have different disease-specific restrictions. [CDC IUD eligibility](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/intrauterine-devices.html)

Product restrictions may exceed MEC: Mirena retains a prior-PID contraindication unless a subsequent intrauterine pregnancy occurred. Resolve product and local prescribing rules rather than generalizing either framework to every IUD. [Mirena contraindications](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=dcbd6aa2-b3fa-479a-a676-56ea742962fc)

Agree on individualized pain management before placement, including local anesthesia and trauma-informed support. Routine misoprostol is not recommended, though recent failed placement may justify it. Consent remains active throughout the procedure. [ACOG 2025 office-procedure pain consensus](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2025/05/pain-management-for-in-office-uterine-and-cervical-procedures)

Initial spotting may be expected, but heavy/prolonged bleeding, fever, severe pelvic pain or pregnancy symptoms need assessment. Missing strings may represent retraction, expulsion or perforation; establish location and pregnancy risk before assuming protection. If pregnancy occurs, promptly evaluate for ectopic pregnancy. For an intrauterine pregnancy continuing with visible/retrievable strings, clinician removal generally improves outcomes; do not blindly instrument when strings are inaccessible. Requested removal or a switch should be accessible even when bleeding is medically benign. [CDC IUD use and complication guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/intrauterine-contraception.html)

**Clinical Case: Same-day screening.** A 23-year-old requesting an IUD has no pain, discharge, or cervicitis, and pregnancy is reasonably excluded. Perform due gonorrhea/chlamydia screening without automatically delaying placement. Known current infection would preclude insertion. Agree on pain care and result communication.

### Provide emergency contraception without a false deadline

Review all exposure times, current contraception and medicines. CDC recommends treatment promptly within five days: ulipristal 30 mg orally once or levonorgestrel 1.5 mg orally once. Ulipristal performs better on days three to five; obesity may reduce levonorgestrel efficacy but does not justify withholding care. Oral emergency contraception mainly delays ovulation, not an established pregnancy. [CDC emergency-contraception pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/emergency-contraception.html)

The established T380A copper-IUD protocol permits placement within five days of the first unprotected act, or later if no more than five days after reliably estimated ovulation. Assess pregnancy and infection. Neither the newer copper device nor a hormonal IUD should be silently substituted without its own emergency-use evidence and service protocol.

After levonorgestrel, start regular hormonal contraception immediately with seven days of backup. After ulipristal, generally wait at least five days, then use backup for seven days after starting or until menses, whichever comes first; protect during the wait too. Selected in-person methods have same-day exceptions weighing interaction against lost access. Test if no withdrawal bleed occurs within three weeks. [CDC post-emergency contraception instructions](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/emergency-contraception.html)

Ulipristal's antiprogestin effect explains why immediate progestin can reduce its intended ovulation delay. Strong enzyme-inducing drugs can also reduce efficacy. Its product labeling uses a barrier-until-next-menses instruction and calls for pregnancy evaluation when the expected period is over a week late. Give a coherent written plan tied to the chosen framework/product, and investigate earlier pain or pregnancy concern rather than waiting for a scheduled test. [Current ulipristal labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=2bf93d23-cddd-4613-9066-5b5fa090404b)

**Clinical Case: Restart after ulipristal.** Four days after unprotected intercourse, a patient chooses ulipristal and a progestin-only pill. Explain the usual five-day delay, backup, and pregnancy testing. Discuss eligible copper-IUD options, later exposures, and return for pain or missed menses; the dose does not protect subsequent intercourse.

### Check interactions in both directions

Rifampin/rifabutin and several enzyme-inducing antiseizure drugs can reduce pill and implant effectiveness. IUDs and DMPA may be useful alternatives when otherwise eligible; verify the exact medication and method. Most ordinary broad-spectrum antibiotics do not require extra contraception simply because they are antibiotics. Combined hormonal contraception can lower lamotrigine concentrations, potentially affecting seizure control, with changes again during the hormone-free interval or discontinuation. Coordinate changes with neurology rather than abruptly stopping either therapy. [CDC progestin interaction guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/progestin-only-contraceptives.html), [CDC combined-method interactions](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html)

Tirzepatide delays gastric emptying and can reduce oral hormonal contraceptive effectiveness. Current Mounjaro labeling advises a nonoral method or an added barrier for four weeks after initiation and four weeks after each dose escalation; nonoral hormonal methods are not expected to be affected through this mechanism. This is a drug-specific instruction, not a rule for every weight-management medicine. Include nonprescription products, including St John's wort, in interaction review. [Tirzepatide label, revised August 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=d2d7da5d-ad07-4228-955f-cf7e355c8cc0)

Orforglipron (Foundayo) also requires a nonoral method or added barrier after starting and each escalation, but its July 2026 label specifies **30 days**, not tirzepatide's four weeks. Check the actual weight-management drug rather than copying one interval across the class. [Foundayo contraceptive interaction](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=8ac446c5-feba-474f-a103-23facb9b5c62)

**Clinical Case: Tirzepatide and oral contraception.** A pill user starts tirzepatide with escalation planned next month. Offer a nonoral method or four weeks of barrier backup after initiation and each escalation. If pregnancy is desired soon, review the broader medication and metabolic plan.

### Plan contraception before postpartum fertility returns

Ovulation can precede postpartum menses. Combined methods are category 4 before 21 days regardless of feeding. Between 21 and 42 days, use the full breastfeeding/VTE-risk table. Beyond 42 days, categories are 2 with breastfeeding and 1 without, absent other conditions. Estrogen-free options may begin earlier but need their own safety review; neither six months nor six weeks is a universal eligibility boundary. [CDC postpartum classification table](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/classifications-mec-contraception.html)

Lactational amenorrhea requires all three conditions: no return of menses, fully/nearly fully breastfeeding and less than six months postpartum. Long gaps between feeds weaken eligibility; the CDC description specifies intervals no longer than four hours by day or six at night. Plan the next method before any condition stops applying. Pumping, supplementation or the absence of bleeding should not automatically be treated as proof of contraceptive protection. [CDC lactational-amenorrhea criteria](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/lactational-amenorrhea-method.html)

Immediate postpartum IUD placement can be appropriate but has a higher expulsion risk than interval placement; postpartum sepsis precludes insertion. Many methods can also begin after a completed nonseptic pregnancy loss without waiting for another period, using procedure- and medication-specific guidance. Respect changing preferences after birth or loss and ensure that an earlier permanent-method request remains voluntary. [CDC postpartum and postabortion IUD guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/intrauterine-contraception.html)

### Discuss permanent contraception as permanent

Tubal procedures and vasectomy are intended to be irreversible; reversal is not a reliable fallback. Explain alternatives without imposing an arbitrary parity requirement or substituting another person's wishes. Review the actual consent, timing and payer rules in advance. Vasectomy is not immediately effective: under SPR, semen analysis is performed at eight to 16 weeks, and another method continues until success is confirmed. Neither procedure eliminates every possibility of future pregnancy or protects against STIs. A pregnancy after tubal surgery needs prompt evaluation, including for ectopic pregnancy. [CDC permanent-contraception guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/permanent-contraception.html)

### Prepare for pregnancy before withdrawing protection

Review pregnancy/family history, chronic disease, infection/immunity, nutrition, substances, mental health, safety, and all medicines/supplements. Coordinate replacement of harmful treatments: abrupt antiseizure or psychiatric withdrawal can be dangerous. Continue suitable contraception during transitions and optimization; today's negative test does not protect a future pregnancy from teratogens. [ACOG/ASRM prepregnancy counseling](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2019/01/prepregnancy-counseling)

| Preconception priority | Action before attempting pregnancy | Follow-through |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chronic illness and medicines | Optimize disease and plan safe treatment transitions | Coordinate primary, specialist and obstetric care |
| Neural-tube-defect prevention | Begin appropriate folic acid before conception | Confirm dose, timing and supplement contents |
| Immunity, infection and inherited risk | Review vaccines, indicated testing and carrier-screening options | Act on results before pregnancy when possible |
| Nutrition, mental health and social safety | Address substance use, food access, distress and coercion | Use feasible supports, not counseling alone |

Before conception in preexisting diabetes, ADA 2026 targets A1C below 6.5% when safely achievable without excessive hypoglycemia, using pregnancy-appropriate treatment. Review kidney/eye disease and medicines before removing contraception. Other conditions and pregnancy stages need their own goals; individualize timing of care. [ADA 2026 preconception diabetes guidance](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards)

### Start folic acid before the neural tube closes

Using the named CDC framework, average-risk people capable of pregnancy should receive 400 micrograms of folic acid daily. For a previous neural-tube-defect-affected pregnancy when planning another pregnancy, CDC recommends 4,000 micrograms daily beginning at least one month before trying and continuing through the first three months. That is 4 mg, ten times 400 micrograms, not 400 mg. Other higher-risk situations require individualized advice rather than an automatic dose copied from an older disease label. [CDC clinician folic-acid guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/folic-acid/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

**Clinical Case: Recurrence-prevention folate.** After a spina-bifida-affected pregnancy, a patient plans conception using a 400-microgram multivitamin. Arrange the higher-dose plan before attempts, confirm units with obstetric care, and avoid stacking prenatal vitamins that add unnecessary other ingredients.

### Address immunity, inherited risk and modifiable exposures

Check rubella and varicella immunity and indicated vaccination before conception. Live MMR and varicella vaccines are not given during pregnancy; CDC advises avoiding conception for 28 days after MMR and one month after each varicella dose. Inadvertent exposure requires counseling rather than an automatic conclusion that pregnancy must end. Other vaccines follow their current age/risk/pregnancy recommendations; do not carry forward a fixed older schedule or assume all vaccines are contraindicated. [CDC pregnancy vaccination guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines-pregnancy/hcp/vaccination-guidelines/index.html)

Offer carrier-screening counseling before pregnancy when possible, including cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy and hemoglobinopathies rather than relying solely on racial labels. Family history may warrant targeted evaluation. A positive carrier result informs partner/donor testing and reproductive counseling; it does not mean every child will be affected. Explain residual risk after negative screening. Parental carrier testing differs from fetal aneuploidy screening and diagnostic testing. [ACOG carrier-screening overview](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/carrier-screening)

Assess tobacco/nicotine, alcohol, drugs, nutrition, iron, weight, dental care, exposures, and mental health. Connect treatment to transport, cost, and insurance support; assess violence/coercion privately. Encourage safe activity without making BMI a barrier to care. Improved health reduces risk but cannot guarantee an uncomplicated pregnancy. [ACOG practical prepregnancy guidance](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/good-health-before-pregnancy-prepregnancy-care)

### Recognize when fertility assessment should start

After most reversible methods, fertility can return promptly; no routine hormone washout is required. DMPA can delay ovulation and conception after the final injection, which should inform method selection. For those trying through intercourse, ASRM describes a six-day fertile window ending on ovulation, with intercourse every one to two days during that window offering good chances when feasible. Timing should fit preferences rather than create a rigid burdensome schedule; calendar predictions are imperfect. [ASRM natural-fertility counseling](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/optimizing-natural-fertility-a-committee-opinion-2021/)

ASRM recommends evaluation after 12 months of attempts below age 35 and six months at 35 or older, with more immediate assessment above 40 or when a known problem exists. Irregular/absent cycles, suspected tubal disease, endometriosis, gonadotoxic exposure or possible male-factor disease can justify earlier testing. Evaluate ovulation, relevant reproductive anatomy/tubal patency and semen in parallel when applicable. Ovarian-reserve testing is not a general screening test that can promise future fertility. Donor-assisted and other family-building pathways need appropriate evaluation without requiring an irrelevant history of unsuccessful intercourse. [ASRM fertility evaluation](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/fertility-evaluation-of-infertile-women-a-committee-opinion-2021/)

Regular 21-35-day cycles usually establish an ovulatory pattern without extra testing unless findings such as hirsutism suggest otherwise. If confirmation is needed, obtain progesterone about one week before the expected next menses, not automatically on day 21; a value above 3 ng/mL supports recent ovulation, not luteal quality. Hysterosalpingography or sonohysterography can assess tubal patency, alongside the parallel semen evaluation. [ASRM initial-test selection](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/fertility-evaluation-of-infertile-women-a-committee-opinion-2021/)

**Clinical Case: Earlier fertility evaluation.** At 37, seven months of attempts warrants evaluation, including semen assessment when applicable, rather than another five months of waiting. Absent cycles can justify earlier testing even in younger patients. Continue folate, medication review, and support.

### Finish with a continuity plan

Document the method or pregnancy goal, contraindications/interactions, start and backup dates, and follow-up ownership. Ensure access to urgent advice, emergency contraception, refills, removal, and medication transitions. Revisit changing preferences without judgment.
