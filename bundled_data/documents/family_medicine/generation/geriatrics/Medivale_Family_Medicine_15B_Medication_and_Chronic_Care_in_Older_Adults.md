# Medivale Family Medicine

## 15B. Medication and Chronic Care in Older Adults

Geriatric prescribing balances current benefit against changing kidney function, nutrition, interactions, treatment burden and ability to manage the regimen. Age and medication count alone do not determine appropriateness.

### Reconstruct what the person actually takes

Medication reconciliation establishes actual use and resolves discrepancies with the record. Examine bottles, packs, inhalers, injections, drops, creams and nonprescription products, including vitamins, herbals, occasional pain/sleep remedies and borrowed medicines. Ask about an ordinary day's use; dispensing does not prove adherence.

For each product, establish the active ingredient, strength, formulation, dose, route, frequency, indication and last use. Identify prescribers/pharmacies and scheduled, as-needed, held or discontinued entries. Distinguish allergy from adverse effects and record reaction severity. Reconcile sources after hospitalization, specialty visits or packaging changes. [NICE medicines reconciliation](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng5/chapter/Recommendations)

Medication review assesses benefit, adverse effects, interactions, organ function, monitoring, cost and ability to use the regimen. Observe container opening, liquid measurement, inhaler or insulin-pen technique. Provide large print, simpler packaging or an agreed helper; check pill organizers against current prescriptions. [NIA taking medicines safely](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/medicines-and-medication-management/taking-medicines-safely-you-age)

**Clinical Case: two correct labels, one unintended regimen.** A 79-year-old takes both hospital generic and home brand-name bottles of the same antihypertensive and develops lightheadedness. Assess standing pressure and other causes, reconcile the duplicate with the pharmacy, and provide one clear regimen rather than adding a dizziness medicine.

| Review question | What it can uncover | Action to document |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is actually being used? | Duplicate ingredients, old bottles, intermittent use | One reconciled list and a way to remove obsolete supplies |
| What benefit is expected now? | No remaining indication or a useful treatment omitted | Continue, change, add or stop with a stated reason |
| What changed before the symptom? | New dose, interaction, illness or withdrawal | Evaluate the timeline rather than assume a new disease |
| Can the person use and obtain it? | Packaging, vision, dexterity, cost or transport barriers | Demonstration, accessible supply and proportionate support |
| Who will check the result? | Unowned laboratory tests or indefinite temporary therapy | Named clinician, review date and escalation instructions |

Polypharmacy means multiple-medicine use, not necessarily poor prescribing. Both unnecessary treatment and omission of beneficial therapy cause harm. Assess cumulative burdens from appointments, fingersticks, refills, food restrictions and administration times. [NICE multimorbidity care](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng56/chapter/recommendations)

### Deprescribe with a purpose, a method and follow-up

Deprescribing is supervised reduction or withdrawal when harms or burden outweigh benefit, or an indication has ended. Candidates include duplicates, symptom-causing drugs, prevention unlikely to help within the remaining lifespan, and unmanageable regimens. Reassess the original indication. [NIA polypharmacy and deprescribing](https://www.nia.nih.gov/news/dangers-polypharmacy-and-case-deprescribing-older-adults)

Agree on the target benefit, possible recurrence and response plan. Address immediate danger first; otherwise, limit simultaneous changes to clarify response. Record dose/taper, monitoring and restart criteria. Inform other prescribers and the pharmacy and remove obsolete supplies.

Distinguish disease recurrence, rebound and withdrawal. Abrupt cessation can be dangerous; individualize reduction to the drug, indication, exposure and patient. Ask about missed doses and previous attempts, and reassess symptoms during tapering. [FDA benzodiazepine withdrawal warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-requiring-boxed-warning-updated-improve-safe-use-benzodiazepine-drug-class)

For benzodiazepines, the ASAM-led 2025 guideline generally starts with reductions of about 5%-10% every 2-4 weeks and usually avoids reductions exceeding 25% every two weeks. Older adults may need smaller changes and longer pauses. Assess withdrawal at each step; slow or pause when symptoms interfere substantially. Do not automatically convert an older adult to a long-acting benzodiazepine, which may accumulate. Treat the underlying anxiety or insomnia concurrently, including appropriate behavioral therapy. Severe withdrawal, seizures or an unsafe outpatient situation requires a higher level of care. [ASAM tapering guideline and resources](https://www.asam.org/quality-care/clinical-guidelines/benzodiazepine-tapering)

**Clinical Case: stopping is not the same as tapering.** An 82-year-old living alone takes a nightly benzodiazepine, has unsteadiness and develops tremor after missed doses. Plan gradual monitored reduction with contact between changes and concurrent insomnia care; a safer lower dose may be the initial goal.

Review long-term acid suppression by indication, not by fear of every reported association. AGA advises considering a withdrawal trial when chronic PPI treatment lacks an indication, assessing gastrointestinal bleeding risk first. Severe complicated reflux, Barrett esophagus or high bleeding risk may justify continuation. Explain possible transient rebound upper gastrointestinal symptoms. Either tapering or abrupt withdrawal can be appropriate for a PPI; that flexibility must not be extrapolated to benzodiazepines. [AGA PPI deprescribing advice](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/de-prescribing-proton-pump-inhibitors-ppis/)

### Recognize medication injury before adding another diagnosis

Medication injury can cause falls, appetite loss, urinary difficulty, constipation, confusion or dependence. Relate symptoms to treatment changes, illness and missed doses; long-term drugs can become harmful as clearance and vulnerability change. Still assess stroke, infection, bleeding and other urgent causes.

A prescribing cascade treats an adverse effect as a new disease without reconsidering the initiating drug. Check symptom timing before adding treatment for edema, sleepiness or constipation. Treating an adverse effect may be justified when the original therapy remains essential, but requires explicit benefit-harm assessment. [NIA medication burden and adverse effects](https://www.nia.nih.gov/news/dangers-polypharmacy-and-case-deprescribing-older-adults)

The AGS Beers Criteria identify potentially inappropriate medicines and combinations for adults 65 or older, principally in U.S. care outside hospice and end-of-life settings. They prompt contextual review rather than automatic discontinuation. Selected concerns include strongly anticholinergic medicines, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs. Concurrent use of **three or more CNS-active drugs** from the listed groups increases fall/fracture risk; do not wait for three different classes. Those groups include antidepressants, antipsychotics, antiepileptics/gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, opioids and skeletal muscle relaxants. Kidney-specific cautions are separate: for example, AGS advises avoiding baclofen in older adults with eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 because of encephalopathy risk. Apply the indication-specific criterion. [AGS Beers 2023, selected criteria](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12478568/)

Antimuscarinic activity can reduce bladder contraction and secretions while also impairing attention and gastrointestinal motility. New dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention and confusion may therefore share one explanation. Count the combined burden from bladder drugs, allergy remedies, nighttime products and other prescriptions. The oxybutynin label specifically warns about confusion, hallucinations and somnolence, and caution with bladder outlet obstruction. Assess retention or delirium promptly rather than merely switching the time of administration. [Oxybutynin prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=6f409d7f-b064-1363-e053-2a91aa0a3a14&version=6)

| Medication-related presentation | Important possibility | A useful next decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dry mouth, constipation and new confusion | Accumulated antimuscarinic effects | Review all products and assess delirium/retention |
| Sleepiness after adding another CNS depressant | Additive sedation or respiratory suppression | Check breathing and responsiveness before routine review |
| Weakness or palpitations after an interacting prescription | Electrolyte disturbance or arrhythmia | Urgent assessment when indicated; review chemistry and the combination |
| Dizziness after duplicate pressure medicines | Excess lowering or orthostasis | Confirm actual dosing and assess seated/standing pressure |
| New bleeding on antithrombotic combinations | Additive impairment of hemostasis | Determine severity, source and the indication for each agent |

Replacing one sedative with another is not necessarily safer. For chronic insomnia, evaluate pain, nocturia, sleep apnea, mood and the sleep schedule; cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is an important initial treatment. Choose condition-specific alternatives; nonprescription products can also impair cognition. [AGS 2025 safer treatment alternatives](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jgs.19500)

Gabapentin and pregabalin can contribute to serious respiratory depression, especially with opioids or other CNS depressants, underlying respiratory impairment and older age. Review indication, kidney function and cumulative sedation. Marked somnolence, difficult arousal or slow breathing requires emergency assessment, not waiting for the next scheduled medication visit. [FDA gabapentinoid safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/neurontin-gralise-horizant-gabapentin-and-lyrica-lyrica-cr-pregabalin-drug-safety-communication)

### Match kidney assessment to the prescribing decision

Serum creatinine is a concentration, not a direct measure of filtration. Reduced muscle mass can make a seemingly ordinary creatinine misleading in an older adult. Consider the trend, body size, acute illness and the precision needed for the medicine. NIDDK advises considering combined creatinine-cystatin C estimation near an important dosing boundary, and additional assessment when creatinine alone is unreliable. Estimates during rapidly changing kidney function need particular caution because they may not reflect current clearance.

Read the product's dosing method. Labels may use estimated creatinine clearance, indexed eGFR or other criteria; those are not interchangeable simply because each has a kidney-related number. For markedly nonaverage body size, an indexed eGFR can be converted to an absolute estimate by multiplying by body surface area and dividing by 1.73. Do not apply that conversion indiscriminately to a threshold that explicitly uses indexed eGFR. [NIDDK kidney function and drug dosing](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/research-funding/research-programs/kidney-clinical-research-epidemiology/laboratory/ckd-drug-dosing-providers)

**Clinical Case: units change the interpretation.** A small older adult has eGFR 42 mL/min/1.73 m2 and body surface area 1.40 m2. De-indexing gives 42 x 1.40 / 1.73, approximately 34 mL/min: different scaling, not different disease stages. Use the label's required measure, confirm uncertain function and reassess after illness; calculation alone does not determine dose.

Metformin illustrates an indexed-eGFR label: it is contraindicated below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, initiation between 30 and 45 is not recommended, and a fall below 45 during existing treatment prompts a benefit-risk review. Declining function, hypoxia or dehydration should trigger timely reassessment rather than continuation based on an old result. Follow the specific formulation's label and contrast-related instructions. [Metformin renal precautions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=96b40564-d7d6-655b-e053-2a95a90a1f95)

Apixaban illustrates indication-specific dosing. For adult nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, the usual dose is 5 mg orally twice daily; use 2.5 mg twice daily when at least two apply: age at least 80, weight at most 60 kg, or serum creatinine at least 1.5 mg/dL. Age alone does not meet that reduction rule. Acute VTE treatment follows a different regimen; do not import the atrial-fibrillation criteria. Strong combined P-gp/CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers also change prescribing decisions. Check the complete current label, particularly with advanced kidney disease, interacting drugs or bleeding. [Apixaban prescribing information, revised August 2026](https://packageinserts.bms.com/pi/pi_eliquis.pdf)

For example, age 84, weight 58 kg and creatinine 1.2 mg/dL meet two AF reduction criteria despite a creatinine below 1.5; age 84 alone does not. With combined strong P-gp/CYP3A4 inhibitors, the label halves a 5-mg twice-daily dose and advises avoiding the combination when the patient already needs 2.5 mg twice daily. Strong combined inducers should be avoided. These rules do not make apixaban appropriate for a mechanical valve or moderate-or-greater mitral stenosis; see 02E.

### Search for interactions at every medication change

Interactions alter exposure, add physiological effects or oppose treatment. Review all prescriptions, supplements and alcohol. Interpret alerts using doses, indication, duration, organ function and alternatives; when a risky combination is necessary, assign monitoring and result ownership.

NSAIDs can impair kidney perfusion, particularly during dehydration or low blood pressure; diuretics and renin-angiotensin system drugs can add vulnerability in that setting. Ask specifically about ibuprofen, naproxen and combination cold products. Give an individualized illness plan with clear advice on which drugs may need temporary adjustment, when to contact the team and how to restart. Avoid blanket stopping instructions or indefinite temporary holds. [NIDDK kidney safety during illness](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/keeping-kidneys-safe)

Trimethoprim can increase potassium, with greater concern in renal impairment or when other drugs raise potassium. The linked sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim label advises avoiding concurrent ACE inhibitor use. Consider an appropriate alternative antibiotic rather than adding a prescription without checking the existing regimen. When an interacting treatment is unavoidable, involve the prescribing team and arrange relevant laboratory follow-up. Assess marked weakness, arrhythmia symptoms or significant biochemical abnormalities urgently. [Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim interaction warnings](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=75b9d194-b960-4943-bedc-96e3d65c4da9&type=display)

Anticoagulants plus antiplatelets or NSAIDs can add bleeding risk without necessarily adding benefit for the current indication. Establish why each was prescribed and whether a time-limited combined regimen has reached its intended endpoint. Avoid unplanned interruption of indicated antithrombotics. Active bleeding, head injury, syncope or suspected internal hemorrhage changes the urgency of assessment. The cardiovascular and hematology volumes address indication-specific pathways.

For atrial fibrillation, evaluate stroke prevention and modifiable bleeding risks rather than treating any fall history as an automatic contraindication. Serious bleeding from recurrent falls with an untreatable cause is a different situation from an isolated, remediable fall; the guideline distinguishes these circumstances. [AHA/ACC atrial-fibrillation guidance, selected anticoagulation scope](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001193)

### Individualize glucose and cardiovascular treatment

Balance target benefits against treatment harm. ADA 2026 suggests A1C below 7.0%-7.5% for many healthy older adults and below 8.0% for selected people with complex health; very poor health calls for avoiding hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia rather than relying on A1C. These are individualized frameworks, not age bands. Deintensify hypoglycemia-producing treatment when appropriate and simplify difficult regimens. In type 2 diabetes with established or high cardiorenal risk, retain appropriate risk-reducing agents irrespective of glycemia. Most older adults with diabetes have a blood-pressure goal below 130/80 mm Hg if safe; a less stringent goal, such as below 140/90, may suit poor health or high treatment risk. [ADA older-adult guidance 2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S277/163928/13-Older-Adults-Standards-of-Care-in-Diabetes-2026)

**Clinical Case: a favorable average hides harm.** An older adult with type 2 diabetes has A1C 6.4% but irregular meals and morning glucose near 55 mg/dL on insulin plus a sulfonylurea. Treat hypoglycemia, revise treatment and arrange glucose follow-up despite the low A1C.

Ask about the ability to recognize a low glucose, obtain food and administer rescue treatment. Confirm the monitoring method and who can help. Current ADA recommendations support CGM for older adults with type 1 diabetes or insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, with training and support matched to the user. A simpler plan can still require substantial teaching; fewer injections do not automatically make the remaining dose safe. [ADA 2026 technology and older-adult update](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-SREV)

Retain the distinction between diabetes types. Basal insulin is life-preserving in type 1 diabetes and must not be omitted simply because the person is older or eating less. Adjust the regimen with appropriate support to prevent both hypoglycemia and ketoacidosis. For type 2 diabetes, select treatment for the person's cardiovascular, kidney, nutritional and functional circumstances, not only the A1C. Match useful devices to available training and support, not age alone. [ADA/EASD 2026 type 1 diabetes consensus, older-person scope](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00125-026-06833-z)

For hypertension outside the diabetes-specific framework, AHA/ACC 2025 generally targets below 130/80 mm Hg, with individualized decisions when intolerance, limited life expectancy or other clinical features warrant less intensive treatment. Confirm measurement quality, home readings where appropriate, orthostatic symptoms and adherence before escalating therapy. After a change, assess symptoms and relevant kidney/electrolyte effects as well as seated pressure. Unsafe standing symptoms outweigh a favorable seated reading. [AHA/ACC 2025 hypertension guidance](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001356)

Separate primary from secondary cardiovascular prevention. AHA/ACC 2026 lipid guidance considers function, frailty, competing illness, treatment burden and expected benefit rather than age alone. Beyond age 75, a moderate-intensity statin may be reasonable for primary prevention after discussion when estimated life expectancy is at least 2.5 years. USPSTF's separate primary-prevention framework finds evidence insufficient to recommend for or against initiation at 76 or older; that is not a direction to stop treatment for established ASCVD. Avoid applying a risk calculator outside its validated ages. [AHA geriatric lipid guidance](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/2EE6BBFF89B244EF9A3FCF3C784EC0A3.pdf), [USPSTF statin scope](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/ClinicalSummaryFinal/statin-use-in-adults-preventive-medication)

USPSTF recommends against starting aspirin for primary cardiovascular prevention at age 60 or older. This does not equate to stopping all aspirin prescribed after myocardial infarction, coronary intervention or ischemic stroke. For an existing user, establish the indication and bleeding risk before discussing continuation. Review current benefit rather than duration on the list alone. [USPSTF aspirin prevention recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/aspirin-to-prevent-cardiovascular-disease-preventive-medication)

### Keep prevention useful and current

Adapt prevention to benefit horizon and burden. Vaccination can protect independence; screening can bring preparation, false positives, invasive follow-up and overdiagnosis. Discuss consequences before testing and individualize care for frailty.

For influenza, current CDC seasonal guidance recommends annual vaccination and preferentially recommends high-dose inactivated, recombinant or adjuvanted inactivated vaccine for adults 65 or older. If none is available, use another age-appropriate product rather than miss the opportunity. September or October is generally preferred; vaccination can continue later while influenza circulates and unexpired vaccine is available. Use the current seasonal product. [CDC current seasonal influenza considerations](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/hcp/vax-summary/seasonal-influenza-vaccines.html)

CDC recommends one RSV vaccine dose for all adults 75 or older and for adults 50-74 at increased risk of severe disease. It is not currently annual. Relevant risk includes conditions such as chronic heart or lung disease, immune compromise and nursing-home residence; isolated hypertension is not the same as the cardiovascular risk category. [CDC adult RSV guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/adults.html)

For adults 50 or older with no prior pneumococcal conjugate vaccine or unknown history, CDC options include PCV20 or PCV21 alone, or PCV15 followed by PPSV23, ordinarily a year later. Certain high-risk circumstances allow an eight-week minimum interval. Prior PCV/PPSV23 combinations require a history-specific pathway. PCV20 or PCV21 does not require subsequent PPSV23. [CDC pneumococcal recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/pneumococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/index.html)

Recombinant zoster vaccine is a two-dose series for adults 50 or older, ordinarily separated by 2-6 months. Prior shingles does not remove the indication, and a late second dose does not require restarting the series. Review immune status and contraindications. Also verify the primary tetanus series, previous Tdap and subsequent Td/Tdap needs, with wound-specific management when relevant. Review hepatitis and other risk-based indications rather than assuming that an older age removes exposure risk. [CDC zoster guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/shingles/hcp/vaccine-considerations/), [CDC adult schedule notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html)

Review the current COVID-19 recommendation using age, prior doses and immune status. In CDC's **2026-2027 non-immunocompromised pathway, adults 65 or older receive two seasonal doses**, ordinarily six months apart. The second-dose minimum is two months for the listed products other than mNexspike, whose recommended minimum is three months; CDC says an mNexspike dose given at least two months after the last dose should not be repeated. CDC explicitly identifies giving more than one dose in this age group as off-label relative to the FDA single-dose indication. Check the product-specific interval from the last prior dose and use the separate immunocompromised pathway when applicable; one seasonal dose should not automatically be recorded as completion. See 01E for the broader schedule. [CDC September 23, 2026 COVID-19 schedule and labeling distinctions](https://www.cdc.gov/covid/hcp/vaccine-considerations/routine-guidance.html)

### Distinguish screening decisions from symptom evaluation

These USPSTF frameworks apply to asymptomatic, generally average-risk people. Prior cancer, hereditary syndromes and other high-risk histories require separate surveillance. Investigate bleeding, masses, unexplained weight loss or anemia according to goals and likely benefit, not screening age cutoffs.

| Service and named USPSTF framework | Older-adult boundary | Clinical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colorectal screening | Selectively offer at ages 76-85; stop after 85 | Consider health, prior screening and preferences; abnormal symptoms are a separate problem |
| Breast screening | Biennial mammography through age 74; evidence insufficient at 75 or older | An I statement is uncertainty about screening benefit, not a ban on evaluating a mass |
| PSA-based prostate screening | Recommend against at age 70 or older | Do not confuse population screening with assessment of concerning symptoms |

A healthy, previously unscreened person may have more opportunity to benefit from colorectal screening than someone of the same age with repeated normal examinations and severe competing illness. Discuss willingness to pursue colonoscopy after a positive stool test and acceptable treatment. A test without feasible follow-up is not a complete screening pathway. [USPSTF colorectal screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening), [breast screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/breast-cancer-screening), [prostate screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/prostate-cancer-screening)

**Clinical Case: the same age does not mean the same decision.** Two 78-year-olds differ: an independent, never-screened person willing to pursue treatment may benefit from colorectal screening; one with advanced illness, repeated negative screening and no wish for invasive follow-up may decline. New symptoms still require assessment.

Revisit screening after a major change in health. Explain overdiagnosis plainly: a test can discover a real abnormality that would not have shortened the person's life, yet lead to burdensome treatment. Avoid presenting a single life-expectancy number as a precise prediction. Record the agreed plan so repeated reminders do not reopen the same settled decision at every visit. Other screening decisions, including osteoporosis, cervical cancer and lung cancer, retain their condition-specific eligibility in the prevention and relevant disease volumes.

### Make transitions a clinical process, not a paperwork event

At discharge, establish what happened, which problems remain active and what changed from baseline. Obtain the discharge summary and reconcile preadmission, inpatient and intended outpatient medicines. Identify what was stopped, started, held or changed, why it changed and when it should be reassessed. Temporary hospital treatments can otherwise become indefinite prescriptions, while a useful chronic treatment may disappear after an acute hold. If the documentation conflicts, contact the responsible team instead of asking the patient to guess.

AHRQ's discharge resources emphasize an understandable plan, communication with outpatient clinicians, pending-test follow-up and practical self-management support. Name who will review a culture, repeat chemistry or imaging recommendation and how the patient will hear the result.  Book appointments with attention to transport, language, hearing, finances and caregiver availability. [AHRQ discharge planning and transitions](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/discharge-planning-and-transitions-care)

Use teach-back: ask the patient or chosen helper to explain which medicine will be taken tonight, what changed and whom to call. Ask for a demonstration of an important task when feasible. Provide an accessible written list and ensure that the community pharmacy has the changes. Resolve supply gaps with an explicit interim plan.

AHRQ's RED model includes a clinical follow-up call 2-3 days after discharge to review health, medicines, appointments, services and emerging problems. This is a named transition model, not a reason to delay urgent assessment until a routine call. NICE's primary-care reconciliation standard is before new prescribing and within one week of receiving discharge information; local U.S. workflows and clinical urgency may require earlier action. [AHRQ RED follow-up tool](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/red/toolkit/redtool5.html)

**Clinical Case: a temporary hold needs an endpoint.** After acute kidney injury, a 76-year-old returns home with a medicine marked "hold" and repeat chemistry but no reviewer. Clarify indication, symptoms and kidney recovery, assign result review, and communicate a safe restart or alternative rather than automatic old-dose resumption or indefinite withholding.

### Follow recovery in the activities that matter

A resolved infection does not guarantee recovery of transfers, walking, cognition or meal preparation. Compare current function with the preillness baseline and look for continuing delirium, deconditioning, pain, malnutrition, sensory barriers and medication adverse effects. Arrange physical or occupational therapy, equipment, nutrition support and home assistance according to the limitation. A person's preference to return home is important, but the plan must specify how essential tasks will be performed safely.

Rehabilitation goals should be concrete and meaningful: reaching the mailbox with a walking aid, preparing breakfast or resuming an independent transfer. Reassess progress rather than labeling post-hospital dependence permanent at the first visit. Equally, do not ask an exhausted relative to provide lifting or supervision beyond their ability. Include the care partner's health, training and respite needs while preserving the patient's participation and privacy.

At review, assess intended benefit, new harm and whether the goal still fits; assign follow-up. Hematology follows in 15C and palliative care in 15D.
