# Medivale Family Medicine

## 15D. Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Palliative care addresses serious-illness burdens and aligns treatment with the person's priorities, alongside disease-directed treatment when appropriate. End-of-life care requires active symptom assessment, relief and dependable support, not simply limits on resuscitation.

### Offer palliation according to need, not a deadline

Palliative care seeks to prevent and relieve physical, psychological, social and spiritual suffering associated with serious illness. It can begin early and accompany treatments intended to prolong life or achieve cure. It is relevant to heart failure, advanced lung or kidney disease, progressive neurologic illness and cancer, and to children as well as adults. Prognostic uncertainty does not remove the need to treat distress. [WHO palliative care scope](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/palliative-care)

Primary care can assess symptoms, explain illness, support caregivers and begin goals discussions; specialist teams add expertise for difficult symptoms, complex choices or substantial distress. An interdisciplinary team may include nursing, social work, pharmacy, rehabilitation, nutrition and spiritual care. Identify the person's priorities; disease-directed treatment such as radiation for a painful tumor may itself be palliative. [NCI palliative care in cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet)

ASCO's 2024 update recommends early specialist palliative involvement alongside active treatment for advanced solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, especially with uncontrolled symptoms or quality-of-life concerns. Provide basic symptom care without awaiting referral; palliative involvement does not imply hospice election. [ASCO palliative care update](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.24.00542)

**Clinical Case: support during active treatment.** A 46-year-old with advanced HF, repeated admissions and disabling breathlessness fears missing a child's graduation and interprets palliation as stopping cardiology care. Explain concurrent symptom and family support, explore priorities, and avoid promising a precise survival date.

### Explain what a hospice election actually changes

Hospice is coordinated end-of-life care, delivered in several settings. Under the Medicare benefit, the patient must meet the relevant Part A requirements, receive certification of a prognosis of six months or less if the illness follows its usual course, and elect hospice care. Election changes payment for terminal-illness and related care, without eliminating all coverage or symptom treatment. [CMS hospice coverage](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/payment/fee-for-service-providers/hospice)

The Medicare benefit has two 90-day periods followed by unlimited 60-day periods when eligibility continues, with required recertification. The third and subsequent benefit periods require a qualifying face-to-face encounter for recertification. Six months is a prognostic eligibility standard, not an automatic discharge date. Routine home hospice does not guarantee a continuously present bedside caregiver. Continuous home care addresses a brief crisis; general inpatient care addresses symptoms that cannot be managed in another setting. Inpatient respite supports caregivers for up to five days at a time, not permanent custodial placement. [CMS levels of care and recertification](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/payment/fee-for-service-providers/hospice), [Medicare hospice benefit details](https://www.medicare.gov/Pubs/pdf/02154-medicare-hospice-benefits.pdf)

Discuss coverage and service availability with the hospice before arranging care for the terminal condition. Medicare generally does not cover ordinary room and board in a home or facility through this benefit; unrelated covered conditions retain their own coverage arrangements. Patients may revoke election and reelect hospice if eligible. [Medicare hospice coverage details](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/hospice-care) [Medicare hospice benefits booklet](https://www.medicare.gov/publications/02154-medicare-hospice-benefits.pdf)

| Term | What it describes | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Palliative care | Relief of serious-illness suffering and support for decisions | A six-month prognosis or a decision to stop active treatment |
| Medicare hospice election | A specific benefit and coordinated terminal-illness care plan | Automatic discharge at six months or unrestricted round-the-clock home staffing |
| DNR/DNAR order | A limit on attempted resuscitation at arrest | A prohibition on antibiotics, admission or symptom relief |
| Comfort-focused plan | A positive plan prioritizing relief and acceptable burdens | One identical list of treatments for every patient |

A DNR order is not a Medicare hospice eligibility requirement; resuscitation preferences still need explicit discussion and realistic planning. Pediatric coverage is different: Medicaid and CHIP-eligible people under 21 who elect hospice can receive concurrent treatment of the terminal condition. Other ages, payers and jurisdictions have distinct rules. [VA hospice clarification, DNR scope](https://www.veteranshealthlibrary.va.gov/HealthyLiving/BeInvolved/3,41109) [Medicaid pediatric concurrent care](https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/benefits/hospice-benefits)

### Discuss prognosis without pretending to know the date

Explain prognosis through illness trajectory, function, setbacks, intake and treatment response. Tools support judgment but cannot predict an exact date; use justified ranges and explain what could change them. Advanced disease does not prove imminent death, and a temporary rally may occur during overall decline.

Using the Ariadne framework, ask about desired information and participants, understanding, hopes, worries, tradeoffs and valued abilities or relationships. Recommend a feasible plan linked to those priorities rather than asking whether the patient wants "everything done." [Ariadne serious-illness conversation framework](https://www.ariadnelabs.org/resources/downloads/serious-illness-conversation-guide/)

Explore tradeoffs among longevity, comfort, alertness, home care and meaningful events, revisiting them after clinical change. Use a trained interpreter when needed and invite cultural or spiritual support according to the person's preference. Family involvement can be central without replacing the patient's own voice. [AMA advance care planning](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/advance-care-planning)

### Assess capacity and support participation

Capacity is decision- and time-specific: assess understanding, appreciation, comparison of consequences and communication of a choice. Address hearing, language, pain, medication effects and delirium. Dementia, an unwelcome refusal or inability to speak alone does not establish incapacity.

When capacity is impaired, involve the patient as much as possible and identify the legally authorized surrogate. Use the designated agent or jurisdictional process; family seniority or insistence does not establish authority. Substituted judgment asks what this patient would choose. If wishes are unknown, weigh benefits, burdens and the patient's experience under the best-interest standard. [AMA decisions when adults lack capacity](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/decisions-adult-patients-who-lack-capacity)

**Clinical Case: recover the patient's voice.** A 73-year-old with infection and intermittent inattention faces a relative's request for a permanent dialysis decision. Treat delirium, support communication and assess decision-specific capacity. Use an authorized surrogate for urgent decisions, returning authority to the patient when capacity recovers.

Document the decision, the information discussed, capacity findings, the patient's priorities and the authority of any surrogate. When preferences conflict or no appropriate surrogate can be found, seek ethics and institutional assistance. Do not substitute the clinician's own quality-of-life preferences. Pediatric decisions require parental or guardian authority, developmentally appropriate participation and the child's interests; adult surrogate rules are not a complete pediatric legal framework.

### Turn preferences into usable, consistent orders

A living will records future preferences; a health care proxy identifies a decision-maker. Check the directive's availability, current applicability and agreement with the person's wishes. A current decision by a patient who has capacity takes precedence over an older directive. In an urgent emergency without usable preferences or a surrogate, provide medically appropriate immediate care while seeking the missing information. [AMA advance directives](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/advance-directives)

Portable medical orders, often called POLST or MOLST, translate a serious-illness discussion into actionable orders across settings. They are distinct from a general advance directive and are not necessary for every healthy adult. The permitted signer, form, EMS recognition and transfer rules depend on jurisdiction. Keep current orders accessible to patients, caregivers, receiving facilities and emergency teams. [NIA advance directives and portable orders](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/advance-care-planning/advance-care-planning-advance-directives-health-care)

| Document or order | Main purpose | Point to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Living will | Future preferences in specified circumstances | Applicability and the patient's current wishes |
| Health care proxy designation | Identify an authorized decision-maker | Who is appointed and when authority operates |
| DNR/DNAR order | Direct what to do at cardiopulmonary arrest | Other treatment decisions remain separate |
| Portable medical orders | Carry serious-illness treatment orders between settings | Local validity, signatures, accessibility and consistency |

DNR does not automatically mean do not intubate before an arrest, do not hospitalize, do not treat infection or do not give analgesia. Discuss these separately, explaining which combinations are medically coherent. Revisit orders before procedures and after a major change in condition rather than assuming automatic suspension or automatic continuation without discussion. Resuscitation benefit depends on the arrest setting, cause and patient's condition. [AMA DNAR orders](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/orders-not-attempt-resuscitation-dnar)

An ICD needs a separate plan: a DNR order does not deactivate its shocks. Discuss whether shock treatment still serves the person's goals and arrange device-team programming when deactivation is chosen. Shock therapy can be stopped while useful bradycardia or resynchronization pacing continues. Pacing withdrawal in a dependent patient has potentially immediate consequences and requires a separate decision. [HRS end-of-life device consensus](https://www.hrsonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2010-HRS-Management-of-CIEDS-in-patients-nearing-end-of-life.pdf)

**Clinical Case: one limit is not a refusal of all care.** A 63-year-old with neuromuscular disease and a DNR order develops pneumonia. She wants admission, antibiotics and a noninvasive ventilation trial, but no invasive ventilation. Assess and treat pneumonia, documenting these choices separately from DNR.

### Use time-limited trials when benefit is uncertain

A time-limited trial tests whether treatment achieves a patient-valued outcome. Agree on treatment, review point, measurable benefit/harm and actions if unsuccessful. Set duration by intervention and illness; scheduled reassessment prevents indefinite treatment by default.

Withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment are ethically equivalent when based on the patient's goals, appropriate consent and clinical circumstances, although they can feel different to a family. When stopping an ineffective intervention, anticipate symptoms, continue comfort care and communicate the plan. A patient with relevant capacity may decline treatment even if death is expected; a surrogate uses the appropriate decision standard rather than personal preference. [AMA withholding and withdrawing treatment](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/withholding-or-withdrawing-life-sustaining-treatment)

When disagreement persists, clarify whether the conflict concerns facts, values, prognosis, authority, trust or access to support. A request for more treatment may mean a fear of abandonment. Offer a clear recommendation, second opinion or ethics consultation as appropriate, and follow the institution's fair process and applicable law for disputed treatment. Maintain care without promising medically inappropriate interventions. [AMA medically ineffective interventions](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/medically-ineffective-interventions)

### Treat pain by mechanism, burden and response

Ask about location, quality, timing, triggers and effects on sleep, movement and interaction. Somatic pain may be localized to bone or soft tissue; visceral pain can be poorly localized or referred; neuropathic pain often has burning, electric or sensory features. More than one mechanism can coexist. Consider fracture, cord compression, retention, impaction, infection and treatment toxicity before attributing every new symptom to expected dying. Use goals to select useful investigations while treating distress.

Combine practical measures with mechanism-directed treatment. Positioning, pressure relief, help with movement and selected local or disease-directed interventions may reduce the need for systemic medicine. Nonopioid and neuropathic-pain agents may be useful when their anticipated benefit outweighs renal, hepatic, bleeding, anticholinergic or sedating harms. Time analgesia to painful care such as turning. [NCI cancer pain, selected mechanism and adjuvant scope](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/side-effects/pain/pain-hp-pdq)

ASCO recommends offering opioids for moderate-to-severe pain from cancer or active cancer treatment unless contraindicated. Start an appropriate immediate-release preparation at a low individualized as-needed dose, assess early and titrate to benefit and tolerability. Persistent requirements can inform a scheduled regimen with an explicit rescue plan. Reassess cause and baseline treatment with frequent rescue use. Substance use disorder calls for coordinated expertise, not untreated cancer pain. [ASCO adult cancer opioid guidance](https://ascopubs.org/doi/full/10.1200/JCO.22.02198)

Specify drug, concentration, route, dose, interval and use limits/escalation instructions. Oral and injectable doses are not interchangeable. Rotation between opioids requires a checked conversion, allowance for incomplete cross-tolerance and close reassessment; methadone requires particular expertise. A family should demonstrate the actual measuring device. CDC's outpatient pain guideline explicitly excludes cancer-related pain treatment, palliative care and end-of-life care; its thresholds must not become an automatic ceiling in these settings. [CDC opioid guideline, scope limits](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/71/rr/rr7103a1.htm)

For a patient already receiving scheduled opioids for cancer pain, ASCO describes an immediate-release breakthrough dose in the range of **5%-20% of the regular daily morphine-equivalent dose**, individualized to response. This is not an opioid-naive starting rule or a direct cross-drug conversion. Specify the selected drug, route and rescue interval, and review frequent rescue use rather than allowing unlimited unsupervised dosing. [ASCO breakthrough-pain recommendation](https://ascopubs.org/doi/full/10.1200/JCO.22.02198)

Liquid concentrations require a new calculation at every product change. For an already prescribed **5-mg oral dose**, a 2-mg/mL solution requires **2.5 mL**. Giving that same volume from a 20-mg/mL bottle delivers **50 mg**, a tenfold error. The 20-mg/mL product is labeled only for opioid-tolerant adults stabilized on therapy. The illustrative 5 mg is not a recommended starting dose. Use a metric oral syringe and write both mg and mL. [Current US morphine-solution label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=bb6b3439-9f0c-45f5-bf6f-c7a971af0a8f)

### Anticipate toxicity and adapt the route safely

Monitor alertness, breathing, function, nausea, bowel movements and urinary symptoms. Kidney decline can increase accumulation of morphine metabolites and produce confusion or myoclonus. Assess opioid, dose, interval and alternatives with palliative/pharmacy support before attributing new movements or hallucinations to dying. Renal and hepatic impairment also affect many adjuvants. Assess each alternative's pharmacology. [Scottish palliative morphine guidance, renal and monitoring scope](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/scottish-palliative-care-guidelines/medicines-information/morphine/)

A fentanyl patch is not a quick rescue treatment or a first opioid for an opioid-naive patient. Its U.S. labeling requires established opioid tolerance and warns that external heat can cause fatal overdose. Fentanyl routes are not dose-equivalent. If swallowing fails, select an appropriate route with trained administration rather than automatically using a patch. [Fentanyl transdermal labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=2a2238e9-4b5d-c56d-8663-dd354ff9ae0c&type=display)

**Clinical Case: apparent progression may be medication toxicity.** A 67-year-old with metastatic cancer on morphine develops hallucinations, jerking and somnolence after kidney decline. Before increasing treatment for presumed pain, urgently assess breathing, exposure, opioid toxicity and other causes; seek help with dose adjustment or rotation.

Analgesia aims for relief with acceptable alertness, not unconsciousness or death. Respiratory depression remains a risk requiring an individualized safety plan. Unexpected marked sedation, difficult arousal or slow breathing requires prompt assessment even in hospice. Maintain secure storage, avoid unintended duplicate dosing and plan disposal according to product instructions and local services.

Discuss access to rescue naloxone and teach the response to an unexpected overdose when initiating or renewing an opioid. Coordinate this with the patient's goals and hospice's after-hours plan; neither a DNR order nor an expected decline automatically answers how to respond to an accidental medication error. [Morphine label, overdose-rescue access](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=bb6b3439-9f0c-45f5-bf6f-c7a971af0a8f)

### Match breathlessness treatment to the population

Breathlessness and oxygen saturation can diverge. Assess distress, respiratory work and reversible contributors such as fluid overload, bronchospasm, pleural effusion, anemia or infection. Positioning, airflow from a fan, pacing and calm coaching can help. Use oxygen for appropriate hypoxemia rather than reflexively increasing it whenever a person reports air hunger. In advanced cancer, ASCO recommends systemic opioids when nonpharmacologic approaches provide inadequate relief, with individualized monitoring. [ASCO advanced cancer dyspnea guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33617290/)

Do not generalize this into routine opioid prescribing for all chronic respiratory disease. ERS 2024 conditionally advises against opioids for breathlessness in its serious respiratory-illness population, with very low-certainty evidence. A person in the final days of life needs a separate individualized dying-care plan; the indication, goals and setting matter. Anxiety may accompany breathlessness, but sedation should not substitute for assessing the cause. [ERS serious respiratory illness symptom guideline](https://publications.ersnet.org/lookup/pmid/38719772)

**Clinical Case: the number and the symptom answer different questions.** A 58-year-old with advanced lung cancer has air hunger despite room-air saturation of 97%. Treat feasible causes; if positioning and airflow help incompletely, discuss goal-concordant advanced-cancer treatment, including a monitored opioid trial when appropriate. More oxygen may not relieve normoxemic breathlessness.

### Investigate nausea and constipation before adding medicines

Nausea may reflect drugs, delayed gastric emptying, constipation, obstruction, metabolic disturbance, vestibular disease or intracranial pathology. Ask about meal timing, early satiety, vomiting, colic, distension and passage of stool or flatus. Choose the treatment and route according to the likely mechanism and ability to retain oral medicine. Treat feasible causes and avoid overlapping antiemetics. Dopamine blockade can worsen parkinsonism; QT prolongation, sedation and anticholinergic burden also matter. [Scottish palliative nausea and vomiting guidance](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/scottish-palliative-care-guidelines/symptom-management/nausea-and-vomiting/)

| Symptom pattern | Decision to clarify | Safety issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early satiety and post-meal vomiting | Gastric stasis versus obstruction or another cause | A prokinetic is inappropriate when stimulating motility is dangerous |
| Colic, distension and loss of flatus | Possible bowel obstruction | Do not escalate oral laxatives without assessment |
| Hard stools after opioid initiation | Prevent and treat opioid-related bowel dysfunction | Consider impaction and the person's fluid intake |
| New somnolence or jerking after kidney decline | Drug accumulation versus other acute illness | Reassess exposure before increasing opioid treatment |

Metoclopramide's prokinetic action can be useful in selected gastric-stasis situations, but the label contraindicates use when motility stimulation is dangerous, including mechanical obstruction, perforation or gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Malignant obstruction requires specialist, goal-concordant selection of decompression, medical palliation or procedures; antiemetics do not correct obstruction. [Metoclopramide labeling, obstruction limitation](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=3fb9a45e-48ba-45c5-86a7-03a8744888f6)

Opioids reduce intestinal propulsion and secretion. Anticipate constipation when starting or escalating regular treatment and choose an appropriate bowel regimen, often involving a stimulant and/or osmotic laxative. Assess stool consistency, discomfort, leakage and evacuation. Overflow can mimic diarrhea. Bulk-forming treatment can be unhelpful when intake is poor, and impaction or obstruction changes the plan. AGA identifies laxatives as first-line treatment for opioid-induced constipation. [AGA opioid-induced constipation guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/medical-management-of-opioid-induced-constipation/)

For a suitable adult without obstruction or impaction, one practical stimulant starting regimen is **sennosides 17.2 mg orally once daily** (two 8.6-mg tablets), often at bedtime. Reassess stool comfort and frequency, titrate under the care plan and add an appropriate osmotic agent if needed. Check sennoside content in combination tablets. Increasing colic, vomiting or absent flatus requires assessment rather than repeated laxative escalation. [Sennosides product directions](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5f7f0df4-dabe-4c8f-a2b5-766f20af2f46)

For refractory opioid-induced constipation, a peripherally acting opioid antagonist may be considered after reassessment. Product and route indications differ: methylnaltrexone injection is labeled for adults with advanced illness or active cancer requiring opioid escalation for palliation. It is contraindicated with known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction; perforation risk, organ function and other label requirements must be considered. Assess other causes of abdominal pain. [Methylnaltrexone label, advanced-illness scope](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=c488fb7c-0a5b-487c-b452-996809d1cb99)

### Separate comfort feeding from a feeding-tube decision

Reduced intake can arise from oral pain, thrush, dry mouth, dentures, dysphagia, nausea, constipation, fatigue, depression or lack of assistance. Assess the cause and what the person wants. Offer desired food with suitable texture, positioning and assistance; stop with distress and explain accepted risks. Avoid forced feeding or inappropriate calorie targets.

Artificial nutrition is a medical intervention with possible benefits and burdens, not a guaranteed way to prevent aspiration or restore strength. The disease, reversibility, expected duration and goals determine the decision. ESPEN 2024 supports temporary enteral nutrition in selected mild or moderate dementia when poor intake is predominantly due to a potentially reversible condition, but advises against initiating it in severe dementia. In terminal dementia, it advises against initiating enteral or parenteral nutrition and parenteral fluids. Retain this dementia-specific distinction when considering a feeding trial. [ESPEN dementia nutrition and hydration update](https://www.espen.org/files/ESPEN-Guidelines/ESPEN-guideline-on-nutrition-and-hydration-in-dementia-update-2024.pdf)

**Clinical Case: feeding is more than the route.** An 83-year-old with severe dementia needs feeding assistance and coughs with some textures. A tube cannot guarantee aspiration prevention or pressure-injury healing. Assess reversible discomfort, swallowing support and known preferences; plan assisted feeding and explain artificial nutrition's evidence and burdens.

### Individualize hydration and provide mouth care

Dry mouth is not the same as intravascular depletion. Medicines, mouth breathing and reduced saliva can cause dryness that an infusion may not correct. Offer frequent mouth and lip care and desired, safe sips when possible. The person's ability to swallow and the risk of aspiration determine how assistance is given. Explain declining intake as part of dying without assigning caregiver blame.

For adults in the last days of life, NICE describes uncertain effects of assisted hydration on survival and supports an individualized therapeutic trial when dehydration-related distress is suspected and oral intake is inadequate. Consider pulmonary edema and other burdens, monitor for benefit and harm at least every 12 hours during the trial, and reduce or stop treatment if harmful or unwanted. The terminal-dementia pathway above remains distinct. [NICE last-days hydration recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng31/chapter/Recommendations)

| Proposed intervention | Define before starting | Reason to reconsider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assisted oral intake | Desired comfort, swallowing support and accepted risks | Coughing, fatigue, distress or refusal |
| Hydration trial in an appropriate patient | Symptom target, route and reassessment plan | Fluid overload, no meaningful benefit or changed wishes |
| Artificial nutrition for a reversible problem | Expected recovery and time-limited goal | Burdens exceed benefit or the goal is not achievable |
| Any continued intervention near death | Current indication and patient-valued benefit | A former routine no longer serves the agreed goal |

### Recognize dying while remaining alert to treatable distress

Increasing sleep, reduced intake, diminished interaction, irregular breathing, cool extremities and mottled skin may occur near death, but no single sign fixes the timetable. Explain uncertainty and select tests that could usefully change goal-concordant care. Reduce burdensome observations while continuing symptom assessment. Review medicines individually to prevent withdrawal or uncontrolled symptoms.

Delirium can be quiet or agitated. Consider pain, retention, impaction, dehydration, hypoxia, metabolic disease and medication effects in the context of feasible treatment. Provide a safe environment and explain changes to caregivers. Reserve individualized drug treatment for severe distress, weighing harms. Opioid-induced neurotoxicity can resemble terminal delirium. Refractory distress deserves specialist help. [NCI last-days assessment, delirium and neurotoxicity](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/caregivers/planning/last-days-hp-pdq)

Noisy upper-airway secretions can distress observers without necessarily causing discomfort to the dying person. Explain the sound and assess positioning and other causes before treatment. A trial of an appropriate antisecretory drug may be considered for distress, with review for dry mouth, delirium and other harms. Noise alone does not establish suffocation, and drugs may not eliminate it. Continue gentle oral care and goal-directed treatment.

### Reserve palliative sedation for refractory suffering

Palliative sedation intentionally reduces awareness for refractory suffering, distinct from ordinary analgesic titration; first address reversible causes. The EAPC 2024 framework emphasizes refractoriness, proportionality, patient autonomy and a separate decision about hydration. Seek specialist interdisciplinary assessment, informed consent or the appropriate surrogate process, a documented symptom target and monitoring proportionate to the plan. Use no more loss of awareness than the clinical situation requires. Its intent is relief, distinct from jurisdiction-specific assisted dying. [EAPC revised palliative sedation framework](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/02692163231220225)

Distress can also reflect loneliness, spiritual conflict, fear or a feeling of being a burden. Ask what the person means rather than assuming every request for treatment withdrawal has the same cause. Address depression, delirium, coercion and safety concerns when present, and involve appropriate mental health, spiritual or social support. Assess understanding and capacity without equating disagreement with psychiatric incapacity.

### Make a home plan the caregiver can actually carry out

Ask who can be present, administer medicine, help with toileting and obtain supplies. Assess health, sleep, work, finances, health literacy and willingness to perform these tasks. Assess whether support makes home care feasible; revisit needs at transitions and offer private caregiver discussion. [NCI caregiver assessment](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/coping/family-friends/family-caregivers-hp-pdq)

Before a crisis, provide the correct contact number, after-hours plan, symptom instructions and a route to urgent help. Teach medication measurement and record keeping, confirm access to prescribed medicines and equipment, and identify a backup if the main caregiver is unavailable. Plan what to do with severe breathlessness, uncontrolled pain, bleeding or an unexpected change, according to the agreed goals. Specify emergency actions separately from DNR.

Discuss what dying may look like and who to call when an expected death occurs; the responsible clinician, hospice and local requirements determine notification and certification arrangements. Explain practical steps with sensitivity to religious and cultural wishes. Provide respite and social support where available, including help with ordinary tasks. Reassure caregivers that brief absences are not failures. [NIA comfort and practical support](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/providing-comfort-end-life)

### Continue care through bereavement

Grief can begin before death and varies across people and cultures. Offer condolences, opportunities to discuss the illness and access to practical, community or spiritual support. Avoid fixed stages or recovery schedules. Ask about sleep, eating, functioning, substance use and safety; major depression and other illnesses can occur during bereavement and deserve treatment.

DSM-5-TR prolonged grief disorder requires more than grief lasting a certain number of months. APA describes the loss as at least 12 months earlier in adults or six months earlier in children and adolescents, with persistent characteristic symptoms, impairment and a response exceeding relevant cultural expectations. Evaluate the complete criteria and alternatives. These duration thresholds are not reasons to withhold support or treatment before they are reached. [APA prolonged grief disorder](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder)

**Clinical Case: support does not require waiting for a label.** A 52-year-old struggles with sleep, meals and bills several weeks after a partner's death. Assess function, depression, substance use and safety, offer support and arrange follow-up without diagnosing prolonged grief from sadness alone. Persistent disabling yearning later warrants grief-specific assessment.

### Maintain continuity of care

Document accessible goals, decision-maker, limits, symptom instructions and contacts. At transfers, reconcile orders, confirm supplies/support and communicate changes. Reassess as illness, capacity and preferences evolve.

Treatment limits never end the duty to relieve suffering, support communication and remain available to patients and families.
