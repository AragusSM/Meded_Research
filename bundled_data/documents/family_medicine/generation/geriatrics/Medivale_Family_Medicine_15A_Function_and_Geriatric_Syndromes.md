# Medivale Family Medicine

## 15A. Function and Geriatric Syndromes

Geriatric assessment centers on priorities, usual function, reserve, support and recent change, not age alone. Falls, incontinence and confusion often reflect interacting causes.

### Begin with the person's priorities and usual function

Ask which activities and abilities matter most, then establish preillness function, current performance and the course of decline. Obtain collateral history with permission while addressing the patient directly; a relative's help does not transfer decision-making authority.

Basic activities of daily living include eating, dressing, bathing, toileting and transfers. Instrumental activities include medicines, finances, shopping, food preparation, communication and transportation. Record independence, equipment, reminders or hands-on assistance. Distinguish inability from lack of opportunity or an unfamiliar household role; physical adaptations do not imply decisional incapacity.

The WHO ICOPE approach links mobility, cognition, psychological well-being, vitality, hearing, vision and social needs to a personalized plan. Translate abilities, effective supports and unmet needs into actions, responsibilities and follow-up. [WHO ICOPE, second edition](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240103726)

Frailty means reduced physiological reserve and greater vulnerability to stressors; it is related to, but not interchangeable with, multimorbidity, disability or chronological age. Infection or immobility may cause disproportionate decline; use validated assessment when it improves planning. The Clinical Frailty Scale summarizes a multidimensional clinical assessment rather than serving as a self-administered questionnaire. Use trained judgment, the person's usual state and the tool's population limitations; do not score only the worst moment of an acute illness. [Dalhousie Clinical Frailty Scale guidance](https://www.dal.ca/sites/gmr/our-tools/clinical-frailty-scale.html)

**Clinical Case: equipment is not a frailty diagnosis.** A 73-year-old wheelchair user with longstanding unilateral weakness manages transport, medicines and assistance. Gastroenteritis increases transfer needs. Establish baseline, treat the illness and plan functional recovery before assigning severe frailty.

| Domain to explore | A useful clinical distinction | Consequence for the care plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-care and transfers | New assistance versus longstanding adaptation | Rehabilitation, equipment or increased personal support |
| Medicines and appointments | Access/packaging problem versus impaired planning | Simplify the task and check actual medication use |
| Meals and household tasks | Appetite, strength, cognition and food availability | Address the cause, not only the weight measurement |
| Money, transport and communication | Physical/sensory barrier versus unsafe judgment | Preserve independence with proportionate safeguards |
| Social support and personal goals | Living alone versus unwanted isolation or unmet needs | Agree on support that the person values |

### Separate capacity, risk and disagreement

A diagnosis of dementia, a low test score or an unconventional choice does not by itself establish incapacity. Assess the particular decision: can the patient understand relevant information, appreciate its consequences, compare the options and communicate a choice? Improve hearing, language access, pain control and the setting before judging performance. Capacity may fluctuate with delirium or differ between simple and complex decisions.

When decision-making capacity is impaired, involve the person as fully as possible and identify an appropriate authorized surrogate. Use known wishes and values, or the relevant best-interest standard when wishes are unknown. Clarify authority and reporting duties under local law; the historian may not be the authorized surrogate. Seek ethics support for unresolved authority or conflict. [AMA decision-making and surrogate guidance](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/decisions-adult-patients-who-lack-capacity)

### Treat a fall as an event to explain

Ask about falls over the prior year, near falls, unsteadiness and fear-driven restriction of activity. Reconstruct the most recent episode: position, activity, prodrome, loss of consciousness, impact, injuries, ability to rise and how long help took. Review witnesses and environmental circumstances. Unexplained collapse requires assessment for syncope, seizure or acute illness despite a "mechanical fall" label.

Look first for head injury, fracture, focal neurological findings, serious illness, bleeding and inability to bear weight. Anticoagulant use, a significant head strike or persistent symptoms lowers the threshold for urgent assessment according to the applicable injury pathway. Evaluate prolonged time on the floor for dehydration, hypothermia, pressure damage or muscle injury as indicated. Unsafe transfers or home conditions require a safe disposition despite negative radiographs.

For the broader assessment, review gait, strength, balance, vision, feet, footwear, cognition, continence, pain, medications and home hazards. Ask how mobility aids are actually used. Nocturia, a dark hallway and a sedating medicine may act together. Use CDC STEADI to identify causes and interventions. [CDC STEADI](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/hcp/clinical-resources/)

In the NICE 2025 framework, a fall in the last year plus frailty, an injury needing treatment, associated loss of consciousness, inability to rise independently or at least two falls warrants comprehensive assessment and management. A single fall without those features still calls for gait/balance assessment. Urgent injury evaluation takes priority over this assessment framework. [NICE NG249 comprehensive falls assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng249/chapter/Recommendations/)

### Measure mobility and pressure in a reproducible way

Observe rising, walking, turning and sitting with usual footwear and aids; record pain and assistance. Performance tests identify difficulty and rehabilitation baselines, not a definitive cause or individual fall probability.

| Assessment | What to observe or measure | Interpretation boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timed Up and Go | Rise, walk a short marked route, turn and return to the chair | Time and movement quality flag a need for further assessment |
| Chair-rise performance | Leg strength, use of arms and ability to control sitting | Pain, technique and the chosen validated protocol matter |
| Standing balance | Ability to maintain progressively challenging positions safely | Guard the patient; do not continue an unsafe task |
| Orthostatic measurements | Pressure, pulse and symptoms after lying and standing | A single normal result does not exclude intermittent symptoms |

CDC's Timed Up and Go uses a three-meter route at the usual walking pace, with the regular walking aid if needed. At least 12 seconds flags risk, without diagnosing its cause or excluding falls at faster times. Observe turning, sway and equipment use. [CDC standardized TUG assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/media/pdfs/STEADI-Assessment-TUG-508.pdf)

For the CDC orthostatic assessment, rest supine for five minutes, measure pressure/pulse, then repeat after one and three minutes standing with safety assistance. A systolic fall of at least 20 mm Hg or diastolic fall of at least 10 mm Hg is abnormal; lightheadedness also warrants attention even without that numerical drop. Record timing and symptoms; distinguish volume depletion, medication effects and autonomic disease. [CDC orthostatic assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/media/pdfs/STEADI-Assessment-MeasuringBP-508.pdf)

**Clinical Case: more than one contributor.** A 78-year-old falls en route to the bathroom after starting an alpha-blocker. Pressure falls from 148/78 supine to 118/66 mm Hg standing with dizziness; a loose mat obstructs the route. Address the abnormal 30/12-mm Hg drop, drug/volume status, toileting access and mat, with mobility follow-up.

### Reduce falls while preserving activity

USPSTF 2024 recommends exercise interventions for community-dwelling adults 65 or older at increased fall risk. Effective programs emphasize gait, balance and functional/strength work, tailored to ability and supported over time. Select multifactorial interventions individually from the patient's risks and circumstances. Walking alone, a generic handout or advice to stay seated should not replace a structured intervention when one is indicated. [USPSTF 2024 falls prevention](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/falls-prevention-community-dwelling-older-adults-interventions/)

Arrange physical therapy or an appropriate community program, assess the fit/use of a cane or walker and consider occupational-therapy home assessment. Improve lighting, handholds, bathroom access and footwear. Review sedatives, anticholinergic drugs, glucose-lowering treatment and medicines contributing to hypotension, making changes with a monitored plan rather than abruptly stopping essential therapy. Address cataracts or other visual problems, foot pain and osteoporosis risk. Do not prescribe vitamin D as though it substitutes for a falls program; nutritional deficiency and bone-health treatment are separate indications.

Fear-driven inactivity worsens weakness and confidence. Plan a safe, graduated return to valued activities; reassess falls, mobility, participation, drug effects and completion of home modifications.

### Recognize delirium, including the quiet presentation

Delirium is an acute or subacute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with additional cognitive change. It may be agitated, hypoactive or mixed. Obtain an informant's timeline: sleepiness, reduced intake or inability to follow conversation may signal hypoactive delirium, not simply dementia progression.

Assess physiology and causes: oxygenation, glucose, infection, dehydration, electrolyte or organ dysfunction, pain, constipation, retention, medication toxicity and withdrawal. Order tests from the findings and exposures; focal deficits, head injury or a concerning neurological course may require urgent imaging. Review recent prescriptions, hospital changes and over-the-counter sleep/allergy products. Delirium in an outpatient setting often requires urgent medical evaluation, not simply another memory-clinic appointment.

NICE's hospital/long-term-care framework recommends assessment with the 4AT by a competent practitioner when indicators are present, with CAM-ICU or ICDSC used in critical care/recovery-room settings. Investigate abnormal results without delaying stabilization. If delirium and dementia are difficult to distinguish, address delirium first. [NICE delirium assessment and management](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg103/chapter/Recommendations)

Treat causes and support orientation, familiar contact, sensory aids, hydration, food, mobilization, pain control and sleep. Avoid unnecessary catheters, restraints and transfers; medication is not routine treatment. If severe distress or immediate danger persists despite de-escalation, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time with frequent reassessment. Check cardiac rhythm/QT risks, electrolytes, interactions and neurological disease before selection. Haloperidol is contraindicated in Parkinson disease and dementia with Lewy bodies; its US label also warns of increased mortality in older adults with dementia-related psychosis. Benzodiazepines can worsen ordinary delirium and are not routine treatment, although withdrawal is a distinct indication. [MHRA delirium-treatment safeguards](https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/haloperidol-haldol-reminder-of-risks-when-used-in-elderly-patients-for-the-acute-treatment-of-delirium), [US haloperidol label](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=b1ad2abd-a9d6-44e3-957b-36a73260f4f5)

Bacteriuria alone does not explain confusion. IDSA recommends looking for other causes and observing rather than treating bacteriuria in an older patient with delirium or a fall but no urinary symptoms or systemic infection signs. Fever or hemodynamic instability changes the assessment and may require urgent empiric treatment while identifying a source.  [IDSA asymptomatic bacteriuria guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/asymptomatic-bacteriuria/)

**Clinical Case: quiet decline still needs acute assessment.** An 86-year-old becomes withdrawn, leaves meals untouched and cannot follow conversation after several days of diarrhea. Promptly assess vital signs, glucose, hydration, renal/electrolyte disturbance and other causes. Arrange acute care if home evaluation or support is unsafe; reassess attention and function after treatment.

### Evaluate persistent cognitive change rather than naming it from a score

| Pattern | Typical time course and clinical distinction | Next reasoning step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delirium | New fluctuation and impaired attention over hours/days | Find acute causes and protect safety |
| Mild cognitive impairment | Objective decline with relative preservation of everyday independence | Establish baseline, etiology and follow-up |
| Dementia syndrome | Cognitive decline interferes with independent daily function | Define the syndrome, contributors and likely disease |
| Depression-associated cognitive symptoms | Mood, motivation and concentration problems may impair performance | Treat depression and reassess; coexistence remains possible |

Obtain history from the patient and a knowledgeable informant, including medicines, mood, sleep, sensory impairment and the pattern of functional loss. Use a validated cognitive assessment appropriate to language, education and sensory/motor ability. An isolated score neither confirms dementia nor excludes it. Financial mistakes, getting lost or failure to manage medicines can be important even when a brief social conversation seems fluent. [NIA assessment of cognitive impairment](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/health-care-professionals-information/assessing-cognitive-impairment-older-patients)

The Alzheimer's Association DETeCD-ADRD framework organizes evaluation into cognitive/functional status, the cognitive-behavioral syndrome and likely etiologies. It recommends Tier 1 laboratory assessment and structural imaging in almost all symptomatic patients: MRI without contrast is preferred when feasible, with noncontrast CT an alternative. The laboratory panel includes CBC/differential, comprehensive metabolic assessment, TSH, B12, homocysteine, ESR and CRP. Interpret abnormalities and direct further testing to the history. Rapid progression, atypical features or diagnostic uncertainty warrants specialist evaluation. [DETeCD-ADRD primary-care recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12173843/)

Specialist pathways may use disease biomarkers to refine etiology and treatment eligibility. The 2025 blood-biomarker guideline applies to objectively cognitively impaired patients in specialized memory care; it does not turn a general-population blood test into a standalone dementia diagnosis. Biomarker testing and disease-modifying treatment require separate eligibility, counseling and safety assessment; see 11C. [Alzheimer's Association 2025 biomarker guidance](https://aaic.alz.org/releases-2025/clinical-practice-guideline-blood-based-biomarkers.asp)

USPSTF's insufficient-evidence statement concerns screening asymptomatic community-dwelling adults 65 or older, not evaluation of a complaint or observed functional change. Explain findings, uncertainty and the follow-up plan; assess driving, medication supervision, finances, falls, wandering and caregiver needs. Plan reassessment after delirium or depression treatment instead of assuming all deficits will disappear. [USPSTF cognitive-screening scope](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/cognitive-impairment-in-older-adults-screening)

### Assess mood without dismissing symptoms as aging

Depression may present with loss of interest, withdrawal, sleep/appetite change, pain complaints or difficulty concentrating. Ask about grief, isolation, hopelessness and self-harm, while checking medical and medication contributors. Depression and neurodegenerative disease can coexist. "Pseudodementia" implies neither feigning nor guaranteed cognitive recovery.

USPSTF recommends depression screening in adults, including older adults, with evaluation and care after a positive result. A PHQ or geriatric depression measure helps identify symptoms, but a score is not the full diagnosis. Assess bipolar history, substance use, function and safety before treatment. Active intent, psychosis or inability to maintain safety requires urgent assessment regardless of the screening recommendation. Psychotherapy, appropriate medication and social support should be linked to response monitoring; detailed treatment is covered in the behavioral-health and older-adult medication volumes. [USPSTF depression screening and follow-up](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/screening-depression-suicide-risk-adults)

### Characterize incontinence before prescribing a bladder drug

Ask about leakage with urgency or exertion, continuous dribbling, stream, incomplete emptying, nocturia, dysuria, hematuria and the ability to reach/use a toilet. Review fluid/caffeine patterns, diuretics, constipation, diabetes, neurological disease and cognition. Use a bladder diary when useful. Examine according to symptoms, including neurological, abdominal and pelvic/prostate findings when indicated; check urinalysis and postvoid residual when the presentation warrants them. Retention, gross hematuria, recurrent infection or neurological red flags may require prompt referral.

| Leakage pattern | Important mechanism or contributor | Initial management direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stress-related | Inadequate urethral support/closure during exertion | Appropriate pelvic-floor treatment and targeted assessment |
| Urgency-related | Urgency with leakage; exclude relevant mimics | Bladder training, access changes and individualized further therapy |
| Retention/overflow | Incomplete emptying, obstruction or impaired contraction | Measure emptying and address obstruction/medicines/neurological causes |
| Functional | Toileting barriers despite potential bladder control | Adapt the environment, clothing, reminders and assistance |

Patterns can coexist. Functional incontinence reflects mobility, communication or cognitive barriers: improve access, prompted/scheduled toileting and skin care. Pads contain leakage without diagnosing it; long-term catheterization is inappropriate for convenience. [NIDDK incontinence mechanisms](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/bladder-control-problems/definition-facts), [NIDDK treatment options](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/bladder-control-problems/treatment)

If medication is considered for overactive bladder, discuss antimuscarinic cognitive risk, constipation and retention along with potential benefit. A beta-3 agonist has a different adverse-effect/interaction profile, not universal safety. Avoid adding a bladder antimuscarinic to counter symptoms created by another medicine without reviewing the entire regimen. The AUA/SUFU 2024 approach emphasizes individualized decisions rather than automatically progressing every patient through the same drug sequence. [AUA/SUFU overactive-bladder guideline](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2024%20Guidelines/OAB%202024%20Unabridgedv2.pdf)

**Clinical Case: treat the route, not only the bladder.** A patient with hand arthritis and slow transfers develops leakage when renovation moves the bathroom upstairs, without new urgency, dysuria or retention. Assess toileting access; a downstairs commode, easier clothing and assistance may help more than an anticholinergic that adds confusion and constipation.

### Address constipation, impaction and skin risk together

Ask about usual stool pattern, consistency, straining, evacuation difficulty, pain, medicines and intake. New bleeding, weight loss, anemia, marked pain, vomiting or an abrupt unexplained change requires evaluation for more than uncomplicated constipation. Loose stool does not exclude impaction; leakage around retained stool can be mistaken for diarrhea. Examine for impaction when indicated, with consent and attention to pain and privacy.

Correct contributing medicines and access problems, encourage appropriate food/fluid/activity and establish an achievable toileting routine. For eligible chronic idiopathic constipation, AGA/ACG recommends polyethylene glycol; choose additional therapy according to response, contraindications and the suspected mechanism. Avoid simply adding bulk to suspected obstruction or an uncleared impaction. Renal impairment and dehydration make some magnesium/phosphate products particularly problematic. [AGA/ACG constipation treatment](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/pharmacological-management-of-chronic-idiopathic-constipation-cic/), [FDA sodium-phosphate safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/media/87428/download)

A common adult PEG 3350 regimen is **17 g orally once daily**, fully dissolved in 4-8 ounces of an appropriate beverage. Do not mix it with a starch-based swallowing thickener, which can lose its thickening effect. Kidney disease, dysphagia, persistent need or worsening pain/vomiting requires clinician review; prolonged treatment is supervised rather than indefinite OTC self-treatment. Adjust to response and stop for significant diarrhea. [Current PEG 3350 product directions and precautions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=219bcbc4-b84a-4209-9f34-ef2b0ef0d273)

Persistent symptoms despite an adequate regimen should prompt reassessment of adherence, medication effects and defecatory disorders. The 2026 AGA refractory-constipation update emphasizes evaluating pelvic-floor dysfunction and appropriate biofeedback rather than escalating indefinitely without understanding the mechanism. Surgery requires specialized physiological evaluation. [AGA 2026 refractory-constipation update](https://gastro.org/news/14-best-practices-for-patients-with-refractory-constipation/)

Limited movement, moisture, poor nutrition and impaired sensation increase pressure-injury risk. Inspect vulnerable skin and device-contact areas, including heels and the sacral region, and ask about pain. Persistent nonblanching discoloration, warmth, firmness or tenderness can precede an open wound; color changes may be less obvious in darker skin. Differentiate pressure/shear damage from moisture dermatitis, skin tears and arterial/venous wounds. Record location, dimensions, depth, tissue appearance, surrounding skin and a validated classification. Do not massage threatened skin. [NICE pressure-injury assessment and classification](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg179/chapter/Recommendations)

Use the full staging assessment in 08D rather than labeling every intact discoloration stage 1: deep-red, maroon or purple change can indicate deeper injury. Healing injuries retain their original stage rather than being reverse-staged as they become shallower. Offloading and follow-up must continue even when the surface looks improved. [International Guideline classification](https://www.internationalguideline.com/classification)

Use an individualized repositioning and pressure-redistribution plan, with heel offloading, moisture management and appropriate support surfaces. A special mattress does not eliminate the need to reposition. The current International Guideline bases intervals on mobility, tissue response, clinical condition, comfort, sleep, goals and the surface used rather than one universal schedule. Escalate deep tissue injury, necrosis, spreading infection or suspected underlying bone infection for appropriate wound/medical assessment. Nutrition and pain management remain part of wound care. [International Guideline, current repositioning chapter](https://www.internationalguideline.com/repositioning)

### Investigate weight loss, intake and hydration

Track weight and the reason for change rather than interpreting BMI alone. Ask about appetite, food availability, shopping/cooking ability, dentures, oral pain, swallowing, nausea, bowel symptoms, medicines, mood and ability to feed oneself. Unintentional loss can reflect social barriers, disease or both; edema can mask loss of lean tissue. Evaluate the history and examination, then select laboratory, gastrointestinal or other investigation from the findings. Do not diagnose malnutrition from albumin alone.

ESPEN's geriatric nutrition guidance uses approximately 30 kcal/kg/day as an initial energy guide and at least 1 g protein/kg/day, with adjustment for nutritional state, activity, disease and tolerance. Individualize these planning guides for kidney disease, fluid restriction, severe illness and refeeding risk. Use acceptable fortified foods, assistance, oral supplements when indicated and dietitian involvement. Review swallowing concerns with the appropriate team; a restrictive texture/fluid plan can worsen intake if its burden is ignored. [ESPEN clinical nutrition and hydration in geriatrics](https://2022.espen.org/files/ESPEN-Guidelines/ESPEN_practical_guideline_Clinical_nutrition_and_hydration_in_geriatrics.pdf)

**Clinical Case: a supplement misses the first obstacle.** An older adult unintentionally loses weight from 65 to 60 kg over six months, about 7.7%, after painful dentures and a bus-route change disrupt eating and shopping. Investigate medical causes and arrange dental care, accessible meals and dietitian support alongside supplementation.

Check hydration when intake is low, particularly with diuretics, fever, diarrhea, heat or dependence for drinks. Thirst and isolated physical signs may be unreliable. Review weight, symptoms, fluid losses, urine output and relevant chemistry in context. Avoid a generic instruction to drink several liters when heart failure or another condition requires a specific plan. Set an intake and monitoring plan the patient and helpers can actually carry out.

### Correct sensory and social barriers before labeling poor cooperation

Hearing loss can impair communication, medication understanding, social participation and cognitive-test performance. Ask about difficulty in noise, telephone use and family concerns, inspect for cerumen or ear disease and arrange audiology when indicated. Face the patient, reduce background noise and confirm understanding; shouting is not a communication strategy. Sudden hearing loss is not ordinary presbycusis and requires urgent medical evaluation. [NIDCD sudden-hearing-loss guidance](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness)

USPSTF's hearing I statement applies to asymptomatic adults 50 or older. It does not mean withholding evaluation or rehabilitation for a symptom or functional concern. Similarly, its visual-acuity screening statement concerns asymptomatic adults 65 or older, not difficulty reading medicine labels, new visual loss or a painful eye. Evaluate symptoms, review glasses and arrange optometric/ophthalmic care for the problem; acute visual change follows an urgent eye pathway. [USPSTF hearing-screening scope](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/RecommendationStatementFinal/hearing-loss-in-older-adults-screening), [USPSTF visual-screening scope](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/final-evidence-summary/impaired-visual-acuity-screening-older-adults)

Social isolation means limited contact; loneliness means unwanted disconnection. Neither household size nor living alone determines the experience. Ask what contact is wanted and what prevents it. Address hearing, mobility, transport, bereavement, depression, finances and digital access. Offer a personally meaningful connection, activity or community service rather than prescribing indiscriminate social contact. [WHO social connection in older age](https://www.who.int/health-topics/ageing/reducing-social-isolation-and-loneliness-among-older-people)

### Address driving and caregiver safety explicitly

Driving depends on vision, attention, judgment, motor ability and the effects of disease/medication, not age alone. Ask about recent collisions, near misses, getting lost, family concerns and driving exposure. A brief cognitive score is not an on-road examination, and a normal office conversation does not establish driving safety. When concerns remain, arrange an appropriate driving evaluation and discuss stopping or restricting driving when clinically necessary. Check current jurisdictional reporting/licensing rules rather than assuming one national rule. [NIA driving and dementia safety](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/safety/driving-safety-and-alzheimers-disease)

Prepare transportation alternatives before a crisis when possible: appointments, food shopping and valued social activities still need to happen. Address substantial current risk promptly. Document the advice, the person's understanding, referrals and reassessment. Include other safety tasks such as cooking, firearms, medication storage or wandering when relevant, without treating every diagnosis as justification for removing all autonomy.

Ask caregivers separately about sleep, health, lifting, work, financial strain and the tasks they cannot safely perform. Teach specific techniques, clarify the care plan and arrange respite, home support or a higher level of care when needs exceed available help. Assess hidden exhaustion. Distinguish caregiver burden, unintentional unmet need and deliberate exploitation while protecting the older person's safety.

Suspect abuse or neglect when the history, injuries, medication access, finances or observed interactions raise concern. Speak privately when safe, use an independent interpreter, examine/document objectively and ask about coercion or fear. Psychological, sexual and financial harm may occur without obvious bruises. Avoid confronting a suspected abuser in a way that increases danger. [ACL definitions of elder mistreatment](https://acl.gov/programs/elder-justice/what-elder-abuse)

USPSTF 2025 finds insufficient evidence for routine caregiver-abuse screening in asymptomatic older/vulnerable adults; this does not apply to recognized signs or concerns. Act on suspected harm, assess capacity and immediate safety, and follow current local reporting duties. Adult Protective Services can investigate and arrange support; immediate danger requires emergency protection. Reporting does not replace treatment or a safe discharge plan. [USPSTF caregiver-abuse screening scope](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/RecommendationStatementFinal/intimate-partner-violence-and-abuse-of-elderly-and-vulnerable-adults-screening), [ACL reporting and protection pathway](https://acl.gov/programs/protection-and-advocacy-systems/what-if-i-suspect-abuse-neglect-or-exploitation)

**Clinical Case: discharge needs a workable caregiver plan.** A daughter reports back pain, sleep loss and missed doses while caring for a parent with increasing transfer needs who wants to remain home. Assess safety without blame, simplify treatment and arrange assistance/respite. Use a safer interim setting if home care remains unworkable.

### Reassess function as an outcome

Link each problem to an action, responsible clinician/helper and review date. Reassess valued activities, adverse effects and caregiver burden. Investigate new decline and adjust support as function improves. Medication review and chronic care follow in 15B.
