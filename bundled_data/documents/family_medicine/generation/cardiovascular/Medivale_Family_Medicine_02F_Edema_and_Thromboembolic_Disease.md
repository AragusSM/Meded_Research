# Medivale Family Medicine

## 02F. Edema and Thromboembolic Disease

### Classify swelling before choosing a treatment

Edema occurs when tissue filtration exceeds venous and lymphatic return. Venous pressure, sodium retention, low plasma oncotic pressure, inflammation, and impaired lymph drainage may coexist. Distinguish these mechanisms before prescribing diuretics.

Start with **onset, laterality, pain, and systemic symptoms**. New unilateral swelling requires attention to DVT, cellulitis, injury, a ruptured popliteal cyst, and local venous or lymphatic obstruction. Chronic asymmetric swelling can reflect previous DVT, venous reflux, or lymphatic damage. Bilateral swelling suggests a broader search for HF, kidney disease with sodium retention or protein loss, liver disease, medications, venous insufficiency, and other systemic causes. Bilateral distribution does not make thrombosis impossible, particularly with proximal obstruction or strong risk factors.

Ask about surgery, hospitalization, immobility, cancer, prior VTE, pregnancy/postpartum status, estrogen exposure, pelvic procedures, and radiation. Assess dependency/overnight variation, clothing fit, breathlessness, orthopnea, weight change, foamy urine, jaundice, fever, and skin breakdown. Review calcium-channel blockers, NSAIDs, thiazolidinediones, hormones, and other contributors. Dihydropyridine-related ankle edema can result from altered capillary pressures rather than whole-body volume excess; adding a loop diuretic may produce a prescribing cascade.

Examine both legs, pulses and temperature, skin, wounds, varicosities, tenderness, and the foot/toe distribution. Evaluate jugular venous pressure, heart and lungs, abdomen, and signs of systemic disease. Pitting does not prove HF, and early lymphedema can pit. Neither calf tenderness nor a Homan sign reliably determines DVT imaging needs. Severe pain with a cool, pale, pulseless limb suggests acute arterial ischemia, a different emergency.

Use focused testing: kidney function and electrolytes, albumin/liver tests, urinalysis with protein quantification, thyroid testing, and cardiac evaluation as indicated. Venous duplex can assess suspected thrombosis or later evaluate reflux, but the protocol and question differ. Sudden swelling with chest pain, syncope, hypoxemia, or breathlessness requires urgent PE assessment. Investigate acute changes despite prior venous insufficiency.

### Use probability to investigate possible DVT or PE

Use a validated probability pathway with its own thresholds and population; DVT and PE Wells rules differ. For PE, relevant features include DVT signs, tachycardia, prior VTE, recent immobilization/surgery, hemoptysis, malignancy, and whether PE is more likely than another diagnosis. Keep each version's items and thresholds together; deterioration overrides outpatient scoring.

For a stable nonpregnant adult, the **two-level DVT Wells rule** assigns one point each for active cancer, lower-limb paralysis/paresis or plaster immobilization, bed rest at least three days or major surgery in the previous 12 weeks, deep-venous tenderness, whole-leg swelling, calf asymmetry at least 3 cm, unilateral pitting edema, nonvaricose collateral superficial veins, and prior documented DVT; subtract two if another diagnosis is at least as likely. Surgery must have required general or regional anesthesia. A score **at least 2 is DVT likely**: obtain ultrasound; **at most 1 is unlikely**: begin with a highly sensitive D-dimer. Measure calf asymmetry at a standardized site. Do not count ordinary varicosities as collaterals.

The separate **two-level PE Wells rule** uses 3 points each for clinical DVT signs and PE being more likely than alternatives; 1.5 each for heart rate above 100/min, prior DVT/PE, and immobilization at least three days or surgery within four weeks; and 1 each for hemoptysis and malignancy on treatment, treated within six months, or receiving palliation. **Above 4 is PE likely** and calls for imaging; **4 or below** permits a D-dimer-first pathway when appropriate. Neither rule diagnoses a clot. [NICE NG158, validated two-level rules and diagnostic pathways](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng158/chapter/Recommendations)

In genuinely low-risk patients, a negative highly sensitive D-dimer can avoid imaging. A positive result is not a clot diagnosis: age, inflammation, cancer, recent surgery, and pregnancy commonly raise it. High clinical probability generally calls for imaging rather than using D-dimer to reassure. For an appropriate outpatient older than 50 using an assay with a standard cutoff of 500 ng/mL FEU, an age-adjusted cutoff is age multiplied by 10 ng/mL FEU. At age 74, that is 740 ng/mL FEU. FEU and DDU are different reporting conventions; use the laboratory's validated units and pathway, not the number alone. [ASH diagnostic recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258916/)

PERC is a no-testing rule for selected adults whose clinical probability is already very low. Its negative criteria include age under 50, pulse under 100/min, oxygen saturation at least 95%, and absence of unilateral leg swelling, hemoptysis, prior VTE, estrogen use, and recent qualifying surgery/trauma. All criteria must be met in a validated setting; a positive item requires further evaluation, not a PE diagnosis. Do not apply it to moderate/high suspicion or casually extrapolate it to pregnancy. [ACEP diagnostic pathway](https://www.acep.org/siteassets/uploads/uploaded-files/acep/advocacy/federal-issues/equal/pe-pathway-ema-final.pdf)

For suspected leg DVT, compression ultrasound is the principal imaging test. Confirm proximal versus whole-leg coverage. A negative proximal study with continuing suspicion may require repeat ultrasound approximately one week later or another appropriate imaging strategy. A technically adequate negative whole-leg examination can complete some lower-probability pathways. Unevaluated segments remain unexcluded. Prior images are valuable when distinguishing recurrent thrombosis from chronic residual changes. [ASH diagnostic implementation](https://www.hematology.org/education/clinicians/guidelines-and-quality-care/clinical-practice-guidelines/venous-thromboembolism-guidelines/diagnosis)

An **isolated distal DVT** below the popliteal vein is not automatically managed like proximal DVT. Without severe symptoms or extension risks, weekly ultrasound for two weeks is an option; severe symptoms, extensive clot, cancer, prior VTE, or proximity to proximal veins favors anticoagulation. If surveillance is selected, ensure reliable imaging and act on extension; proximal extension requires treatment. When treating, use a therapeutic VTE regimen, not a prophylactic dose. A markedly painful, cyanotic, extensively swollen limb needs emergency assessment for limb-threatening venous obstruction. [CHEST distal-DVT recommendations](https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012-3692(21)01506-3/fulltext)

CT pulmonary angiography is a common definitive test for suspected PE when imaging is indicated. A V/Q study can be useful when iodinated contrast is unsuitable or in selected radiation-sensitive pathways, with chest imaging and local expertise informing the choice. A nondiagnostic result is not negative. Pregnancy requires a pregnancy-specific algorithm and coordinated imaging decisions, not avoidance of necessary testing. For an unstable patient who cannot safely undergo CT, urgent bedside assessment and specialist-directed treatment take priority over the ordinary stable-patient sequence. [2026 PE diagnostic framework](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.005)

For substantial suspicion with delayed imaging, arrange urgent supervised assessment of interim anticoagulation, weighing bleeding risk, contraindications, delay, and setting. Chest radiography, ECG, and blood gases may identify alternatives or severity clues, but none independently rules out PE. Likewise, a normal oxygen saturation does not exclude a small or early embolus.

### Determine PE severity independently of clot detection

After confirming PE, assess pressure, perfusion, oxygen needs, clinical severity, RV function, and biomarkers. Neither normotension nor clot location alone defines physiologic severity. Use a validated severity/disposition tool such as PESI/sPESI or Hestia appropriately, together with bleeding risk, competing illness, treatment access, and reliable follow-up.

| 2026 AHA/ACC PE category | Broad clinical distinction |
| --- | --- |
| A | Incidental, asymptomatic PE. |
| B | Symptomatic PE with a low clinical severity score. |
| C | Symptomatic PE with an elevated severity score; RV/biomarker subcategories refine risk. |
| D | Incipient cardiopulmonary failure. |
| E | Established cardiopulmonary failure, including persistent hypotension or arrest. |

Apply the full operational criteria when assigning categories. Selected A/B patients can be treated without prolonged hospitalization when the rest of the assessment supports it. Categories C-E require hospitalization. Escalation includes multidisciplinary PE-response expertise; advanced reperfusion is considered for selected deteriorating or high-risk patients, not routinely for every abnormal RV study. [2026 category and treatment overview](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-for-the-evaluation-and-management-of-acute-pulmonary-embolism-in-adults/top-things-to-know)

Anticoagulation limits propagation and recurrence but does not immediately dissolve obstruction. Specialists select systemic thrombolysis, catheter treatment, or embolectomy according to shock, deterioration, contraindications, and bleeding/procedural risk. Transfer urgently rather than selecting reperfusion in an unmonitored office.

### Choose anticoagulation for the indication and the patient

Before prescribing, confirm the indication, treatment phase, weight, CBC/platelets, kidney and liver function, bleeding history, pregnancy possibility, and interacting medications. Confirm immediate and continuing access. Specify the first dose, transitions, follow-up, and contacts for missed doses or supply interruption.

For eligible PE patients, DOACs are preferred over VKAs. When initial parenteral therapy is required, the 2026 guideline generally favors LMWH over UFH; severe kidney dysfunction, planned procedures, or other clinical circumstances may change the choice. Stable uncomplicated DVT and selected low-risk PE can be treated at home when clinical and practical criteria are met. Home treatment requires the same structured assessment and follow-up. [2026 anticoagulant framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-for-the-evaluation-and-management-of-acute-pulmonary-embolism-in-adults)

| Regimen distinction | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Apixaban or rivaroxaban for acute VTE | Use the specific intensified initial oral phase, then the ongoing treatment regimen. |
| Dabigatran or edoxaban for acute VTE | Require an initial therapeutic parenteral anticoagulant course before the oral phase. |
| Warfarin for acute VTE | Requires therapeutic parenteral overlap during initiation and INR-guided dosing. |
| Extended prevention | A later reduced-dose option is not interchangeable with acute treatment. |

For an otherwise eligible adult, oral apixaban is **10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily** for DVT/PE treatment. Its **2.5 mg twice-daily** recurrence-prevention regimen follows at least **6 months** of treatment. Do not import the AF age/weight/creatinine reduction rule into initial VTE treatment. Strong combined P-gp/CYP3A4 inhibitors require dose reduction or avoidance according to the label; strong combined inducers should be avoided. Severe kidney disease needs individualized assessment: the label's renal dosing statements are not proof of trial efficacy in dialysis or CrCl below 15 mL/min. Oral apixaban is not initial substitution for heparin in unstable PE or when thrombolysis/embolectomy is needed. [Apixaban prescribing information, revised August 2026](https://packageinserts.bms.com/pi/pi_eliquis.pdf)

Dabigatran treatment requires **5-10 days of parenteral anticoagulation first**; the adult VTE regimen is **150 mg orally twice daily only with CrCl above 30 mL/min**, with additional interaction exclusions. Its AF renal regimen is not a VTE alternative. Warfarin initiation overlaps therapeutic parenteral anticoagulation for at least 5 days and until the INR is therapeutic under the protocol; the usual VTE target INR is **2.0-3.0**. Maintain reasonably consistent dietary vitamin K rather than avoiding all vegetables, and review new antibiotics, illness, and interactions. DOACs do not use INR for routine dose adjustment. [Dabigatran labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=ba74e3cd-b06f-4145-b284-5fd6b84ff3c9); [ASH anticoagulation management](https://www.hematology.org/education/clinicians/guidelines-and-quality-care/clinical-practice-guidelines/venous-thromboembolism-guidelines/anticoagulation-therapy)

LMWH is generally preferred for pregnancy-associated VTE; coordinate delivery, postpartum therapy, and neuraxial plans. Mechanical valves are not a DOAC indication. DOACs are also inappropriate for some high-risk antiphospholipid presentations, particularly triple-positive APS. Active cancer requires attention to tumor bleeding sites, GI absorption, platelet count, and drug interactions, with LMWH or a suitable DOAC chosen individually. Advanced kidney/liver disease is another reason not to extrapolate a standard regimen. [Pregnancy VTE recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258928/) [Current DOAC statement](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.05.033)

During breastfeeding, heparins or warfarin are established options when anticoagulation is needed; avoid DOACs. [ASH lactation guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258928/) Treatment of a pregnancy-associated clot generally continues through pregnancy, for at least six weeks postpartum, and for at least three months overall, with obstetric/hematology coordination. [RCOG acute pregnancy-VTE guidance](https://www.rcog.org.uk/media/wj2lpco5/gtg-37b-1.pdf)

During ongoing anticoagulation, review adherence, bleeding, new medicines, and changes in weight or organ function. Repeat CBC and kidney/liver assessment according to clinical risk; acute illness or suspected blood loss warrants earlier testing. ASH describes renal monitoring every 6-12 months for VTE patients taking a DOAC with creatinine clearance at least 50 mL/min, and approximately every 3 months when clearance is below 50, if that drug remains appropriate. Reassess drug eligibility and dose when function changes; monitoring cannot override a contraindication. [ASH monitoring recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258922/)

### Plan duration and follow-up before the prescription ends

Distinguish a major reversible provoking factor, a persistent risk factor, and an event without an identified major reversible trigger. Primary treatment is usually 3-6 months. A first event after major surgery, once that risk resolves, often needs a finite course, commonly 3 months; persistent risk or an unprovoked event may warrant extended therapy after bleeding-risk discussion. A long flight is not equivalent to major surgery. Individualize duration to recurrence risk, bleeding, preferences, and feasibility. [ASH treatment framework](https://www.hematology.org/education/clinicians/guidelines-and-quality-care/clinical-practice-guidelines/venous-thromboembolism-guidelines/treatment)

For PE without a major reversible factor, or with a persistent risk factor, current guidance recommends continuing beyond the initial phase when appropriate. Arrange communication or a visit within one week of discharge and a clinic visit by 3 months to review symptoms, adherence, bleeding, and duration. Ask about functional limitation at visits for at least a year. Persistent dyspnea, exercise intolerance, or right-heart findings can indicate chronic thromboembolic pulmonary disease or another diagnosis; do not dismiss them as inevitable deconditioning. [2026 follow-up recommendations](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2026/02/19/19/27/first-ahaacc-acute-pulmonary-embolism-guideline-prompt-diagnosis-and-treatment-are-key)

Routine broad hereditary-thrombophilia testing after a clearly surgery-provoked event seldom improves management. Testing is selective when its result would change a real decision; pregnancy/hormone-related events and unusual histories need more nuanced assessment. Anticoagulants and acute thrombosis can distort some assays. An incidental panel abnormality alone should not determine duration. An IVC filter is not routinely added to effective anticoagulation; selected patients with an acute clot and a contraindication require specialist review and a retrieval plan. [ASH testing and filter cautions](https://www.hematology.org/education/clinicians/guidelines-and-quality-care/choosing-wisely)

### Respond to bleeding and plan procedures explicitly

Teach patients to seek urgent assessment for substantial bleeding, black stools, vomiting blood, new severe headache, neurologic symptoms, syncope, or significant head trauma. Bruising is different from a critical-site hemorrhage, but recurrent lesser bleeding still deserves review. Check the anticoagulant, last dose, kidney function, interacting antiplatelets/NSAIDs, and source of bleeding. A normal INR does not exclude clinically important DOAC exposure. Evaluate the cause before reducing an indicated dose.

Major bleeding requires immediate stabilization, interruption of anticoagulation, source control, and emergency specialist care. Severe warfarin bleeding is generally managed with IV vitamin K plus four-factor PCC; idarucizumab specifically reverses dabigatran. Factor Xa inhibitor bleeding may require PCC-based reversal under an emergency protocol; its use for this indication is off-label and carries thrombotic risk. Reversal is not necessary for every minor bleed, and the restart decision must balance rebleeding with the original thrombotic indication. [ACC bleeding framework](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2020/07/10/11/26/2020-ACC-Expert-Consensus-Decision-Pathway-on-Bleeding)

**Andexanet alfa is not a routinely available US rescue option.** The FDA concluded that Andexxa's risks outweighed its benefits and reported that US manufacturing/sales would end after December 22, 2025. Follow the current emergency protocol and formulary. [FDA safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/safety-availability-biologics/update-safety-andexxa)

Before a procedure, document its bleeding risk, the anticoagulant and indication, kidney function, thrombotic risk, last dose, interruption schedule, and restart plan. Some minor procedures need little or no interruption; neuraxial procedures require separate safety intervals. Routine heparin bridging is not appropriate for most interrupted oral anticoagulation and increases bleeding risk. Selected very-high-risk warfarin patients need an individualized team decision. Give individualized interruption and restart instructions. [2026 perioperative guidance](https://www.jacc.org/doi/full/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.06.017)

### Treat venous and lymphatic swelling without causing ischemia

Chronic venous hypertension causes dependent heaviness, swelling, varicosities, gaiter-region pigmentation, stasis dermatitis, and sometimes ulcers near the medial ankle. Walking/calf-muscle activity, elevation, weight management, skin care, and appropriate compression improve venous return. Persistent symptoms, recurrent ulcers, bleeding varicosities, or suspected obstruction warrant vascular evaluation; duplex reflux testing can guide intervention. Venous inflammation is not automatically infection, so bilateral stasis redness should not trigger reflex antibiotics. [SVS venous guidance](https://vascular.org/your-vascular-health/vascular-conditions/chronic-venous-insufficiency)

Assess arterial perfusion before substantial compression when PAD is possible. Claudication, ischemic pain, cool feet, weak pulses, or nonhealing wounds require assessment, often ABI and toe-based testing if arteries are noncompressible. A high ABI does not prove good perfusion. Significant arterial insufficiency can make ordinary compression unsafe; mixed disease needs vascular/wound-directed pressure selection. Use the active-ulcer compression pathway in 08D. Acute limb ischemia and decompensated HF require assessment before routine edema treatment. [WOCN venous-disease assessment and compression](https://vlu.wocn.org/)

Lymphedema can follow lymph-node surgery, radiation, malignancy, infection, or primary lymphatic abnormalities. It may involve the foot/toes and evolve from soft pitting swelling to skin thickening and fibrosis. A positive Stemmer sign supports the diagnosis, but its absence does not exclude it. New unilateral swelling after cancer treatment still needs assessment for thrombosis or recurrence rather than automatic attribution to old lymphatic injury. [SVS lymphedema overview](https://vascular.org/your-vascular-health/vascular-conditions/lymphedema)

Management uses appropriately fitted compression, exercise, skin protection, and specialist decongestive therapy; manual lymph drainage or pneumatic devices may be selected components. Treat fissures and infection promptly. Diuretics do not correct isolated lymphatic failure or uncomplicated venous reflux, although a separate systemic indication may coexist. Lipedema more often causes symmetric tender fatty enlargement with relative foot sparing and easy bruising; it is not simply another name for lymphedema. Reassess uncertain or worsening swelling before escalation. [NCI lymphedema review](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/side-effects/lymphedema/lymphedema-hp-pdq)

For a chronic leg wound, document location, dimensions, surrounding skin, drainage, pain, perfusion, and change over time. Venous appearance does not exclude an arterial component or another cause. Arrange cause-directed wound care and healing reassessment. Increasing pain, spreading warmth/redness, fever, tissue necrosis, or systemic illness needs prompt reassessment. Patients using compression should know to seek review for new severe pain, numbness, coolness, discoloration, or skin injury; poorly fitted garments can harm rather than help. Functional ability, hand strength, caregiver support, and cost affect whether the prescribed plan can actually be followed.

### An integrated outpatient example

A 64-year-old develops symmetric ankle swelling after an amlodipine increase, without breathlessness, weight gain, focal tenderness, lung findings, or elevated jugular pressure. Assess kidney disease, protein loss, venous disease, and cardiac causes while reviewing the likely medication contribution; avoid automatic diuretic addition.

Two months later, abrupt unilateral calf enlargement after surgery requires a new DVT assessment. Chest pain, fainting, or new breathlessness warrants the urgent PE pathway despite the earlier medication-related edema.
