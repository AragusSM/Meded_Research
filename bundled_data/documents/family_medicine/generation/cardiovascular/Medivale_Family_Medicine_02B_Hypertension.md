# Medivale Family Medicine

## 02B. Hypertension

Adult nonpregnant treatment decisions below use the 2025 AHA/ACC framework. Pregnancy and childhood require distinct pathways.

### First establish what was actually measured

Blood pressure reflects cardiac output, vascular resistance, and measurement conditions. Pain, a full bladder, activity, talking, poor positioning, and an undersized cuff can distort readings; correct technique before diagnosing hypertension.

Use a validated upper-arm device and a cuff appropriate to measured arm size. Avoid smoking, caffeine, and exercise during the preceding 30 minutes; empty the bladder and sit quietly for at least five minutes. Support the back and arm, keep feet flat and legs uncrossed, and position the bare upper arm at heart level. Do not talk during measurement. Repeat readings, allowing about a minute between them. At the initial assessment, compare arms and use the consistently higher-reading arm for subsequent measurements when a difference is established. [AHA home measurement guidance](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/monitoring-your-blood-pressure-at-home)

Except when severity or an acute presentation changes the pathway, base diagnosis on averages from repeated careful readings on more than one occasion and confirm outside the office. **Ambulatory monitoring** samples day and night; **home monitoring** is more accessible and supports longitudinal care. Teach the technique, compare the home device with clinic equipment, and review the complete record rather than a selected extreme. Obtain two readings about one minute apart morning and evening for at least three days, preferably seven; this provides at least 12 readings over three days. Smartwatch estimates should not replace validated cuff measurements. [ACC 2026 home-measurement protocol](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.08.028)

The following adult office categories use the higher category when systolic and diastolic values differ. They are not pregnancy or pediatric diagnostic criteria.

| Adult category | Systolic, mm Hg | Diastolic, mm Hg |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Below 120 | And below 80 |
| Elevated | 120-129 | And below 80 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130-139 | Or 80-89 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | At least 140 | Or at least 90 |

Home, daytime ambulatory, nighttime, and 24-hour averages are not universally interchangeable. For example, the current correspondence table pairs office 130/80 with home/daytime 130/80 but a 24-hour mean of 125/75; office 140/90 corresponds to home/daytime 135/85. Identify the monitoring method before applying a threshold. [2025 guideline, classification and measurement tables](https://www.nefro.cl/web/docs/AHA_Hypertension_Guidelines_2025%5B1%5D.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 39-year-old has pressure of 152/88 after hurrying upstairs and an average of 138/84 after quiet rest with a correctly sized cuff. Explain the measurement effect, confirm the usual pressure, and assess risk before diagnosing sustained stage 2 hypertension.

### Reconcile office and out-of-office results

**White-coat hypertension** means elevated office pressure with normal out-of-office pressure in an untreated person. **Masked hypertension** is the reverse. Treatment creates analogous patterns: a white-coat effect or masked uncontrolled hypertension. Resolve the discrepancy before changing treatment.

Confirm modest office elevations before long-term therapy; marked hypertension or suspected acute organ injury requires prompt assessment. A person with repeatedly normal office readings but kidney disease, unexplained left-ventricular hypertrophy, or consistently high reliable home values deserves assessment for masked hypertension. A treated patient who becomes dizzy at home but remains high in clinic may be harmed by automatic escalation based on the clinic value alone.

White-coat hypertension still needs lifestyle care and follow-up because sustained hypertension may develop. Masked hypertension can expose the patient to substantial pressure outside the examination room. Review cuff accuracy, technique, timing relative to medicines, sleep/work schedules, and the completeness of the record. Use ambulatory monitoring when the discrepancy remains important or nocturnal information is needed. [2025 hypertension guideline, out-of-office diagnosis](https://www.nefro.cl/web/docs/AHA_Hypertension_Guidelines_2025%5B1%5D.pdf)

### Evaluate risk, organ effects, and potentially reversible contributors

Most adult hypertension reflects interacting renal sodium handling, vascular tone, neurohormonal activity, aging, and environmental exposures. Even asymptomatic hypertension increases stroke, coronary disease, heart failure, kidney injury, and vascular cognitive impairment. Treatment balances outcome reduction against harm.

Ask about prior cardiovascular events, diabetes, kidney disease, pregnancy history, sleep apnea, family history, sodium and alcohol intake, physical activity, tobacco, and the actual medication regimen. Include NSAIDs, decongestants, stimulants, estrogen-containing products, glucocorticoids, supplements, and recreational drugs. Establish actual use, including on difficult days. Assess weight, pulse, cardiovascular examination, peripheral pulses, volume status, and findings suggesting endocrine or renal disease. Measure standing pressure when dizziness, falls, frailty, or autonomic dysfunction makes orthostasis relevant.

Baseline testing helps identify comorbidity and makes later treatment safer. Obtain kidney function and electrolytes, assess glycemia and lipids, and evaluate urine for renal abnormalities, including albumin loss. CBC, thyroid testing, and ECG are also included in the current initial evaluation. Urine albumin quantification adds information that a negative routine dipstick may miss. Tailor further evaluation to the history rather than ordering an indiscriminate endocrine imaging panel. [2025 initial evaluation recommendations](https://www.nefro.cl/web/docs/AHA_Hypertension_Guidelines_2025%5B1%5D.pdf)

### Match treatment to pressure and cardiovascular risk

Lifestyle treatment accompanies every stage. In the 2025 framework, medication is recommended for confirmed average pressure **at least 140 systolic or 90 diastolic**. At **at least 130 systolic or 80 diastolic**, medication is also recommended with clinical cardiovascular disease, previous stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or a 10-year **PREVENT cardiovascular risk of at least 7.5%**. For lower-risk adults at this level, begin with lifestyle treatment, adding medication if pressure remains elevated after **three to six months**. [AHA treatment-threshold summary](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know)

For blood-pressure decisions, the relevant PREVENT estimate is **total cardiovascular disease**, including heart failure, not simply the ASCVD-only estimate used for some lipid decisions. Use calculators only within their eligibility limits; established disease and pregnancy-specific indications require their own pathways. [AHA risk-assessment statement](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/use-of-risk-assessment-to-guide-decision-making-for-blood-pressure-management)

The general adult treatment goal is **below 130/80 mm Hg**. Individualize implementation for symptomatic orthostasis, frailty, limited life expectancy, institutional care, pregnancy, and competing priorities. Confirm technique and tolerability before intensifying; avoid abrupt perfusion loss and retain disease-specific targets within their named frameworks.

**Comparison.** Two adults have confirmed averages of 134/82. Established coronary disease supports medication now; low estimated risk without relevant comorbidity supports initial lifestyle treatment with scheduled reassessment and medication if elevation persists.

### Turn lifestyle advice into a feasible prescription

A DASH-style pattern emphasizes vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and appropriate dairy and protein sources, with less saturated fat and sodium. Limit high-sodium packaged and restaurant foods using per-serving sodium labels. NHLBI describes 2,300 mg daily sodium as a DASH planning level, with further blood-pressure reduction at 1,500 mg. Adapt changes to food access and cultural preferences. [NHLBI DASH eating plan](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/dash-eating-plan)

Combine dietary changes with gradual weight reduction when appropriate, regular activity, less alcohol, and attention to sleep. Increasing dietary potassium can help selected patients, but potassium supplements or potassium-based salt substitutes can be unsafe with kidney impairment, hyperkalemia, or potassium-retaining medicines. Review the whole regimen first. Tobacco cessation is important for cardiovascular protection even when it is not the largest contributor to the office pressure.

Agree on attainable dietary and activity changes, home measurements, and follow-up. Address structural barriers while providing indicated medication.

### Choose complementary medicines and anticipate their problems

First-line classes for uncomplicated adult hypertension are a thiazide-type/thiazide-like diuretic, a long-acting dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker, an ACE inhibitor, or an ARB. Stage 2 hypertension generally starts with **two first-line agents from different classes**, preferably in a single-pill combination when appropriate. Stage 1 treatment often begins with one agent. Select products and doses according to baseline pressure, kidney function, adverse-effect risks, availability, and the patient's other treatment needs. [AHA initial-therapy recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline)

| Class or example | Useful clinical reasoning | Problem to anticipate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thiazide-type/thiazide-like diuretic | Reduces sodium-related volume and pressure; often useful in combination | Low sodium or potassium, volume depletion, and increased uric acid; monitor rather than assuming all fatigue is unrelated. |
| Long-acting dihydropyridine CCB, such as amlodipine | Lowers vascular resistance without routinely slowing conduction | Ankle edema, flushing, and headache; edema does not automatically indicate heart failure or a need for another diuretic. |
| ACE inhibitor or ARB | Particularly useful with albuminuric kidney disease and selected cardiovascular indications | Hyperkalemia and a creatinine change; ACE-inhibitor cough or angioedema; fetal toxicity. |
| Beta blocker | Appropriate with a compelling cardiac indication, not a default for every uncomplicated case | Bradycardia, fatigue, conduction effects, and withdrawal risk; select the agent for the actual indication. |

Albuminuric CKD favors tolerated renin-angiotensin-system blockade; diabetes without albuminuria permits other first-line agents. Heart failure requires its own outcome-improving regimen rather than an arbitrary blood-pressure combination. Gout, previous electrolyte problems, orthostasis, pregnancy plans, and medication cost can change a reasonable initial selection. Do not use race as a substitute for kidney assessment, comorbidity review, or evaluation of the individual's treatment response.

Do not combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB or direct renin inhibitor. Additional blockade can increase kidney injury and hyperkalemia without providing the expected benefit. Review NSAIDs, dehydration, and other potassium-raising medicines before interpreting an adverse laboratory change. A small expected hemodynamic creatinine change is different from progressive kidney injury; evaluate the magnitude, timing, volume status, and clinical context. [KDIGO CKD guideline, renin-angiotensin-system practice points](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf)

### Apparent resistance requires verification before escalation

Suspect resistant hypertension when pressure remains above goal despite three complementary agents at adequate tolerated doses, usually an ACE inhibitor or ARB, a long-acting dihydropyridine CCB, and an appropriate diuretic; patients requiring four or more agents for control also need this assessment. Exclude inaccurate measurement, white-coat effect, missed or unaffordable doses, an inadequate regimen, excess sodium, and interfering drugs before escalation.

Look for secondary contributors when onset, severity, examination, laboratory results, or resistance raises suspicion. Kidney disease and sleep apnea are common considerations. Primary aldosteronism can occur with **normal potassium**. The **2025 Endocrine Society guideline conditionally suggests screening all people with hypertension**, subject to resources and local expertise; resistance or hypokalemia makes assessment particularly important, not uniquely permissible. Measure aldosterone and renin together, interpret their concentrations and assay-specific ratio, correct low potassium, and review interfering medicines without unsafe unsupervised withdrawal. See 04E for the positive-screen pathway. A renal bruit, abrupt worsening, or a disproportionate kidney-function decline after renin-angiotensin blockade raises renovascular concerns. Adrenergic episodes, cushingoid or thyroid findings, and arm-leg pressure/pulse differences guide other focused testing; they do not prove a cause. [Endocrine Society final PA recommendations](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-aldosteronism-2)

After confirming genuine resistance and optimizing the regimen, a mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonist is often the next pharmacologic option when kidney function and potassium permit. Obtain an appropriate aldosterone/renin assessment before starting one when evaluating aldosteronism, because treatment can obscure interpretation. The current ACC pathway calls for potassium and creatinine checks **one to two weeks after initiation or titration**, with ongoing surveillance. Severe kidney impairment, hyperkalemia, pregnancy considerations, and interacting medicines can make this inappropriate. Specialist-directed alternatives and device therapy are selected options, not routine substitutes for confirming the diagnosis. [2026 ACC resistant-hypertension pathway](https://www.jacc.org/doi/full/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.08.028)

**Clinical Case.** On three agents, a patient has clinic pressure near 160/90, reliable home averages near 124/76, and morning dizziness. Reconcile timing, measurement, adherence, and out-of-office pressure before escalation; spironolactone could worsen hypotension without true resistance.

### Severe pressure and hypertensive emergency are different problems

Very high pressure requires a careful repeat measurement and assessment for **acute target-organ injury**. New confusion, focal deficits, seizures, acute visual symptoms, ischemic chest pain, pulmonary edema, suspected aortic dissection, or acute kidney injury changes the setting and urgency. Evaluate organ injury rather than attributing these findings to anxiety. A hypertensive emergency can occur at a lower absolute pressure when the rise is abrupt or in a susceptible setting.

In a nonpregnant adult with severe elevation, such as systolic above 180 or diastolic above 120, but no evidence of acute organ injury, evaluate causes, restore or intensify appropriate oral treatment, and arrange timely reassessment. This is different from an emergency requiring monitored treatment. A symptom-free patient still needs assessment; absence of symptoms is not proof that organ injury is absent. If the necessary evaluation or safe close follow-up cannot be arranged, a higher-acuity setting may be needed. [AHA severe-pressure guidance](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know)

Do not try to normalize chronic severe pressure rapidly with repeated rescue doses or short-acting sublingual nifedipine. Abrupt reduction can reduce brain, coronary, or kidney perfusion. In a true emergency, the reduction rate and drug depend on the affected organ; aortic disease, stroke, and pregnancy have distinct protocols. Emergency referral should not be delayed while attempting an office target.

### Monitoring completes the prescription

After starting or intensifying medication for uncontrolled hypertension, reassess approximately **monthly until controlled**, sooner for severe pressure, symptoms, or a high-risk change. Review home readings, orthostatic symptoms, edema, pulse, actual doses taken, affordability, and adverse effects. Once stable, continue periodic monitoring and revisit the plan after acute illness, new medicines, or changed kidney function. Laboratory safety checks may be needed before the next BP visit. [2025 follow-up recommendation](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001356)

With ACE inhibitors/ARBs, check creatinine and potassium after initiation or dose escalation; KDIGO specifies **within two to four weeks in CKD**, depending on baseline filtration and potassium, with earlier testing when risk warrants. Diuretics also require electrolyte and kidney-function follow-up. Hyperkalemia may be asymptomatic; assign result review and patient notification. [KDIGO medication monitoring](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf)

Pregnancy planning is a prescribing decision now, not only after a positive test. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibitors should not be used during pregnancy. Labetalol and extended-release nifedipine are commonly preferred options when treatment is indicated, with contraindications and obstetric assessment considered. Pregnancy uses separate diagnostic, treatment, and severe-pressure pathways; do not apply the nonpregnant 180/120 discussion to a pregnant patient. Severe headache, visual symptoms, epigastric pain, or new dyspnea in pregnancy or postpartum warrants urgent assessment for hypertensive complications. Detailed maternal management is reserved for chapter 12. [2025 pregnancy considerations](https://www.nefro.cl/web/docs/AHA_Hypertension_Guidelines_2025%5B1%5D.pdf)
