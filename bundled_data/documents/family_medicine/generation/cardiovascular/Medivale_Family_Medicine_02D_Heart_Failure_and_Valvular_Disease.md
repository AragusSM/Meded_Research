# Medivale Family Medicine

## 02D. Heart Failure and Valvular Disease

### Identify a clinical syndrome rather than an isolated ejection fraction

Heart failure results from structural or functional cardiac disease causing impaired circulation, elevated filling pressures, or congestion. Breathlessness, reduced stamina, early satiety, abdominal fullness, swelling, and orthopnea also have pulmonary, renal, hematologic, venous, endocrine, or deconditioning-related causes. Ankle swelling alone is insufficient for diagnosis.

The 2026 universal definition emphasizes the combined clinical assessment, evidence of underlying cardiac abnormality, and supportive findings of congestion or altered hemodynamics. It recognizes reduced, preserved, and improved-EF phenotypes without forcing every patient into one rigid numerical cutoff. Record the **actual current and prior LVEF**, the cause, and the clinical course. Check each treatment's trial, guideline, and product eligibility criteria separately. [2026 universal definition](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/47/32/4357/8719720)

With reduced EF, impaired systolic contraction is central, although abnormal filling and right-sided involvement can coexist. With preserved EF, the ventricle may eject a normal proportion of its volume while requiring abnormally high filling pressures, particularly during exertion. A normal percentage does not demonstrate normal filling, reserve, valves, or pulmonary circulation. Conversely, reduced EF without current or previous HF symptoms can represent pre-HF rather than symptomatic disease.

| Clinical stage | Meaning for primary care |
| --- | --- |
| At risk, stage A | Risk factors are present without HF symptoms or established cardiac changes; prevention matters. |
| Pre-HF, stage B | Structural, functional, or relevant biomarker abnormalities exist without prior/current HF symptoms or signs. |
| HF, stage C | Current or previous HF manifestations are attributable to cardiac disease. |
| Advanced HF, stage D | Severe/recurrent disease despite treatment needs specialist assessment and goal-aligned advanced or supportive care. |

Improvement in symptoms or EF does not erase the history or justify stopping protective therapy automatically. Describe functional capacity separately: NYHA class concerns symptom limitation with activity, not a different measurement of EF or a replacement for disease stage. [Current staging consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/47/32/4357/8719720)

### Establish the cause, congestion, and perfusion

Ask about the time course, exertional threshold, orthopnea, nocturnal breathlessness, weight trajectory, chest discomfort, palpitations, fainting, and recent infection or pregnancy. Review coronary disease, longstanding hypertension, valve disease, cardiotoxic treatment, alcohol/stimulants, familial cardiomyopathy, and systemic disease. Identify treatable arrhythmia, ischemia, medication effects, and valve lesions.

Examine vital signs, oxygenation, mentation, jugular venous pressure, heart sounds and murmurs, lung findings, abdomen, edema, and peripheral temperature/perfusion. Look for both **congestion** and **low output**. A patient can be fluid overloaded but hypotensive and poorly perfused. Absence of edema or crackles does not exclude HF, particularly when symptoms occur mainly with activity or the patient is already treated. Obesity and chronic lung disease can make examination more difficult.

ECG evaluates rhythm, conduction, and clues to ischemic or structural disease. Chest imaging can show congestion or another pulmonary explanation. Initial laboratory evaluation usually assesses CBC, electrolytes, kidney and liver function, glycemia, thyroid status, and iron status as appropriate to the presentation. Evaluate suspected acute ischemia using the pathway in 02A. Direct testing to the clinical presentation.

BNP and NT-proBNP can support diagnosis and prognosis, but are **not interchangeable stand-alone verdicts**. Kidney disease, AF, and age can raise concentrations; obesity can lower them. A comparatively low value does not reliably exclude an obese patient with convincing HFpEF physiology. Interpret the result with the presentation and assay. Sacubitril/valsartan also changes how BNP measurements should be interpreted; NT-proBNP is not a neprilysin substrate. [Current HFpEF diagnostic pathway](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.06.018)

**Transthoracic echocardiography with Doppler** establishes ventricular function, chamber dimensions, regional motion, valves, and clues to filling and pulmonary pressures. Assess these findings beyond EF alone. Persistent unexplained exertional symptoms may require specialist-directed stress or invasive hemodynamic assessment when resting tests are insufficient. Suspected infiltrative, inflammatory, genetic, or ischemic cardiomyopathy can warrant additional targeted imaging or testing rather than identical treatment for every patient. [AHA/ACC/HFSA diagnostic recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/832EA0F4E73948848612F228F7FA2D35.ashx)

**Clinical Case.** A 67-year-old with obesity, hypertension, and AF develops exertional breathlessness despite LVEF of 61% and a modest peptide result. Evaluate congestion, diastolic/structural findings, rate control, anemia, pulmonary disease, and functional testing needs; these results do not exclude HFpEF.

### Reduced-EF treatment needs complementary disease-modifying therapy

For eligible symptomatic HFrEF, core therapy includes an **ARNI, an evidence-based beta blocker, an MRA, and an SGLT2 inhibitor**. The standard four-class HFrEF treatment evidence applies particularly to **LVEF at most 40%**; a newer descriptive phenotype does not expand every drug or device indication. ACE inhibitors or ARBs are alternatives when ARNI therapy is not feasible or appropriate. Diuretics relieve congestion but do not replace disease-modifying therapy. Eligibility depends on blood pressure, kidney function, potassium, stability, and contraindications, not just whether the patient has diabetes. [US treatment framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/832EA0F4E73948848612F228F7FA2D35.ashx)

Introduce complementary classes early and titrate promptly toward target or maximally tolerated doses. There is no mandatory sequence; appropriate low doses across classes can precede maximization of one agent. Reassess frequently, with an aim of implementing the core regimen within approximately three months when feasible. [ACC HFrEF implementation recommendations](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2024/03/06/19/22/2024-acc-expert-consensus-hfref)

| Treatment role | Practical safety check |
| --- | --- |
| Sacubitril/valsartan or an appropriate RAS alternative | Blood pressure, potassium, kidney function, pregnancy, and angioedema history; do not overlap an ACE inhibitor with ARNI. |
| Carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, or bisoprolol | Begin/titrate when clinically compensated; assess pulse, conduction, perfusion, and congestion. Other beta blockers are not automatically equivalent. |
| Spironolactone or eplerenone | Assess renal eligibility and potassium before prescribing and after changes; hyperkalemia can be dangerous without obvious symptoms. |
| A suitable SGLT2 inhibitor | Assess volume and kidney status, infection risks, ketoacidosis precautions, and perioperative/acute-illness instructions. HF benefit is not dependent on glucose lowering. |

An ACE inhibitor requires a **36-hour separation** from sacubitril/valsartan because of angioedema risk. Sacubitril/valsartan already contains an ARB; do not add another RAS blocker automatically. For the standard HFrEF steroidal-MRA recommendation, initiation requires **eGFR above 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and potassium below 5.0 mEq/L**, with close subsequent monitoring. Other MRA indications and products have separate thresholds. [AHA/ACC/HFSA drug-safety recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/832EA0F4E73948848612F228F7FA2D35.ashx)

Start or increase a beta blocker after the patient is compensated; do not use a large new dose as an immediate response to pulmonary edema or shock. Conversely, do not reflexively stop every chronic disease-modifying drug for a small creatinine change without examining perfusion, volume, potassium, and the precipitating illness. Hypotension with poor perfusion, severe hyperkalemia, significant acute kidney injury, and cardiogenic shock require a different response from a stable patient's modest hemodynamic laboratory change.

Selected adjuncts include hydralazine/isosorbide dinitrate, ivabradine, digoxin, vericiguat, and devices. Hydralazine/nitrate therapy can benefit appropriate persistently symptomatic patients, including the guideline-defined self-identified Black population, or selected patients unable to use RAS therapy. Digoxin has a narrow safety margin and is not a universal survival-improving substitute. ICD and resynchronization decisions consider EF, rhythm/conduction, treatment duration, prognosis, and patient goals. Primary- and secondary-prevention ICD criteria differ; refer for assessment beyond a single EF. [ACC HFrEF pathway](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2023.12.024)

For primary-prevention ICD assessment, a common qualifying pattern is LVEF at most 35%, NYHA II-III symptoms despite chronic guideline-directed therapy, and meaningful expected survival beyond one year; ischemic disease must be at least 40 days beyond MI, with reassessment after treatment/revascularization. **CRT** is strongly indicated for eligible symptomatic patients on therapy with LVEF at most 35%, sinus rhythm, LBBB, and QRS at least 150 ms. Other pacing/conduction patterns have different criteria. These anchors guide referral, not automatic implantation. [US ICD/CRT recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/832EA0F4E73948848612F228F7FA2D35.ashx)

### Preserved EF also has active treatment options

HFpEF care requires diagnostic confirmation, symptom control, and phenotype-appropriate prevention. Relieve congestion, address hypertension, AF, coronary disease, kidney disease, diabetes, obesity, sleep-disordered breathing, and physical deconditioning. Do not assume that an edema response to a diuretic proves the diagnosis or that every HFrEF drug automatically has the same benefit with preserved EF.

The 2026 ACC pathway identifies SGLT2 inhibitors as cornerstone therapy. It also incorporates finerenone and incretin-based treatment for appropriate patients rather than limiting management to diuretics and comorbidities. Distinguish demonstrated HF-event reduction, symptom improvement, weight change, and mortality outcomes. Beta blockers may be useful for another indication, such as rate control or angina, but are not compulsory disease-modifying therapy for every HFpEF patient. [2026 ACC HFpEF update](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/journal-scans/2026/07/22/17/25/updated-acc-ecdp-addresses-management-of-hfpef)

Finerenone has a US indication to reduce cardiovascular death, HF hospitalization, and urgent HF visits in adults with **HF and LVEF at least 40%**, independent of its separate CKD indications. Do not initiate if potassium is above **5.0 mEq/L**; initiation is not recommended if eGFR is below **25 mL/min/1.73 m2**. Initial oral dosing is 10 mg daily at eGFR 25 to below 60 and 20 mg daily at eGFR at least 60. HF target doses are respectively 20 and 40 mg daily, if potassium and kidney function permit. Recheck both at **four weeks after initiation or dose adjustment**, earlier when risk warrants; use the HF-specific label table for subsequent changes. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and adrenal insufficiency are contraindications. Do not combine MRAs or import the CKD-only titration table into HF prescribing. [Finerenone prescribing information, revised September 2026](https://labeling.bayerhealthcare.com/html/products/pi/Kerendia_PI.pdf)

Semaglutide and tirzepatide have evidence in obesity-related HFpEF, but patient selection, approved indications, tolerability, nutrition, and monitoring still matter. These findings do not establish eligibility from breathlessness or normal EF alone. ARNI/ARB or other therapies may also fit selected patients. Coordinate cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic care to prevent duplicate prescriptions and conflicting instructions. [2026 HFpEF treatment pathway](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.06.018); [CKM integration framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-for-the-prevention-detection-evaluation-and-management-of-ckm-syndrome)

### Manage fluid status without treating the scale alone

Loop diuretics reduce sodium and water retention and can improve breathing and edema. Select dose and route from severity, kidney function, previous response, and absorption; substantial gut edema can limit oral response. In persistent congestion, adding a thiazide-type drug can enhance natriuresis, but also markedly increase electrolyte depletion and kidney risk. Monitor combination therapy closely.

Follow symptoms, weight, orthopnea, jugular pressure, edema, blood pressure, perfusion, and kidney/electrolyte trends to relieve congestion without depletion, hypotension, or electrolyte disturbance. A rising creatinine may reflect either worsening cardiorenal disease or successful decongestion with a hemodynamic change; it cannot be interpreted independently of the patient. Persistent edema can also have venous, medication-related, hepatic, or lymphatic causes.

Teach consistent morning weights and agree on reportable changes, actions, and after-hours contacts. Common warning thresholds include more than two to three pounds in a day or five pounds in a week, but a sudden increase in breathlessness matters even without that weight change. Do not imply that patients should independently double medicines without an agreed written plan. [AHA symptom-management guidance](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-failure/warning-signs-of-heart-failure/managing-heart-failure-symptoms)

Review sodium exposure, alcohol, NSAIDs, adherence, and access. Fluid restriction is individualized; a universal rigid restriction for every stable patient can create thirst and confusion without addressing the actual problem. Monitor sodium, potassium, magnesium when appropriate, and kidney function after clinically important diuretic or RAS/MRA changes. Encourage appropriate exercise or rehabilitation when stable, vaccination, nutrition support, and assessment of iron deficiency and other treatable limitations.

**Clinical Case.** After starting an NSAID, a patient with HF develops weight gain, orthopnea, and edema. Assess severity and volume status and address the NSAID rather than only increasing diuresis. Resting distress or poor perfusion requires emergency assessment.

### Recognize deterioration and make follow-up useful

Severe breathlessness at rest, hypoxemia, chest pain, syncope, confusion, cold extremities, oliguria, hypotension, or a dangerous arrhythmia warrants urgent/emergency evaluation. Look for ischemia, infection, arrhythmia, severe pressure elevation, acute valve disease, renal injury, medication interruption, and harmful exposures. Do not delay transfer to complete a routine office echo or laboratory panel. Oxygen, ventilatory support, IV diuresis, vasodilators, or circulatory support are selected according to the emergency physiology, not delivered as an identical bundle to every patient.

After an HF admission, arrange early follow-up, generally **within seven days**. Reconcile actual medications, the volume/weight plan, pending tests, laboratory dates, and specialist care. Confirm prescriptions were obtained and distinguish rescue instructions from chronic treatment. Assess residual congestion, overdiuresis, bradycardia, hypotension, and laboratory abnormalities. [US transition recommendation](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/832EA0F4E73948848612F228F7FA2D35.ashx)

Repeated admissions, escalating diuretic needs, poor perfusion, falling function, or intolerance of otherwise indicated treatment should prompt HF-specialist assessment. Discuss prognosis and goals early; palliative support can accompany disease-modifying treatment and advanced-therapy assessment.

### A murmur asks a hemodynamic question

Characterize murmur timing, location, radiation, and intensity alongside symptoms, pulse, pressure, rhythm, ventricular response, and congestion. A quiet murmur can accompany severe disease when forward flow is low; a loud murmur does not quantify severity by itself. A new diastolic murmur, exertional syncope, HF symptoms, or suspected significant valve disease generally needs echocardiographic evaluation rather than reassurance based only on auscultation.

Echo integrates anatomy, flow, pressure gradients, valve area or regurgitant severity, chambers, and ventricular response. If symptoms, examination, and initial imaging disagree, investigate the discordance rather than accepting one reassuring number. Severe aortic stenosis with symptoms or ventricular consequences needs valve-team evaluation; selected apparently asymptomatic patients also warrant intervention assessment. Primary severe mitral disease and secondary functional regurgitation follow different pathways, with optimization of HF treatment central to the latter. Medicines can improve congestion or associated conditions without correcting a fixed mechanical obstruction. [Active ACC/AHA valve recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/8ba3a767f9024c63863c5bfc86ff17d5.ashx)

| Pattern to recognize | What it should prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Aortic stenosis: ejection systolic murmur, often radiating to carotids | Assess exertional dyspnea, angina, syncope, ventricular function, and Doppler severity. |
| Mitral regurgitation: apical systolic murmur, often radiating toward the axilla | Determine primary valve disease versus ventricular/atrial functional regurgitation. Acute severe regurgitation may cause pulmonary edema with little prior warning. |
| Aortic regurgitation: early diastolic decrescendo murmur | Evaluate aortic/valve disease and ventricular size/function; acute severe disease can be unstable without chronic peripheral signs. |
| Mitral stenosis: apical diastolic findings | Assess obstruction, pulmonary pressures, AF, and embolic risk; define rheumatic versus other anatomy. |
| Tricuspid regurgitation: systolic murmur with systemic venous congestion | Assess right-heart/valve disease and pulmonary or left-heart contributors rather than assuming all edema is isolated left-HF. |

Mitral valve prolapse may produce a midsystolic click and late systolic apical murmur. Confirm suspected clinically significant disease by echo and assess regurgitation; do not assign every palpitation to prolapse or give routine dental prophylaxis for uncomplicated prolapse. [AHA mitral-prolapse guidance](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-valve-problems-and-disease/heart-valve-problems-and-causes/problem-mitral-valve-prolapse)

For **aortic stenosis**, high-gradient severe disease generally has peak velocity at least 4 m/s or mean gradient at least 40 mm Hg, with valve area usually at most 1.0 cm2. Low-flow disease can be severe below those gradients. Symptoms or LVEF below 50% prompt intervention assessment; do not order routine exercise testing for symptomatic severe AS. In stable asymptomatic AS with normal LV function, echo is usually repeated every 3-5 years if mild, 1-2 years if moderate, and 6-12 months if severe, earlier with clinical change. For **severe primary MR**, symptoms, LVEF at most 60%, or LV end-systolic diameter at least 40 mm warrant valve-team assessment: an apparently normal EF can already represent dysfunction in a regurgitant ventricle. [ACC/AHA lesion-specific assessment and surveillance](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/8ba3a767f9024c63863c5bfc86ff17d5.ashx)

A **mechanical valve requires vitamin K antagonist anticoagulation**, not substitution with a direct oral anticoagulant. Target intensity depends on the valve and associated risks. A bioprosthesis has a different early and long-term plan; obtain the procedure details. Coordinate interruptions for procedures with the treating team rather than applying a generic stop-and-bridge rule. Pregnancy with a mechanical valve requires specialist planning. [ACC/AHA prosthetic-valve framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2020-acc-aha-guideline-for-the-management-of-patients-with-valvular-heart-disease)

### Fever with valve findings may represent endocarditis

Consider infective endocarditis with otherwise unexplained fever, bacteremia, a new or changing murmur, embolic findings, immune manifestations, or new HF, especially with prosthetic material, prior endocarditis, injection-drug exposure, or an intracardiac device. Classic skin findings may be absent. Diagnosis integrates the presentation, cultures, and imaging, with or without a murmur.

Obtain appropriate blood-culture sets before antibiotics when feasible; avoid a casual outpatient antibiotic course that suppresses cultures without treating the infection adequately. Sepsis or instability must not wait for a prolonged outpatient diagnostic sequence. Echocardiography is central, and transesophageal imaging may be needed when suspicion remains high or prosthetic/device disease is present despite an unrevealing transthoracic study. Acute valve failure, conduction changes, uncontrolled infection, or embolic complications require coordinated hospital/specialist care.

Prevention emphasizes good oral health and selective dental prophylaxis, not antibiotics for every murmur. High-risk groups include prosthetic valves/valve-repair material, prior endocarditis, specified congenital lesions, and cardiac transplantation with qualifying valve disease. The dental procedure must also meet the relevant criteria. Native uncomplicated valve disease or routine gastrointestinal/genitourinary procedures do not automatically require prophylaxis. [AHA endocarditis guidance](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/infective-endocarditis); [AHA prophylaxis criteria](https://www.heart.org/-/media/files/health-topics/infective-endocarditis/infective-endocarditis-wallet-card.pdf)

Qualifying dental procedures manipulate gingiva, the tooth's periapical region, or perforate oral mucosa. For an eligible adult able to take oral medicine without penicillin allergy, use **amoxicillin 2 g once, 30-60 minutes beforehand**. Clindamycin is no longer recommended; select a listed alternative for allergy, avoiding cephalosporins with a history of penicillin-associated anaphylaxis, angioedema, or urticaria. Unrepaired cyanotic congenital disease or residual shunt/regurgitation next to prosthetic repair material qualifies, not every repaired childhood defect. [AHA prophylaxis regimen and exclusions](https://www.heart.org/-/media/files/health-topics/infective-endocarditis/infective-endocarditis-wallet-card.pdf)
