# Medivale Family Medicine

## 02A. Chest Pain and Ischemic Disease

### Decide whether the patient can safely remain in the office

First identify plausible dangerous causes and whether the office can evaluate them safely. Assess appearance, airway and breathing, pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, mental status, and ongoing symptoms while obtaining a focused history. Collapse, shock, severe respiratory distress, a new neurologic deficit, or persistent ischemic symptoms requires emergency action. A patient who looks comfortable between episodes may still have an acute coronary syndrome (ACS).

Ask about quality, location, radiation, onset, duration, exertional threshold, progression, and differences from usual symptoms. New rest symptoms or symptoms occurring with progressively less activity are concerning even when previous episodes were brief. Do not require a particular pain duration before acting.

Pressure, heaviness, breathlessness, upper abdominal discomfort, nausea, and unusual exercise intolerance can reflect myocardial ischemia. Do not dismiss symptoms because they are not described as pain or because the person is young, female, or without known coronary disease. Ask about prior infarction or revascularization, diabetes, tobacco, family history, stimulant exposure, pregnancy/postpartum status, clotting risks, and current medicines. These alter probability; no single risk factor establishes the diagnosis.

| Finding or context | Important possibility | Immediate implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exertional or resting pressure with diaphoresis, breathlessness, or radiation | Coronary ischemia | Evaluate urgently when new, worsening, or ongoing. |
| Abrupt severe chest/back pain, syncope, pulse difference, or neurologic findings | Acute aortic syndrome | Emergency assessment; avoid reflex antithrombotic treatment when dissection is a serious concern. |
| Pleuritic discomfort with unexplained dyspnea, hypoxemia, or venous thrombosis risks | Pulmonary embolism | Use an appropriate emergency diagnostic pathway, not an isolated symptom or office D-dimer. |
| Sudden unilateral pain with respiratory distress and reduced breath sounds | Pneumothorax, including tension physiology | Stabilization and emergency care take priority over routine outpatient imaging. |
| Severe pain following forceful vomiting or instrumentation | Esophageal rupture | Emergency evaluation even when the initial complaint sounds gastrointestinal. |

Examine for lung asymmetry, murmurs, a friction rub, congestion, impaired perfusion, chest-wall tenderness, and abdominal findings. Reflux, chest-wall pain, pneumonia, pericardial disease, and panic are common alternatives, but tenderness or improvement after an antacid does not independently exclude ACS. Nitroglycerin response is also not a diagnostic test. Suspected ACS or another life-threatening cause warrants urgent emergency-department transport, preferably by emergency medical services (EMS), without waiting for office laboratory results. [AHA/ACC chest-pain recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/-/media/PHD-Files-2/Science-News/2/2021/2021-Chest-Pain-Guideline-Slide-Set-PDF-102821.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 46-year-old develops burning upper abdominal discomfort while carrying boxes. An antacid helps briefly, but exertion brings recurrent discomfort and sweating. Arrange emergency evaluation and monitoring for possible ACS despite the antacid response.

### Use ECG and troponin for the questions they can answer

An ECG detects electrical evidence of ischemia, arrhythmia, and alternative disease, not coronary patency. Acquire and review it within **10 minutes of arrival** for acute chest pain when feasible, without delaying transfer. Compare with prior tracings if available. A normal or nonspecific initial tracing does not exclude ACS. Persistent or recurrent symptoms may require serial tracings, additional leads, and expert interpretation in the receiving setting. New conduction abnormalities require clinical interpretation, not automatic assignment to a benign baseline variant or a single infarction category.

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin detects myocardial injury. Interpret the value using the local assay, its appropriate upper reference limit, the time from symptom onset, and the change on serial testing. An early result below the cutoff can precede detectable injury. Validated accelerated pathways may rule out infarction in selected patients, sometimes using a single very low value under defined conditions; this is different from treating any single negative troponin as a universal rule-out. Do not delay EMS transfer to obtain an outpatient sample.

**Myocardial injury is not synonymous with myocardial infarction.** The 2026 universal definition identifies acute injury through a troponin rise/fall with at least one value above the sex-specific 99th-percentile reference limit. Infarction also requires evidence of ischemia. Its current clinical categories are primary, secondary, and procedure-related infarction. Primary infarction includes acute coronary causes such as atherothrombosis, dissection, embolism, and spasm. Secondary infarction follows another acute illness with supply-demand imbalance; objective coronary or cardiac imaging findings help confirm it when feasible and appropriate. Do not label every troponin elevation during sepsis, anemia, tachycardia, or kidney disease as infarction. [2026 universal definition](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.07.025)

Integrate symptoms, serial biomarkers, ECG, and imaging before selecting antithrombotic therapy. Chronic kidney disease can accompany persistently elevated troponin, but it does not protect against a new infarction. A meaningful acute change or concerning presentation still requires evaluation. Chest radiography can identify some alternative causes but cannot rule out coronary ischemia, pulmonary embolism, or aortic dissection by itself.

### Recognize pericardial and myocardial inflammation

Pericarditis can cause pleuritic, positional pain, a friction rub, widespread ST elevation or PR depression, and a new effusion. Not every patient has every finding, and a pain description or diffuse ECG pattern alone must not bypass ACS assessment. Obtain ECG, troponin, inflammatory markers, and echocardiography as indicated. Myocardial involvement becomes important with troponin elevation, ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmia, or HF; cardiac MRI may help clarify it.

Hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, tachycardia, or worsening dyspnea with an effusion raises concern for tamponade and requires emergency assessment; the complete classic examination triad need not be present. Fever, a large effusion, immunosuppression, anticoagulation, myocardial involvement, or failure to improve makes routine low-risk outpatient care inappropriate. For confirmed uncomplicated inflammatory pericarditis, aspirin or an NSAID with gastroprotection plus colchicine is usual first-line therapy when appropriate. Kidney function, drug interactions, bleeding risk, pregnancy, and the cause alter selection. Arrange early review and limit strenuous activity until clinical remission and clearance; recurrent or complicated disease needs specialist care. [ESC 2025 myocarditis/pericarditis guideline](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/40/3952/8234483)

### Stable symptoms need a deliberate diagnostic strategy

Stable angina produces reproducible exertional symptoms from myocardial oxygen supply-demand imbalance, relieved by rest at a consistent threshold. A falling threshold, longer episodes, or new symptoms at rest changes the safety assessment. Assess limitations in work, caregiving, and daily activity.

After excluding an acute presentation, estimate coronary-disease likelihood to guide testing. Some low-risk patients can defer cardiac testing. For others, **coronary CT angiography** assesses coronary anatomy, while **stress testing** evaluates the response to demand or pharmacologic stress. The choice depends on the question, known coronary disease, ability to exercise, resting ECG, kidney function, contrast considerations, local expertise, and likely downstream decisions. An uninterpretable resting tracing or inability to exercise adequately limits a plain treadmill ECG. Stress imaging can address those limitations; CT is not automatically preferable in every patient. [Chest-pain testing framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/-/media/PHD-Files-2/Science-News/2/2021/2021-Chest-Pain-Guideline-Slide-Set-PDF-102821.pdf)

Microvascular dysfunction and vasospasm can cause ischemic symptoms without flow-limiting epicardial stenosis; nonobstructive plaque still matters for prevention. Reassess persistent symptoms for coronary and noncardiac causes, referring when needed. Plaque alone does not establish the cause of symptoms.

### Separate symptom relief from prevention of future events

Chronic coronary care addresses symptoms and long-term risk. Tobacco cessation, a sustainable eating pattern, physical activity appropriate to stability, and management of blood pressure, lipids, diabetes, and weight are complementary to medication. Address medication access, work schedules, mood, and follow-up barriers.

Beta blockers or calcium-channel blockers are first-line antianginal options in the current chronic-coronary framework. Selection depends on heart rate, blood pressure, ventricular function, conduction disease, and other conditions. Additional antianginal therapy may be needed. Nitrates can relieve symptoms but should not be combined with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors or riociguat because profound hypotension can occur. Ask specifically about erectile-dysfunction and pulmonary-hypertension medicines. Verapamil or diltiazem can worsen systolic heart failure or bradycardia in susceptible patients. Do not combine rate-slowing medicines casually. [Chronic coronary disease guideline](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2023.04.003); [current nitroglycerin interaction warnings](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5265648f-3062-48a2-a4e3-fea1207807a8)

Give a clear rescue plan: new, severe, persistent, or changing symptoms require emergency assessment; repeated home treatment must not postpone calling EMS. Revascularization is considered for appropriate anatomy, prognostic indications, or symptoms that remain unacceptable despite medical treatment. A coronary procedure does not eliminate systemic atherosclerosis or the need for preventive care. Routine repeat ischemic or anatomic testing in an unchanged, asymptomatic patient is not automatically useful. [AHA chronic-coronary guidance](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2023-guideline-for-the-management-of-patients-with-chronic-coronary-disease/top-things-to-know)

For established coronary disease without an oral-anticoagulation indication, low-dose aspirin, usually **81 mg daily**, is a standard long-term antiplatelet option; clopidogrel may be selected instead, including for intolerance. Recent ACS or PCI has its own time-limited combination plan. Do not add aspirin automatically to anticoagulation for AF in stable remote coronary disease. [2026 ACC antiplatelet statement](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.05.037)

### Recognize ACS and make the transfer useful

ACS includes ST-elevation infarction, non-ST-elevation infarction, and unstable angina. An urgent reperfusion decision can precede biomarker confirmation when the clinical and ECG findings indicate an occluded artery. A patient with a nondiagnostic tracing may still need urgent invasive assessment because of ongoing ischemia, shock, acute heart failure, or dangerous arrhythmia. Do not schedule an outpatient stress test for an unstable presentation.

Activate EMS, keep the patient under observation, obtain available monitoring and IV access without delaying departure, and communicate symptom onset, ECG findings, vital signs, allergies, bleeding history, anticoagulants, and medicines already given. For suspected ACS without a contraindication, the current initial aspirin load is **162-325 mg**, preferably nonenteric-coated and chewed. Important exceptions include serious aspirin hypersensitivity, active major bleeding, or a competing diagnosis in which antithrombotic treatment could be dangerous. Supplemental oxygen is indicated for hypoxemia; it is not a routine treatment for a normally oxygenated patient. Nitrates require assessment for hypotension, preload-dependent states, and relevant drug interactions. Morphine is not a compulsory first step. [2025 ACS guideline, medical therapies](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.11.009)

The receiving team selects anticoagulation, additional antiplatelet therapy, and reperfusion using ischemic risk, bleeding risk, timing, and available facilities. Primary PCI is generally the preferred reperfusion approach for STEMI when achievable promptly. Fibrinolysis is a selected STEMI strategy when timely PCI cannot be achieved and contraindications have been excluded; it is **not** treatment for NSTEMI or unstable angina. These decisions belong to a coordinated emergency pathway, not a prolonged office workup.

### After infarction, reconcile the plan rather than renew a list

At the first follow-up, obtain the discharge summary, procedure report, ventricular-function assessment, and intended antithrombotic duration. Ask about recurrent discomfort, breathlessness, orthopnea, edema, syncope, palpitations, bleeding, mood, and practical access to medication. Examine pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, and congestion. Recurrent ischemic symptoms or instability require urgent reassessment rather than routine titration.

After ACS, aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor for **at least 12 months is the default when bleeding risk is not high**. Shorter or modified regimens can be appropriate, especially with bleeding risk or a separate anticoagulation indication. Neither dual antiplatelet nor triple antithrombotic therapy is routinely indefinite. Do not independently interrupt a recent stent regimen for an elective procedure; coordinate with the treating team. Include cardiac rehabilitation and lipid reassessment after treatment changes. [AHA ACS follow-up recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-guideline-for-the-management-of-patients-with-acute-coronary-syndromes/top-things-to-know)

High-intensity or maximally tolerated statin therapy is foundational after an atherosclerotic coronary event, with additional lipid lowering when indicated; goals and escalation are in 02C. ACE inhibitors/ARBs and HF therapy depend on ventricular function and associated conditions. Reassess long-term beta-blocker use when MI was more than a year ago, LVEF is above 50%, and there is no other indication such as angina, arrhythmia, or uncontrolled hypertension. This is not an instruction to stop indicated HF treatment or withdraw a beta blocker abruptly. [Chronic-coronary follow-up](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2023-guideline-for-the-management-of-patients-with-chronic-coronary-disease/top-things-to-know)

**Clinical Case.** Two weeks after PCI, a patient has not started the P2Y12 inhibitor because of cost. Contact the treating team and resolve access immediately; establish time without therapy and assess recurrent symptoms.

### Leg symptoms can reveal the same systemic disease

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) limits activity, threatens tissue, and signals increased cardiovascular risk. Ask about reproducible calf, thigh, or buttock discomfort with walking and relief with rest, as well as reduced walking undertaken to avoid symptoms. Neurogenic claudication is more related to spinal posture and may improve with flexion; arthritis, neuropathy, and venous disease can coexist. Inspect both feet, footwear, skin, wounds, and pulses rather than relying only on reported pain.

A resting **ankle-brachial index (ABI) of 0.90 or less is abnormal**; 0.91-0.99 is borderline, 1.00-1.40 is normal, and above 1.40 suggests noncompressible arteries. Suspected PAD with a normal/borderline resting result can require exercise ABI; a noncompressible result calls for toe pressure/toe-brachial testing rather than reassurance. Calcified arteries, particularly with diabetes or kidney disease, can make an apparently high ankle pressure misleading. [AHA PAD diagnostic recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/-/media/PHD-Files-2/Science-News/2/2024/2024-PAD-guideline-slide-set.pdf)

Chronic symptomatic PAD requires tobacco treatment, lipid lowering, appropriate antithrombotic therapy, blood-pressure and diabetes care, foot protection, and structured exercise. Single antiplatelet therapy is recommended; selected patients benefit from low-dose rivaroxaban plus low-dose aspirin when bleeding risk permits. Full-dose anticoagulation is not a substitute without another indication. Cilostazol may improve claudication but must not be used in heart failure. Revascularization is considered when function remains limited despite appropriate treatment. [ACC PAD treatment summary](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2024/05/09/15/00/2024-guideline-for-lower-extremity-pad)

Persistent ischemic rest pain, a nonhealing ischemic wound, or gangrene suggests **chronic limb-threatening ischemia** and needs prompt vascular assessment. A suddenly painful, cold, pale, numb, weak, or pulseless limb suggests **acute limb ischemia**, an emergency requiring immediate specialist-directed care. Exercise alone is inappropriate for either presentation; delay threatens tissue viability.
