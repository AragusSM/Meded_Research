# Medivale Family Medicine

## 02E. Palpitations and Syncope

AF decisions below use the US ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS framework. Emergency rhythm care follows AHA resuscitation guidance.

### Start with the event and its consequences

Palpitations are awareness of racing, fluttering, pounding, pauses, or premature beats, not a specific arrhythmia. Sinus acceleration from fever, anemia, pain, pregnancy, dehydration, or thyroid excess can feel similar to an intrinsic rhythm disorder. Anxiety can cause symptoms or coexist with arrhythmia.

Reconstruct activity, posture, onset, duration, regularity, termination, and associated chest discomfort, breathlessness, dizziness, or loss of consciousness. Sudden onset and termination of a rapid regular rhythm suggest reentry, whereas gradual acceleration with a recognizable physiologic stressor suggests sinus tachycardia. Confirm the suspected mechanism. Ask about exertional events, prior heart disease, and relatives with unexplained sudden death, drowning, seizures, or inherited cardiomyopathy.

Review prescribed drugs, missed doses, inhaled beta agonists, decongestants, supplements, stimulants, nicotine, alcohol, and other substances without implying blame. Combining QT-prolonging drugs with hypokalemia creates a different risk from uncomplicated awareness of occasional ectopic beats. Look for bleeding, infection, thyroid symptoms, sleep-disordered breathing, and volume depletion when the history supports them. Contributors may coexist.

Assess vital signs, perfusion, oxygenation, pulse regularity, murmurs, congestion, and orthostatic symptoms. Obtain a 12-lead ECG; a normal recording between events does not exclude an intermittent arrhythmia. Evaluate PR and QRS intervals, QT, preexcitation, conduction block, ischemic changes, and atrial or ventricular rhythm. Target laboratory testing to the presentation, commonly including CBC, electrolytes, kidney function, and thyroid testing when relevant. Echocardiography is appropriate when structural disease is suspected, not automatically for every isolated palpitation.

**Escalate immediately** for ongoing palpitations accompanied by hypotension, altered mentation, ischemic symptoms, acute HF, or syncope with concerning features. Preserve wearable recordings when possible without delaying monitored resuscitation. [2025 adult advanced life support](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)

### Recognize AF and flutter without reducing care to the pulse

AF produces disorganized atrial activation and usually an irregular ventricular rhythm without consistent discrete P waves. Flutter is an organized atrial circuit; fixed AV conduction can produce a regular ventricular rhythm. A rate near 150/min may suggest flutter with 2:1 conduction, but rate alone cannot identify the mechanism. Variable conduction, medications, pacing, and coexisting disease can obscure classic findings. Confirm the rhythm by clinician review of an ECG-quality tracing; an optical pulse alert is a reason to evaluate, not a stand-alone AF diagnosis.

Management combines symptom relief, circulatory support, indicated thromboembolic prevention, and treatment of contributing disease. Evaluate hypertension, HF, valve disease, thyroid disease, alcohol exposure, obesity, and possible sleep apnea. Exercise and weight management, tobacco cessation, and reducing alcohol can improve the overall AF care plan. [AHA treatment overview](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/atrial-fibrillation/treatment-and-prevention-of-atrial-fibrillation)

Stroke prevention depends on estimated risk, not simply whether AF is intermittent or persistent. The US framework recommends anticoagulation at an estimated annual thromboembolic risk of at least 2%, commonly represented by CHA2DS2-VASc of at least 2 in men or 3 in women. At an intermediate estimate, corresponding commonly to 1 in men or 2 in women, anticoagulation is reasonable after individualized discussion. Female sex alone does not establish an indication. Reassess risk as age and comorbidity change. [Current US risk interpretation](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Articles/2026/03/01/01/Focus-on-EP)

CHA2DS2-VASc assigns 1 point each for HF, hypertension, diabetes, vascular disease, age 65-74, and female sex, and 2 each for age at least 75 and prior stroke/TIA/systemic embolism. The age categories are alternatives, not cumulative. For example, a 69-year-old man with hypertension and diabetes has 3 points: one for age and one for each condition. At borderline risk, include additional clinical factors and patient preferences.

DOACs are generally preferred when an oral anticoagulant is appropriate, but mechanical valves and moderate-or-greater mitral stenosis require a different pathway, ordinarily warfarin. Aspirin, alone or with clopidogrel, is not a substitute for indicated anticoagulation. A bleeding score should identify modifiable hazards and inform discussion, not independently deny treatment. Confirm device-detected atrial high-rate episodes and assess duration and risk separately from clinical AF. [ACC AF key perspectives](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2023/11/27/19/46/2023-acc-guideline-for-af-gl-af)

For device-detected episodes **without a prior clinical AF diagnosis**, anticoagulation is reasonable after shared assessment for an episode at least 24 hours with CHA2DS2-VASc at least 2; it may be reasonable for 5 minutes to below 24 hours with a score at least 3. An episode below 5 minutes alone is not an indication. These conditional thresholds require clinician confirmation, not automatic treatment from a pulse alert. In clinical AF with stable coronary disease beyond one year after revascularization and no previous stent thrombosis, anticoagulant monotherapy is generally preferred to adding an antiplatelet. [US device-event and stable-coronary recommendations](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2023/11/27/19/46/2023-acc-guideline-for-af-gl-af)

### Separate rate control, rhythm control, and cardioversion safety

Rate control slows ventricular conduction while AF may continue. Rhythm control attempts to restore and maintain sinus rhythm through cardioversion, antiarrhythmic therapy, or catheter ablation. Rate control can relieve symptoms while arranging early rhythm treatment; failure of all rate-control drugs is not a prerequisite. Earlier rhythm treatment can be important for symptomatic patients and selected people with recently diagnosed AF or HF. [AHA treatment selection](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/atrial-fibrillation/treatment-and-prevention-of-atrial-fibrillation/afib-treatment-options)

For stable AF, beta blockers or nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers are common rate-control options, selected according to ventricular function, pressure, and other illness. Diltiazem and verapamil are unsuitable in significant LV systolic dysfunction; excessive AV nodal blockade can cause hypotension or bradycardia. Digoxin has a selected role, with attention to kidney function and interactions. A rate that is acceptable at rest may still be excessive during activity. Individualize the pulse target to symptoms, exertional response, and ventricular function.

Antiarrhythmic selection depends on structural disease, ischemia, QT, kidney function, and drug interactions. Flecainide and propafenone are not generic choices for patients with prior MI or significant structural heart disease. Amiodarone has important thyroid, pulmonary, hepatic, and interaction burdens. Refer for a patient-specific rhythm plan; catheter ablation can be appropriate earlier in selected patients, including some with HF. A pacemaker treats selected slow rhythms; it does not itself prevent AF-related embolic stroke.

Elective cardioversion requires a separate embolic-safety plan. Under the US AF guideline, AF lasting at least 48 hours or of uncertain duration generally requires at least 3 weeks of uninterrupted therapeutic anticoagulation or imaging to exclude intracardiac thrombus before planned conversion. Establish therapeutic anticoagulation before conversion and continue for at least 4 weeks afterward. Shorter reported duration does not guarantee absence of risk. Hemodynamic instability demands urgent cardioversion without waiting weeks; address anticoagulation as soon as feasible. [US cardioversion recommendations](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2023.08.017)

After the peri-cardioversion period, continued stroke prevention follows the underlying risk assessment, not merely a normal office ECG. Ask specifically about missed anticoagulant doses before a scheduled procedure. If imaging shows atrial thrombus, defer elective conversion and coordinate treatment and reassessment. Electrical conversion neither immediately restores atrial mechanical function nor eliminates recurrence risk. [AHA cardioversion explanation](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/prevention--treatment-of-arrhythmia/cardioversion)

### Distinguish common SVT from dangerous fast and slow rhythms

For a stable, regular narrow-complex reentrant tachycardia, vagal maneuvers are a usual initial intervention; a modified Valsalva may improve success. Adenosine can terminate AV-node-dependent reentry in a monitored setting with resuscitation capability. It can reveal atrial activity without terminating flutter or another atrial rhythm. Do not mistake sinus tachycardia from hemorrhage or infection for a circuit that should be interrupted. Recurrent symptomatic SVT warrants discussion of electrophysiology referral and potentially curative ablation. [SVT guideline overview](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/guideline-for-the-management-of-adult-patients-with-supraventricular-tachycardia/top-things-to-know)

Review asthma/bronchospasm and conduction disease before adenosine; AHA guidance identifies asthma as a contraindication because severe bronchospasm can occur. Hypotension or poor perfusion changes the priority to urgent electrical treatment, not repeated medication trials. [AHA 2025 narrow-complex tachycardia recommendations](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)

| Rhythm or presentation | Immediate distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Regular narrow tachycardia, stable | Identify sinus versus reentrant rhythm; monitored vagal/adenosine pathway when appropriate. |
| Wide-complex tachycardia | Treat an uncertain rhythm as potentially ventricular; avoid empiric diltiazem or verapamil. |
| Irregular wide tachycardia | Consider preexcited AF or polymorphic ventricular rhythm; do not give adenosine empirically. |
| Unstable tachycardia with a pulse | Urgent electrical treatment; synchronized cardioversion for an organized rhythm that can be synchronized. |
| VF, pulseless VT, or sustained polymorphic VT | Defibrillation pathway; initiate CPR when pulseless. |

Synchronized cardioversion avoids shocking during the vulnerable phase of an organized cardiac cycle. Polymorphic VT cannot be reliably synchronized. Selected stable regular monomorphic wide rhythms permit expert monitored drug assessment; adenosine is not suitable for every wide rhythm. [2025 emergency rhythm recommendations](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)

Preexcited AF can conduct rapidly through an accessory pathway. AV nodal blockers, including adenosine, beta blockers, diltiazem/verapamil, digoxin, and amiodarone, are contraindicated in this setting because deterioration or VF may follow. Obtain emergency rhythm expertise. [US preexcitation recommendations](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2023.08.017)

For bradycardia, ask whether the slow rhythm causes impaired perfusion and whether a reversible cause is present, such as medication effect, ischemia, electrolyte disturbance, or hypothermia. Symptomatic high-grade block needs urgent monitoring and possible temporary pacing. Acquired Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block without a reversible or physiologic cause can warrant permanent pacing even without reported symptoms. Sinus-node dysfunction instead requires symptom-rhythm correlation; no isolated minimum heart rate establishes a universal pacemaker indication. [Bradycardia and conduction guidance](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2018-guideline-for-the-evaluation-and-management-of-bradycardia-and-cardiac-conduction-delay/top-things-to-know)

Inherited electrical disease deserves particular attention when symptoms occur with exercise, startle, fever, or a family history of sudden death. Long-QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, and catecholaminergic polymorphic VT have different ECG patterns and triggers; a normal resting tracing does not exclude every inherited disorder. A normal echocardiogram does not remove the need for specialist assessment. A prolonged QT also requires review of drugs, electrolytes, and the correction method, not reliance on a machine label alone. [Ventricular arrhythmia guidance](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2017.10.053)

For suspected acquired QT-related ventricular arrhythmia, stop contributing drugs and urgently correct reversible disturbances in a monitored setting. Do not add another QT-prolonging agent reflexively. Individualize channelopathy treatment, family screening, and sports advice to the confirmed syndrome.

**Torsades de pointes** is polymorphic VT associated with prolonged QT, not every polymorphic rhythm. Sustained polymorphic VT requires immediate unsynchronized shock; IV magnesium may suppress recurrent torsades while potassium and triggers are addressed. Magnesium is not routine treatment for polymorphic VT with a normal QT, where ischemia is an important cause. [AHA 2025 polymorphic-VT recommendations](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)

Structural disease also matters in a young person with exertional syncope or a family history of sudden death. A systolic murmur accentuated by standing or Valsalva raises concern for dynamic outflow obstruction such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Obtain echocardiography and specialist assessment rather than clearing strenuous activity from a normal resting pulse. Confirmed HCM needs family evaluation and individualized exercise advice; nonvasodilating beta blockers have a therapeutic role in symptomatic obstruction, not a blanket contraindication. [AHA/ACC 2024 HCM guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001250)

### Match monitoring to symptom frequency

Choose monitoring to establish symptom-rhythm correlation. A short Holter suits daily events. A longer continuous patch, external loop recorder, or event system suits less frequent symptoms; device capabilities and the patient's ability to trigger a recording matter. Rare, recurrent, unexplained syncope may require an implantable monitor after appropriate evaluation. High-risk syncope may need inpatient monitoring instead of waiting for an outpatient event. [Ambulatory ECG consensus](https://www.hrsonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2017-ISHNE-HRS-Ambulatory-ECG.pdf)

Three episodes in six weeks may escape a 24-hour Holter. A symptom-free recording does not exclude arrhythmia; the usual symptom captured during sinus rhythm provides stronger evidence against an arrhythmic cause for that event. Confirm that the recorded event was representative.

Interpret incidental ectopy with burden, morphology, symptoms, and structural assessment. Occasional isolated premature beats in a person without concerning features often require reassurance and trigger management. Frequent ventricular ectopy, exertional symptoms, reduced EF, or a family history of sudden death changes the evaluation. Assign result review, follow-up, and escalation instructions.

### Evaluate syncope by mechanism and risk

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness from cerebral hypoperfusion, with spontaneous recovery. Clarify whether consciousness was truly lost and obtain a witness account when possible. Ask about posture, exertion, prodrome, palpitations before collapse, duration, color change, movements, injury, and recovery. Brief jerks can occur during syncope; prolonged confusion or other seizure features need a different assessment. Persistent neurologic deficit is not explained by uncomplicated fainting.

| Mechanism | Useful clinical pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Reflex/vasovagal | Pain, emotion, heat, or prolonged standing; warmth, nausea, sweating, and visual dimming may precede collapse. |
| Orthostatic | Symptoms after standing, medication changes, volume loss, meals, or autonomic dysfunction. |
| Cardiac | Exertional or supine collapse, sudden events with little warning, preceding palpitations, abnormal ECG, or known structural disease. |

Initial evaluation centers on history, examination, ECG, and targeted testing. Hospital assessment is appropriate when a serious condition is identified or suspected. Without relevant findings, routine broad laboratory panels, brain imaging, EEG, and carotid studies are unhelpful. Obtain echo when structural disease is suspected and use specialist-directed exercise or electrophysiology testing selectively. A risk score can support, but not replace, clinical disposition. [Syncope guidance](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2017-acc-aha-hrs-guideline-for-the-evaluation-and-management-of-patients-with-syncope/top-things-to-know)

Measure orthostatic pressure and pulse with attention to technique and symptoms, ideally after supine rest and again during standing. A sustained fall of at least 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic within 3 minutes meets the usual OH definition. A single normal measurement does not exclude intermittent or delayed symptoms. Review volume depletion, anemia, autonomic disease, and medication contributors. Do not reflexively abandon all beneficial hypertension treatment; adjust the responsible factors and the regimen. [AHA orthostatic statement](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/orthostatic-hypotension-in-adults-with-hypertension/top-things-to-know)

For a convincing low-risk vasovagal presentation, explain the mechanism, recognize warning symptoms, and discuss lying down promptly and appropriate counterpressure maneuvers. Hydration and salt advice must respect HF, kidney disease, and hypertension. Recurrent injury, uncertain diagnosis, absent warning, or persistent symptoms warrants further evaluation. Until dangerous causes are addressed, advise against hazardous driving, heights, swimming alone, or strenuous activity as relevant; driving restrictions depend on the diagnosis and jurisdiction. Specify which recurrences require emergency reassessment.
