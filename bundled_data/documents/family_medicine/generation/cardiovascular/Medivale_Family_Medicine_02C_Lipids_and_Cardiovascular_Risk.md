# Medivale Family Medicine

## 02C. Lipids and Cardiovascular Risk

Treatment below follows the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia framework. USPSTF preventive recommendations are identified separately.

### Interpret the particles, the patient, and the purpose of testing

A lipid profile assesses atherogenic exposure, inherited disorders, secondary disease, and treatment response, not coronary obstruction. LDL cholesterol is the cholesterol carried in LDL particles; non-HDL cholesterol, calculated as total cholesterol minus HDL cholesterol, includes cholesterol in the broader collection of atherogenic particles. Apolipoprotein B reflects their particle burden and can clarify discordance, particularly when triglycerides are elevated. A high HDL value does not cancel the risk associated with markedly elevated LDL.

Atherosclerotic myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, or symptomatic PAD requires **secondary prevention**. Diabetes or high LDL without established ASCVD remains **primary prevention**, although treatment may be strongly indicated. Heart failure alone is not proof of an atherosclerotic cause. This distinction determines calculator eligibility, intensity, and goals.

Review previous results, current therapy, the fasting state, and recent illness. A nonfasting profile can be useful for routine assessment, but a fasting assessment is important when substantial triglyceride elevation, pancreatitis risk, or a suspected inherited disorder requires clarification. High triglycerides can impair calculated-LDL accuracy; use a validated method and consider non-HDL cholesterol or apoB. [2026 dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)

Look for secondary causes before assuming a primary inherited disorder. Hypothyroidism, poorly controlled diabetes, nephrotic protein loss, kidney dysfunction, cholestatic liver disease, alcohol, and some medicines can alter the profile. Ask about weight change, dietary patterns, pregnancy, glucocorticoids, retinoids, antipsychotics, antiretrovirals, and other relevant exposures. Select thyroid, glucose, kidney, urine-protein, and liver testing from this context. Finding a reversible contributor does not erase a separate ASCVD treatment indication.

**Clinical Case.** A 43-year-old has newly elevated LDL, constipation, cold intolerance, and fatigue after a previously lower profile. Assess thyroid function before diagnosing familial hypercholesterolemia; after correcting a secondary cause, reassess lipids and the prevention indication.

### Use the current risk model in its intended population

For ordinary risk-based primary prevention, the 2026 ACC/AHA framework uses **PREVENT-ASCVD**, not the total-CVD estimate used for blood-pressure treatment in 02B. Eligible risk-based assessment generally includes adults aged 30-79 with LDL 70-189 mg/dL and no known ASCVD or subclinical atherosclerosis. Thirty-year estimates apply to ages 30-59. Established ASCVD, severe LDL elevation, and specific high-risk diseases follow their own pathways; a low calculated short-term risk should not be used to negate those indications.

| Ten-year PREVENT-ASCVD estimate | Practical treatment discussion |
| --- | --- |
| Below 3% | Emphasize health behaviors; selected younger adults still merit treatment for high cumulative exposure or high thirty-year risk. |
| 3% to below 5% | Moderate-intensity statin therapy is reasonable after individualized discussion. |
| 5% to below 10% | At least moderate-intensity statin therapy is recommended; greater intensity may fit the higher end of this range. |
| At least 10% | High-intensity statin therapy is recommended. |

For selected adults aged 30-59 with low ten-year risk, LDL 160-189 mg/dL or a thirty-year risk of at least 10% can support a moderate-intensity statin. Consider cumulative exposure alongside short-term risk. [AHA PREVENT-ASCVD implementation resource](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/731B2098BB23427F9EA8EDDF98E08145.ashx)

Adults aged 40-75 with diabetes, stage 3 or 4 CKD, or HIV have a current recommendation for LDL-lowering therapy independent of a high calculator result or a particular elevated LDL value. Select intensity from the disease-specific risk profile. Above age 75, consider potential benefit, competing illness, frailty, treatment burden, and preferences rather than treating a birthday as an automatic instruction to start or stop therapy.

For primary prevention beyond age 75, initiating a moderate-intensity statin may be reasonable after discussion when estimated life expectancy is at least 2.5 years. This is a benefit-horizon consideration, not a precise survival prediction; see 15B for functional and medication-burden assessment. [AHA geriatric lipid resource](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/-/media/2EE6BBFF89B244EF9A3FCF3C784EC0A3.pdf)

Personalize the estimate with family history, inflammatory disease, reproductive history, kidney/metabolic status, and atherogenic biomarkers. Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is largely inherited and should be measured at least once. Levels of at least 125 nmol/L or 50 mg/dL are risk-enhancing. A high result supports intensive management of modifiable risks, although lifestyle changes do not reliably normalize Lp(a). Mass and particle-concentration units have no universal conversion factor. Selective apoB measurement can reveal residual particle-related risk even when LDL appears satisfactory. [AHA disease-specific and biomarker recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-on-the-management-of-dyslipidemia/top-things-to-know)

Selective apoB testing is particularly useful with triglycerides **at least 150 mg/dL**, diabetes, or low achieved LDL despite apparent LDL/non-HDL goal attainment. These settings can conceal an excess particle burden. [Corrected guideline recommendation](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001457)

For uncertain prevention decisions, **coronary artery calcium (CAC)** can refine selected patients' risk; it does not evaluate acute chest pain. A score of zero does not exclude noncalcified plaque, cancel every disease-specific treatment indication, or make tobacco exposure safe. A positive result may change treatment intensity and goals. Test when the result could change management, without delaying clearly indicated treatment. [Current ACC/AHA risk-assessment framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-on-the-management-of-dyslipidemia)

In the selective CAC pathway for men at least 40 and women at least 45, **CAC zero** can support deferral at borderline/intermediate risk when the patient prefers it and has no major exception such as FH/severe hypercholesterolemia, diabetes after age 40, current smoking, or strong premature family history. Reassess in 3-7 years if therapy is deferred. **CAC 1-99 below the 75th percentile** generally supports treatment to LDL below 100 mg/dL; **CAC at least 100 or at least the 75th percentile** supports more intensive lowering to below 70. **CAC at least 1,000** targets below 55. These are treatment decisions after selective testing, not population-screening mandates. [Guideline selective-imaging and subclinical-atherosclerosis recommendations](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423)

The **2022 USPSTF** recommendation remains a separately named framework for starting statins in adults 40-75 without CVD: at least one specified risk factor and a ten-year CVD risk of at least 10% supports treatment; 7.5% to below 10% supports selective offering. Evidence is insufficient for initiating primary-prevention statins at age 76 or older. Its statement excludes LDL above 190 mg/dL and known familial hypercholesterolemia from its scope. Do not attach these percentages to a PREVENT estimate and claim the result is the USPSTF pathway. [USPSTF final recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/statin-use-in-adults-preventive-medication)

### Severe LDL elevation is not a calculator problem

Repeated untreated **LDL of at least 190 mg/dL** warrants a separate severe-hypercholesterolemia pathway, evaluation for secondary causes, and maximally tolerated statin therapy rather than waiting for a high ten-year estimate. Young age can conceal a large lifetime burden. Familial hypercholesterolemia becomes more likely with very high untreated LDL, premature ASCVD in close relatives, tendon xanthomas, or unusually early corneal arcus. Absence of visible findings does not exclude it.

Establish relatives' specific events and ages, especially premature infarction in a parent or sibling. Consider specialist assessment, genetic evaluation where useful, and **cascade screening** of relatives. A negative genetic panel does not exclude every inherited or polygenic lipid disorder. Severe phenotypes, childhood disease, pregnancy with very high risk, or inadequate response to combination therapy merit coordinated specialty care.

Treatment goals depend on the entire risk category. In uncomplicated severe primary hypercholesterolemia without additional risk features, the current pathway uses LDL below 100 mg/dL. With confirmed heterozygous FH, additional ASCVD risk factors, or documented coronary calcification, the goal is below 70; severe hypercholesterolemia with clinical ASCVD calls for below 55. Add suitable nonstatin therapy when maximally tolerated statin treatment does not achieve the indicated goal. Established vascular disease requires its higher-risk goal. [2026 severe-hypercholesterolemia recommendations](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423)

**Clinical Case.** A 32-year-old has repeat untreated LDL of 224 mg/dL, normal thyroid and kidney testing, and paternal coronary revascularization in the forties. Treat the severe phenotype and assess inherited disease and relatives despite low ten-year risk.

### Choose a statin by expected response and individual safety

Statins reduce hepatic cholesterol synthesis and increase LDL clearance. Intensity describes expected percentage reduction; actual response depends on adherence, biology, dose, and interactions. Confirm tolerance and measured response.

| Expected LDL reduction | Common adult once-daily examples |
| --- | --- |
| High intensity: at least 50% | Atorvastatin 40-80 mg; rosuvastatin 20-40 mg |
| Moderate intensity: 30%-49% | Atorvastatin 10-20 mg; rosuvastatin 5-10 mg |

These examples are intensity categories, not universal starting doses. Kidney function, ancestry-associated pharmacokinetics, age, interactions, and product labeling can require a different dose or drug. [Current guideline statin-intensity table](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423)

For example, rosuvastatin starts at **5 mg daily and must not exceed 10 mg daily** in severe renal impairment (label creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) without hemodialysis. Atorvastatin does not require a renal dose adjustment, although renal impairment increases muscle-injury risk. A high-intensity category must never override a product-specific limit. [Rosuvastatin renal labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=1d8b875f-3884-13b3-e063-6394a90a0c88); [atorvastatin labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=a60cc18b-0631-4cf0-b021-9f52224ece65)

Assess baseline liver status and relevant muscle symptoms; consider liver-enzyme testing before treatment and afterward when clinically indicated. Routine CK surveillance in asymptomatic patients does not replace symptom and interaction assessment. New severe weakness, substantial muscle pain, dark urine, or systemic illness warrants prompt evaluation for muscle injury, including kidney consequences. Review hypothyroidism, recent exertion, and interacting drugs. Macrolides, azoles, some antivirals, cyclosporine, and gemfibrozil can be important, but interaction management is product- and dose-specific. [Current atorvastatin safety information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=a60cc18b-0631-4cf0-b021-9f52224ece65)

Evaluate reported symptoms and competing causes. Depending on severity, pause treatment, then consider a lower dose, another statin, or an alternative schedule with follow-up. Severe injury or persistent weakness requires further evaluation before rechallenge. Preserve tolerated LDL lowering and add nonstatin therapy when needed.

Pregnancy planning deserves an explicit conversation. Most pregnant patients should stop statins; very-high-risk exceptions require individualized specialist assessment. Current FDA guidance does not make every pregnancy-capable patient ineligible for a statin. Patients who require ongoing statin therapy should not breastfeed. Accidental early exposure warrants counseling, not an automatic assumption of fetal injury. [FDA pregnancy and lactation guidance](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-requests-removal-strongest-warning-against-using-cholesterol-lowering-statins-during-pregnancy)

### Assess achieved goals and know when to add therapy

The current ACC/AHA approach considers **both percentage reduction and the achieved lipid level**. For ordinary borderline/intermediate primary prevention, LDL below 100 mg/dL is a goal; high-risk primary prevention generally targets below 70. Secondary prevention generally targets below 70 when not at very high risk and below **55 mg/dL when at very high risk**. Corresponding non-HDL goals are below 130, 100, and 85 mg/dL for the respective LDL goals of 100, 70, and 55. Disease-specific pathways, including severe hypercholesterolemia above, can modify the goal; these anchors are not a replacement for assigning the correct category. [ACC implementation discussion](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Articles/2026/09/01/01/Feature-Getting-to-Goals)

**Very-high-risk ASCVD** includes at least two major ASCVD events, or one plus at least two high-risk features. Major events include recent ACS, prior MI, ischemic stroke, and symptomatic PAD; do not count the same recent ACS again as a separate prior MI. Features include age at least 65, coronary revascularization, smoking, diabetes, HF, hypertension, and LDL at least 100 despite statin plus ezetimibe. Thus, an MI survivor with diabetes and hypertension is not automatically in the lower-risk secondary-prevention category. [2026 guideline risk definition](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423)

Diabetes requires an explicit framework: ACC/AHA recommends at least moderate-intensity therapy at ages 40-75 and supports high intensity with multiple risk factors. **ADA 2026** recommends high intensity and LDL below 70 at those ages when higher cardiovascular risk includes even one additional ASCVD risk factor; diabetes with established ASCVD targets below 55. Use the diabetes-specific plan in 04B rather than interpreting a low PREVENT result as permission to omit treatment. [ADA 2026 cardiovascular recommendations](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S216/163933/10-Cardiovascular-Disease-and-Risk-Management)

Recheck a lipid profile **four to twelve weeks after starting or intensifying treatment**, then generally every **6-12 months** according to stability and risk. Post-ACS follow-up may use a shorter disease-specific schedule. Before adding drugs, confirm actual use, response, secondary causes, and the agreed goal. [2026 monitoring recommendations](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)

Ezetimibe reduces intestinal cholesterol absorption and is often a practical oral addition. PCSK9-directed therapy can produce substantial additional LDL lowering; monoclonal antibodies such as evolocumab and alirocumab differ from the RNA-targeting agent inclisiran in administration and supporting evidence. Bempedoic acid is another oral option, including for selected patients with statin-attributed adverse effects. Choice depends on the LDL gap, outcomes evidence for the population, adverse effects, injections, access, and cost. Similar lipid effects do not establish equivalent clinical outcomes.

Bempedoic acid can raise uric acid, precipitate gout, and cause tendon injury; assess interactions. [Current bempedoic-acid safety information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=88d06d89-a3da-40b4-b273-8f4f7d56c4c9)

Bile-acid sequestrants can worsen triglycerides and interfere with absorption of other drugs. Raising HDL with niacin is not a routine substitute for evidence-based LDL lowering. Specialist treatments for severe inherited disorders have distinct eligibility and safety requirements. Arrange referral when the phenotype is unusual, a goal remains substantially unmet, or adverse effects prevent adequate treatment. Continue dietary, activity, tobacco, and weight interventions alongside medication.

**Calculation.** LDL falls from 212 to 102 mg/dL, about 52%: (212 - 102) / 212. This meets the expected high-intensity response, but an achieved LDL of 102 must still be judged against the risk-specific goal.

### Triglycerides create two related but different treatment tasks

Moderate triglyceride elevation often accompanies insulin resistance, obesity, diabetes, alcohol use, dietary carbohydrate excess, or medicines. Address the contributor and the patient's ASCVD risk; a triglyceride result does not replace LDL-directed prevention. In selected statin-treated patients, prescription icosapent ethyl can reduce cardiovascular events. This evidence should not be generalized to every over-the-counter fish-oil product or every person with a modest triglyceride elevation.

At **500 mg/dL or higher**, pancreatitis prevention becomes increasingly important, especially at **1,000 mg/dL or higher**. Confirm the pattern, review glycemic control and secondary causes, and address alcohol and diet promptly. Very high triglycerides can require a very-low-fat strategy to reduce chylomicron production; a high-fat weight-loss diet may worsen the immediate problem. Persistent severe elevations can warrant a fibrate or prescription omega-3 therapy in addition to dietary treatment. Fenofibrate requires kidney-appropriate selection and dosing; do not combine gemfibrozil casually with a statin because of muscle-injury risk. [2026 hypertriglyceridemia recommendations](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423)

Severe upper abdominal pain, vomiting, dehydration, or systemic illness in this setting needs urgent assessment for pancreatitis or another acute illness, not only a routine repeat lipid order. Recurrent severe elevations, childhood onset, or pancreatitis despite treatment can suggest chylomicronemia requiring specialist evaluation. Long-term ASCVD treatment still matters after the immediate triglyceride threat is controlled.
