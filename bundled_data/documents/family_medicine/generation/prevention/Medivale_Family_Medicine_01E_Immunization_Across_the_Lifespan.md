# Medivale Family Medicine

## 01E. Immunization Across the Lifespan

Use current CDC adult guidance and its notes. For pediatric decisions, **volume 11A uses the AAP 2026 schedule and respiratory-season addendum**; maternal RSV timing follows the current specialist seasonal guidance below. Childhood-series and catch-up details are consolidated in volume 11A.

### Start with the record, not an isolated age cutoff

Select vaccines using age, valid documented doses, immunity, pregnancy, immune status, exposures, and product availability. Retrieve registry and outside records. **Continue a delayed series** rather than restart it solely for a long interval; minimum ages and intervals still determine dose validity. [CDC general immunization principles](https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-2-general-best-practice-guidance.html)

Schedule notes distinguish routine, risk-based, shared-decision, and catch-up vaccination. Read product-specific notes and disease-specific seasonal updates alongside the overview table. [CDC adult schedule](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-age.html), [adult notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html), and [AAP pediatric schedule](https://publications.aap.org/redbook/resources/15585/AAP-Immunization-Schedule)

**Clinical case.** A 22-year-old has documentation of doses given several years earlier. Confirm product, starting age, and dose validity, then complete the series without restarting solely because of the delay.

### Live vaccines and precautions: identify the actual contraindication

Live attenuated vaccines such as MMR and varicella are generally avoided during **pregnancy and severe immunocompromise**. Nonlive vaccines are not automatically contraindicated in these settings, although timing and immune response may differ. Mild illness is not a routine reason to postpone vaccination; moderate or severe acute illness is a precaution requiring assessment. Anaphylaxis to a prior dose or a relevant component is different from a nonspecific prior symptom. Rotavirus vaccine has additional contraindications, including severe combined immunodeficiency and a history of intussusception. [CDC contraindications and precautions](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-best-practices/contraindications-precautions.html)

Injected or intranasal live vaccines not given on the same day generally need at least **four weeks** between them. Antibody-containing blood products can interfere with certain live vaccines; use the applicable product-specific timing guidance. [CDC general immunization principles](https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-2-general-best-practice-guidance.html)

Pregnancy creates opportunities as well as restrictions. Tdap is given during each pregnancy, whereas live vaccines needed for future protection are generally addressed before pregnancy or postpartum. An inadvertently administered contraindicated vaccine requires informed assessment rather than an automatic conclusion that fetal harm has occurred. [CDC vaccination in pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines-pregnancy/hcp/vaccination-guidelines/index.html)

### Influenza and COVID: use the applicable seasonal guidance

Influenza vaccination is recommended **annually from six months of age**. Vaccine selection depends on age, pregnancy, and medical conditions; a live intranasal product is not a substitute for an appropriate nonlive product in pregnancy. Prior influenza infection does not establish protection against all subsequently circulating strains. [CDC influenza vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/vaccines/keyfacts.html)

The CDC **2026-2027 COVID framework** recommends vaccination for adults **18 or older**. Pediatric decisions follow the AAP seasonal pathway in volume 11A. At age 18 these frameworks overlap: distinguish the CDC adult recommendation from the AAP adolescent pathway, which includes age 18. Dose number, product, and intervals depend on age, previous vaccination, and immune status. Product authorization and a public-health recommendation are not identical; consult the seasonal table and its product-specific annotations rather than applying a universal booster count. [CDC adult seasonal COVID guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/covid/hcp/vaccine-considerations/routine-guidance.html) and [AAP pediatric schedule](https://publications.aap.org/redbook/resources/15585/AAP-Immunization-Schedule)

### Tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis: routine care differs from wound care

The routine adolescent Tdap dose is given at **11-12 years**. Adults who have never received Tdap should receive it, then receive a Td or Tdap booster every **10 years** after completion of the primary series. An unknown or incomplete primary series needs completion, not merely a label that the patient is overdue for a booster. [CDC tetanus vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/index.html)

For an unvaccinated adult, give **Tdap, Td or Tdap at least four weeks later, then Td or Tdap six to twelve months after dose two**. Count valid prior doses when completing an interrupted series. [CDC adult catch-up notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html)

Wounds introduce a separate assessment. For a complete primary series, use the interval from the last tetanus-containing vaccine together with wound type:

| Wound situation | Vaccine / immune globulin decision |
| --- | --- |
| Clean, minor wound; complete primary series | Booster if the last dose was at least 10 years ago. Tetanus immune globulin is not indicated. |
| Dirty or major wound; complete primary series | Booster if the last dose was at least 5 years ago. Assess immune status for immune globulin eligibility. |
| Any wound; unknown or incomplete series | Give indicated vaccination and arrange series completion. For a dirty/major wound, also give indicated tetanus immune globulin. |

Dirty/major wounds include examples such as punctures and wounds contaminated with soil or devitalized tissue. Tetanus immune globulin is also indicated for dirty/major wounds in patients with HIV or severe immunodeficiency. Wound cleaning and debridement remain necessary; vaccination does not remove contaminated tissue. [CDC tetanus wound management](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Give **Tdap in each pregnancy at 27-36 weeks**, preferably early in that window, to improve infant protection. Previous adult Tdap does not eliminate this pregnancy-specific indication. [CDC vaccination in pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines-pregnancy/hcp/vaccination-guidelines/index.html)

**Clinical case.** Six years after Tdap in a patient with a complete series, a clean paper cut needs no booster, but a soil-contaminated puncture does. Wound type changes the interval.

### Pneumococcal protection: product and prior doses change the next step

For adults **50 or older** without a prior pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, or with unknown conjugate-vaccine history, CDC offers **PCV15, PCV20, or PCV21**. PCV20 or PCV21 generally completes that pathway without PPSV23. PCV15 is ordinarily followed by **PPSV23 after one year**; an eight-week minimum can be considered in specified higher-risk settings, including immunocompromise, CSF leak, or cochlear implant. Younger adults may qualify through medical or other risk indications. Previous PCV13, PPSV23, or mixed series require the history-specific algorithm, not automatic repetition of a vaccine-naive schedule. [CDC pneumococcal recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/pneumococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/index.html)

Record pneumococcal product names and dates; "pneumonia shot received" is insufficient to select the next dose.

### Zoster and HPV: prevention is not treatment of current disease

Give two doses of recombinant zoster vaccine to adults **50 or older**, and to adults **19 or older who are or will be immunodeficient or immunosuppressed** because of disease or therapy. The usual interval is **two to six months**, with a shorter interval available in selected immunocompromised situations. A history of shingles does not remove the preventive indication, but vaccination is not treatment for an active episode. Recombinant zoster vaccine is not a live vaccine. [CDC zoster guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/shingles/hcp/vaccine-considerations/index.html)

CDC's routine HPV target is **11-12 years**, with initiation permitted at **nine**; AAP recommends starting at **9-12** in the pediatric framework in volume 11A. Catch-up is recommended through **26** if inadequately vaccinated; ages **27-45** use shared decision-making. Starting before 15 generally requires two doses six to twelve months apart; starting at 15 or later, or immunocompromise, calls for three. A two-dose series less than five months apart needs an additional dose. Defer remaining doses during pregnancy. Vaccination prevents new infection, not existing HPV disease, and does not replace cervical screening. [CDC HPV guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/hpv/hcp/vaccination-considerations/index.html)

### Hepatitis A and B: combine routine eligibility with exposure assessment

Hepatitis A vaccination is routine at **12-23 months**, with catch-up for unvaccinated children and adolescents. Adult indications include relevant travel, chronic liver disease, certain exposure risks, and a request for protection. Monovalent HepA uses **two doses at least six months apart**; the combination HepA-HepB product has a different schedule. [CDC hepatitis A vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/vaccine-administration/index.html) and [adult notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html)

Hepatitis B vaccination includes infants, unvaccinated people younger than 19, adults **19-59**, and adults **60 or older with risk factors**; older adults without identified risk may also receive it. Adult products can use different series, and dialysis adds special considerations. Do not allow unavailable screening to become a barrier to indicated vaccination. [CDC hepatitis B vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/vaccine-administration/index.html)

For adults, **two Heplisav-B doses at least four weeks apart** complete its series. Engerix-B or Recombivax HB ordinarily uses **0, 1, and 6 months**; minimum intervals are four weeks between doses one and two, eight weeks between two and three, and sixteen weeks between one and three. A mixed-product history is not automatically a two-dose series; dialysis requires the appropriate formulation/regimen. [CDC adult notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html)

A newborn of an HBsAg-positive mother needs vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin promptly, **within 12 hours of birth**, with completion and follow-up arranged. [CDC perinatal HBV prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/perinatal-provider-overview/)

### MMR, varicella, meningococcal disease, and travel

Routine childhood MMR doses occur at **12-15 months and four to six years**. Most adults without evidence of immunity need one dose; higher-exposure settings such as healthcare work, international travel, and postsecondary education can require **two doses at least four weeks apart**. A verbal recollection is not equivalent to documented immunity. Apply pregnancy and immune-status precautions. [CDC MMR guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/hcp/vaccine-considerations/index.html) and [adult notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html)

Varicella protection requires evidence of immunity or an indicated two-dose series. Birth before 1980 is not accepted as evidence of immunity in certain important groups, including pregnant patients, healthcare personnel, and immunocompromised people. For susceptible people **13 or older**, the usual two-dose interval is **four to eight weeks**. Apply live-vaccine precautions rather than confusing varicella vaccination with nonlive zoster vaccination. [CDC varicella guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/chickenpox/hcp/vaccine-considerations/index.html)

MenACWY is routinely given at **11-12**, with a booster at **16**. MenB uses shared clinical decision-making for healthy adolescents and young adults **16-23**, preferably **16-18**, while medical-risk indications have separate schedules. MenACWY and MenB protect against different serogroups; one does not automatically substitute for the other. Asplenia and complement-related risks require explicit review. [CDC meningococcal recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/index.html)

Before travel, assess destination, itinerary, departure date, pregnancy, immune status, and prior immunization. Address destination-specific protection and entry requirements alongside missing routine vaccines. [CDC travel immunization principles](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/preparing-international-travelers/vaccination-and-immunoprophylaxis-general-principles.html)

### RSV: distinguish adult vaccination, maternal vaccination, and infant antibodies

CDC recommends one RSV vaccine dose for **all adults 75 or older** and adults **50-74 at increased risk of severe RSV disease**. It is **not currently an annual vaccine**; a previously vaccinated adult should not automatically receive another dose each season. [CDC adult RSV guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/adults.html)

For maternal protection, use **Abrysvo at 32 weeks 0 days through 36 weeks 6 days**. The **2026-2027 specialist seasonal pathway uses September 1 through March 1** in most of the continental United States, with regional adjustments. Do not use maternal vaccination when delivery is planned within two weeks or infant antibody protection is planned. A maternal RSV dose in a previous pregnancy is not repeated; assess antibody protection for the current infant instead. This differs from Tdap's each-pregnancy rule. [SMFM current-season addendum](https://www.smfm.org/rsv-toolkit) and [AAP schedule/addendum](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/AAP-Immunization-Schedule.pdf)

The CDC maternal overview specifies September-January, a different window from the specialist 2026-2027 pathway above. Apply the named seasonal framework consistently. [CDC maternal RSV guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/pregnant-people.html)

Infant long-acting antibodies are **passive immunization, not vaccines**. Nirsevimab or clesrovimab can protect eligible infants **younger than 8 months** born during or entering their first RSV season if the mother was unvaccinated, her status is unknown, or birth occurred **less than 14 days after maternal vaccination**. Most infants born at least 14 days after maternal vaccination do not also need an antibody, except in selected high-risk circumstances. Selected high-risk children **8-19 months** entering a second season may receive **nirsevimab**, not clesrovimab. [CDC infant RSV guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/infants-young-children.html)

### Leave a complete vaccination plan

Document product, date, contraindications, and the next valid dose. Explain the target infection and whether protection is active or passive. Assign responsibility for missing records and follow-up.
