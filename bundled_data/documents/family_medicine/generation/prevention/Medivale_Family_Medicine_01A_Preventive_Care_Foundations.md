# Medivale Family Medicine

## 01A. Preventive Care Foundations

### The preventive visit is a sequence of decisions

A preventive visit reduces future illness through risk assessment, behavioral counseling, vaccination, appropriate screening, and follow-up. The plan should reflect prior care, the patient's priorities, and the benefits and harms of intervention.

Ask about current concerns first: a new symptom may take priority over routine prevention. Establish what care has occurred, what remains due, and which actions the patient can realistically complete.

Organize the encounter around five questions:

1. Is there a current symptom or immediate safety concern?
2. Which diseases, exposures, and complications is this patient at risk for?
3. Which preventive actions offer a meaningful benefit in this population?
4. What information or preferences might change the choice?
5. Who will review the result and complete the next step?

Use shared decision-making to select a feasible plan. [AHRQ shared decisions](https://www.ahrq.gov/sdm/share-approach/index.html)

### Three purposes of prevention

**Primary prevention** prevents disease or injury through measures such as vaccination and tobacco cessation. **Secondary prevention** detects disease or a precursor before symptoms, when earlier action can improve outcomes. **Tertiary prevention** limits complications or disability in established disease, such as diabetic foot care or stroke rehabilitation. These categories describe purpose, not relative importance. [CDC prevention framework](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/142637/cdc_142637_DS1.pdf)

### Screening, diagnosis, and surveillance are different tasks

**Screening** applies to people without the relevant signs or symptoms. **Diagnostic evaluation** investigates an existing concern. **Surveillance** follows a known disease, abnormality, treatment, or high-risk state. Using the wrong pathway can delay care: a routine screening interval does not explain how to evaluate a new lump, visible bleeding, or progressive swallowing difficulty.

A positive screen generally requires diagnostic evaluation; a negative screen does not dismiss a subsequent symptom. [NCI screening overview](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/patient-screening-overview-pdq)

| Clinical situation | Task | What must be established |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A person without symptoms asks about future disease risk | Screening or primary prevention | Eligibility, expected benefit, harms, and preferences |
| A new symptom appears during a preventive appointment | Diagnostic evaluation | Urgency, differential diagnosis, and an appropriate work-up |
| A prior test was abnormal | Follow-up of the abnormality | Exact result, completed testing, and next required action |
| A condition or precursor has already been treated | Surveillance | Pathology, treatment, recurrence risk, and applicable surveillance guidance |

### Build a usable risk history

A history becomes useful when it changes a decision. Ask about the patient's age, organs present, pregnancy possibility, personal diagnoses, prior procedures, medications, immune status, exposures, and earlier preventive results. Ask which relatives had relevant diseases and how old they were at diagnosis. A vague statement that cancer runs in the family is less useful than knowing that a parent developed colorectal cancer at 46.

Confirm test completion, adequacy, and findings. Obtain a recalled colonoscopy report before assigning a normal-screening or polyp-surveillance interval.

Avoid treating a patient characteristic as an explanation by itself. Work schedules, language access, financial constraints, family responsibilities, and prior experiences with health care can change which plan is feasible. Ask about these directly rather than infer them from appearance or demographic labels.

### Benefits and harms occur along the entire testing pathway

Screening helps when detection leads to effective intervention. Harms include procedural complications, anxiety, false reassurance, and unnecessary treatment. **Overdiagnosis** detects real disease that would never have harmed the patient; a **false positive** occurs when the target disease is absent. [NCI screening harms](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/patient-screening-overview-pdq)

Before ordering a screen, consider the consequence of either result. If the patient would decline the necessary diagnostic procedure or would not benefit from treatment, the value of starting that screening pathway changes. Discuss the concern without assuming that older age alone determines the answer.

### Why test accuracy does not equal clinical usefulness

Sensitivity describes how often a test is positive among people with the target disease. Specificity describes how often it is negative among people without that disease. The probability that a positive result represents disease also depends on how common the disease is in the population being tested.

When prevalence is low, false positives may outnumber true positives even with a reasonably accurate test. Use the worked two-by-two table and likelihood-ratio calculations in **16D, Clinical Evidence and Statistics**, rather than equating sensitivity with the probability that a positive result represents disease. [CDC test-performance definitions](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson5/appendixa.html)

Longer survival after diagnosis can also be misleading. Finding the same disease earlier starts the survival clock sooner even if the date of death does not change: **lead-time bias**. Screening can preferentially find slower disease because it remains detectable longer: **length bias**. A favorable screening study should demonstrate meaningful health outcomes, rather than rely only on the number of diagnoses or survival measured from diagnosis. [NCI professional overview](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/hp-screening-overview-pdq)

Discuss **absolute benefit over a defined time**, not just relative risk reduction. The same relative effect produces less absolute benefit at a lower baseline risk. Section 16D develops absolute risk reduction, number needed to treat, and the assumptions behind extrapolating an effect to another population. [CDC measures of association](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson3/section5.html)

### Read a recommendation as a conditional statement

A recommendation applies to a defined population, intervention, and outcome. Its age range or interval is not a rule for every patient with the same organ. Before using a cutoff, ask whether symptoms, previous disease, an inherited syndrome, pregnancy, immune suppression, or an abnormal prior result place the patient outside the routine population.

USPSTF grades communicate the balance of benefit and harm and the certainty of evidence:

| Grade | Practical interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| A or B | Offer the service to the specified population because the expected net benefit supports it. |
| C | Offer selectively; the expected net benefit is small and individual factors matter. |
| D | Avoid the service in the specified population because benefit is absent or harms outweigh it. |
| I | Evidence is insufficient to determine the balance; this is different from a recommendation against the service. |

An I statement does not establish that a symptomatic person should receive no evaluation. It addresses the preventive question the panel studied. [USPSTF grade definitions](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/about-uspstf/methods-and-processes/grade-definitions)

### Three Visits, Three Decisions

An asymptomatic 46-year-old needs colorectal screening that fits an unpredictable schedule. A 39-year-old reports weeks of visible rectal bleeding. A 61-year-old brings a positive stool-test result without completed follow-up.

The first needs a feasible screening strategy, the second diagnostic evaluation, and the third completion of an abnormal-result pathway. Age eligibility alone cannot determine all three decisions.

### Make the plan understandable and complete

Shared decisions require more than asking whether the patient wants a test. Explain the relevant options, the likely consequences, and where uncertainty remains; elicit what matters to the patient and check understanding. A person may value avoiding a procedure, reducing the chance of cancer, minimizing missed work, or resolving uncertainty differently. [AHRQ SHARE Approach](https://www.ahrq.gov/sdm/share-approach/index.html)

Document the selected action and the follow-up owner. A useful note distinguishes a declined test, an informed decision to defer, an access barrier, and a result that is still pending. Where appropriate, record what should prompt earlier contact. Prevention is completed through a chain of care, not through an order alone.
