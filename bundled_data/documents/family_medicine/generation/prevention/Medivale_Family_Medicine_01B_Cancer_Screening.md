# Medivale Family Medicine

## 01B. Cancer Screening and Hereditary Risk

### Start with the clinical task

Screening schedules apply to asymptomatic people with the relevant organ present. Previous cancer, precancer, inherited risk, and abnormal results may require a different pathway; new symptoms require diagnostic assessment. [NCI screening overview](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/patient-screening-overview-pdq)

### Colorectal screening: choose a complete strategy

The 2021 USPSTF framework recommends screening average-risk asymptomatic adults from **45 through 75 years**. Screening from 76 through 85 is selective, considering health, prior screening, and preferences; discontinue routine screening after 85. The average-risk pathway excludes prior colorectal cancer, adenomatous polyps, inflammatory bowel disease, and known high-risk hereditary syndromes.

| Strategy | Routine interval in the USPSTF framework |
| --- | --- |
| FIT | Every year |
| High-sensitivity guaiac fecal occult blood testing | Every year |
| Stool DNA combined with FIT | Every 1-3 years |
| CT colonography | Every 5 years |
| Flexible sigmoidoscopy alone | Every 5 years |
| Flexible sigmoidoscopy plus annual FIT | Sigmoidoscopy every 10 years, FIT annually |
| Colonoscopy | Every 10 years |

These intervals assume an appropriate negative examination or result; an abnormal finding changes the next step. [USPSTF colorectal screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening)

A positive stool-based screen requires colonoscopy, not another stool test. Discuss this possibility before choosing a stool strategy. In the **ACG 2021 familial-risk pathway**, colorectal cancer or an advanced polyp in **one first-degree relative before 60**, or **two or more at any age**, calls for colonoscopy starting at **40 or 10 years before the youngest diagnosis**, whichever is earlier, then every **5 years**. With only one affected first-degree relative diagnosed at **60 or older**, start at **40** and subsequently use average-risk intervals. These rules concern cancer or an **advanced** polyp, not every small polyp. A suspected inherited syndrome needs a syndrome-specific plan. [ACG guideline resources](https://gi.org/education/educating-you-your-colleagues/colorectal-cancer-awareness-education-resources/), [ACG 2021 guideline](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000001122)

**Clinical case.** A 52-year-old chooses FIT because transportation for colonoscopy is difficult. A positive result now requires colonoscopy: address the transportation barrier rather than repeat the stool test.

### Breast screening: routine risk and high risk need different plans

The 2024 USPSTF recommendation is **biennial mammography from 40 through 74 years**. This framework includes people assigned female at birth in the specified screening population, including those with dense breasts or an affected first-degree relative, but excludes certain high-risk groups such as carriers of a high-risk genetic variant, previous high-dose chest radiation at a young age, previous breast cancer, or a high-risk lesion.

For people 75 or older, evidence is insufficient to determine the screening balance. Evidence is also insufficient to recommend for or against supplemental ultrasound or MRI solely because breasts are dense after an otherwise negative mammogram. An insufficient-evidence statement is not a universal prohibition, and it does not replace high-risk assessment or evaluation of a breast symptom. [USPSTF breast screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/breast-cancer-screening)

### Cervical screening: establish the cervix and the history

The **USPSTF 2018** routine-screening framework is:

| Population | USPSTF 2018 approach |
| --- | --- |
| Younger than 21 | Do not routinely screen. |
| Ages 21-29 | Cytology every 3 years. |
| Ages 30-65 | Cytology every 3 years, primary high-risk HPV testing every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. |
| Older than 65 | Stop only with adequate prior negative screening and no relevant high-risk history. |
| Total hysterectomy with cervix removed | No routine screening when there is no history of high-grade precancer or cervical cancer. |

Confirm removal of the cervix from the operative record or examination. For stopping after 65, the named USPSTF framework defines adequate prior screening as **three consecutive negative cytology tests or two negative cotests during the preceding 10 years**, with the latest within **5 years**. Unknown or inadequate screening can justify continuing. A high-risk history, including treated CIN2+, overrides routine stopping rules. HPV vaccination does not eliminate screening. [USPSTF cervical screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/cervical-cancer-screening)

### Abnormal cervical results are managed by risk

ASCCP management combines the current result with previous HPV, cytology, biopsy, and treatment history. **NILM** means negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy; **ASC-US** means atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance; **LSIL** and **HSIL** mean low- and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions.

The following anchors apply to **immunocompetent, nonpregnant patients 25 or older**, using conventional HPV/cytology triage without previous CIN2+ treatment. They do not replace the history-based risk calculation.

| Current result and relevant history | Usual next step |
| --- | --- |
| HPV-negative ASC-US, unknown previous screening | HPV-based testing in 3 years. |
| HPV-positive ASC-US or LSIL, unknown previous screening | Colposcopy. |
| New HPV-positive ASC-US or LSIL after a documented negative screening HPV test or cotest within about 5 years | HPV-based testing in 1 year; previous negative cytology alone does not supply the same reassurance. |
| HPV16- or HPV18-positive, even with NILM | Colposcopy. |
| Other high-risk HPV positive with NILM, no additional concerning history | Usually HPV-based testing in 1 year; persistent positivity changes management. |
| ASC-H: atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude HSIL | Colposcopy regardless of HPV result. |

HSIL requires prompt high-grade evaluation. **Expedited excisional treatment** is preferred for eligible nonpregnant patients 25 or older with HPV16-positive HSIL or HPV-positive HSIL after **more than 5 years without screening**; discuss reproductive effects and alternatives. Do not apply an expedited-treatment rule to pregnancy or patients younger than 25. [CDC abnormal-result management](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/hpv-cancer.htm), [ASCCP consensus guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7147428/), [ASCCP risk estimates](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7147417/)

An **atypical glandular cell** result generally requires colposcopy and endocervical sampling; add endometrial sampling at 35 or older, or earlier with endometrial risk factors. Atypical **endometrial** cells have a separate initial sampling pathway. Pregnancy prohibits endocervical curettage and endometrial biopsy. These findings cannot be dismissed by a negative HPV result. For patients younger than 25, low-grade abnormalities generally use repeat cytology rather than the adult HPV-risk table, while high-grade findings require colposcopy. [ASCCP consensus guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7147428/)

An **unsatisfactory cytology** result with no, unknown, or negative HPV requires repeat age-appropriate screening as soon as practical and **within 4 months**, not a mandatory two-month wait. After CIN2+ treatment, three negative HPV-based tests at **6, 18, and 30 months** precede a 3-year interval. Continue surveillance for at least **25 years**, even beyond 65 when appropriate. [ASCCP updates through 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10755815/)

ASCCP **Enduring Guidelines** adapt management to newer tests; they do not make every HPV specimen interchangeable. For eligible average-risk, immunocompetent, nonpregnant patients **25 or older**, an approved **self-collected vaginal HPV test** can improve screening access. A negative result uses a **3-year** interval, not the five-year clinician-collected interval. HPV16/18 positivity requires colposcopy with cervical cytology obtained there. Other positive results follow assay-specific triage; vaginal self-collection does not supply a cervical cytology sample. Use only approved assay/device/setting combinations, including for home collection. Clinician collection remains preferred for surveillance after abnormalities or treatment. [ASCCP self-collection recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11939108/) and [2026 home-collection update](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12994907/)

**p16/Ki67 dual stain** helps triage selected HPV-positive results. In eligible HPV-positive NILM/ASC-US/LSIL pathways, dual-stain positivity leads to colposcopy; negativity generally permits one-year HPV-based follow-up. A negative dual stain does not cancel HPV16/18 positivity or high-grade/glandular cytology requiring evaluation. Extended genotyping has its own risk groups; use the reported assay's pathway rather than automatically sending every non-16/18 result to the same branch. [ASCCP dual-stain recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11331430/) and [current management resources](https://www.asccp.org/guidelines/)

**Clinical case.** A 67-year-old treated for high-grade cervical precancer eight years ago still needs surveillance. Obtain pathology and follow-up records rather than apply the routine stopping age of 65.

### Lung screening: smoking history requires arithmetic

The 2021 USPSTF framework uses **annual low-dose CT** for adults **50-80 years** with **at least 20 pack-years** who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years. Stop when the smoking-cessation cutoff is reached or health substantially limits life expectancy or the ability or willingness to undergo curative lung surgery. A chest radiograph is not the recommended screening test.

Calculate pack-years as packs per day multiplied by years smoked, adding separate periods if consumption changed. One-half pack daily for 32 years is 16 pack-years, not 32. Record both cumulative exposure and the quit date. Other organizations may use different eligibility rules; do not silently exchange their criteria for USPSTF criteria. [USPSTF lung screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/lung-cancer-screening)

**Clinical case.** Two asymptomatic 63-year-olds each smoked one pack daily for 24 years. One quit nine years ago; the other quit 19 years ago. Only the first meets the USPSTF quit-date criterion. Hemoptysis in either patient requires diagnostic assessment.

### Prostate screening: make the trade-off explicit

The active final **USPSTF 2018** recommendation supports an individual decision about PSA screening for men **55-69 years** and recommends against routine PSA screening at **70 or older**. Discuss the small potential benefit, false positives, biopsy, overdiagnosis, and possible treatment-related urinary or sexual harms. Do not screen someone who does not prefer screening after that discussion. Other specialty guidance may frame age and risk differently; name the framework used. [USPSTF prostate screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/prostate-cancer-screening)

### Hereditary cancer risk: the family pattern changes the task

Ask which relatives were affected, the cancer sites, age at diagnosis, bilateral or multiple cancers, and whether a pathogenic familial variant is known. In the USPSTF BRCA framework, an appropriate brief familial risk tool is used for women with relevant personal/family histories or ancestry; a positive assessment leads to genetic counseling and, when indicated, testing. Routine testing is not recommended for a history that does not suggest increased risk. [USPSTF BRCA risk assessment](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/brca-related-cancer-risk-assessment-genetic-counseling-and-genetic-testing)

BRCA1 and BRCA2 help maintain DNA integrity. A harmful inherited variant increases certain cancer risks, but does not mean that cancer is inevitable. A **variant of uncertain significance** is not equivalent to a pathogenic variant and should not be presented as a confirmed hereditary syndrome. Interpretation belongs with counseling and the family context. [NCI BRCA fact sheet](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/genetics/brca-fact-sheet)

Two important inherited colorectal patterns require more than the usual family-history screening rule:

| Pattern | Clinical significance |
| --- | --- |
| **Lynch syndrome** | Inherited DNA mismatch-repair dysfunction; early colorectal and endometrial cancers, sometimes other associated cancers, without the massive polyp burden of classic FAP. A suspicious pedigree warrants genetic assessment even without numerous polyps. |
| **Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)** | An **APC**-associated syndrome, classically with hundreds to thousands of adenomas. Attenuated disease can have fewer. Refer for genetics, early specialist endoscopic surveillance and risk-reducing surgical planning; routine adult screening is inadequate. |

Both typically show autosomal dominant inheritance, but an unremarkable reported family history does not exclude them. Mismatch-repair loss or microsatellite instability in a tumor can also be sporadic; an abnormal tumor test does **not by itself establish inherited Lynch syndrome**. Confirmatory evaluation determines the gene-specific surveillance and family-testing plan. [NCI hereditary colorectal cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/types/colorectal/hp/colorectal-genetics-pdq)

**Clinical case.** A 35-year-old whose mother had ovarian cancer and maternal uncle had breast cancer needs hereditary-risk assessment and counseling before selecting a screening plan.

### Knowing when screening is unsupported

For asymptomatic women without a known high-risk hereditary syndrome, USPSTF recommends **against ovarian cancer screening**. Routine CA-125 or transvaginal ultrasonography is not a substitute for a demonstrated beneficial screening program. The recommendation does not address evaluation of symptoms or a known hereditary syndrome. [USPSTF ovarian screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening)

Evidence is **insufficient** for population skin-cancer screening in asymptomatic adolescents and adults. An evolving lesion still requires assessment. [USPSTF skin screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/skin-cancer-screening)

USPSTF recommends **against routine testicular cancer screening** in asymptomatic adolescent and adult males. A newly noticed mass or acute pain is a different clinical problem and should not be dismissed because population screening lacks benefit. [USPSTF testicular screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/testicular-cancer-screening)

### Records that change the answer

Record the selected guideline, eligibility, most recent test and result, relevant pathology, family history, preferences, and follow-up owner. For an abnormal test, name the unresolved action rather than labeling the patient simply up to date or overdue.
