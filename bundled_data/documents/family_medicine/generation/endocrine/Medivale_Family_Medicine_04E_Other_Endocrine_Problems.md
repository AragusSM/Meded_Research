# Medivale Family Medicine

## 04E. Other Endocrine Problems

Calcium decisions use the Fifth International Workshop for primary hyperparathyroidism and ESE 2025 for chronic hypoparathyroidism. Adrenal care uses Endocrine Society guidance, including the 2024 joint glucocorticoid guideline and 2025 primary aldosteronism guideline. Pituitary Society statements inform pituitary assessment. Adult pathways below are not pediatric, pregnancy, or complete intensive-care dosing protocols.

### Interpret the response to a hormone, not just its reference interval

Interpret hormones against physiology: PTH should fall with rising calcium; ACTH should rise with adrenal cortisol failure. A reference-range value can be inappropriate for its paired result. Lesion size alone establishes neither secretion nor preserved gland function.

Establish symptoms, time course, medicines, and paired tests. Ask about steroids by every route, lithium, diuretics, supplements, antipsychotics, opioids, sex hormones, surgery/radiation, immunotherapy, pregnancy events, fractures, stones, and family history. Hypotension, confusion, seizures, visual loss, dehydration, or arrhythmia requires stabilization and urgent transfer.

### Confirm a calcium abnormality and use PTH to organize it

Ionized calcium is biologically active; protein-bound and complexed fractions also contribute to total calcium. Albumin loss or dehydration can alter total calcium without comparable ionized changes. Adjustment formulas are estimates. Measure properly handled ionized calcium when binding, pH, illness, or discordance complicates interpretation. Alkalosis increases binding and can lower ionized calcium despite normal total calcium. [Endotext calcium interpretation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK279037/)

PTH increases renal calcium conservation, promotes phosphate excretion, and stimulates renal production of active vitamin D, which supports intestinal calcium absorption. Interpret calcium with PTH, phosphate, magnesium, creatinine/eGFR, and selected vitamin D testing. The usual vitamin D store assessment is 25-hydroxyvitamin D; 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D answers selected mechanistic questions rather than replacing it. In CKD, phosphate retention and altered vitamin D metabolism complicate the expected patterns, so an elevated PTH is not automatically a primary parathyroid tumor. [NIDDK CKD mineral physiology](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/mineral-bone-disorder)

| Paired pattern | Initial interpretation | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High calcium; PTH high or not suppressed | PTH-dependent hypercalcemia | Consider primary hyperparathyroidism, FHH, lithium and the kidney history |
| High calcium; suppressed PTH | PTH-independent hypercalcemia | Investigate malignancy, vitamin D mechanisms, medicines and other causes |
| Low calcium; PTH low or not appropriately increased | Impaired PTH secretion | Neck surgery and severe magnesium depletion are important clues |
| Low/normal calcium; high PTH | Secondary response | Consider vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption and CKD before a primary diagnosis |
| Low calcium; high PTH and high phosphate | CKD or impaired PTH action among possibilities | Interpret renal function, age, treatment and inherited features together |

Phosphate varies with diet, renal function, timing, and treatment; normal phosphate does not exclude parathyroid disease. PTH resistance can mimic deficiency despite elevated PTH. Severe magnesium depletion impairs both PTH secretion and action, preventing correction of hypocalcemia until magnesium is restored. [ESE hypoparathyroidism physiology and assessment](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/193/5/G83/8321487) [NIH magnesium review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)

### Evaluate persistent hypercalcemia before ordering localization scans

Hypercalcemia may cause thirst, polyuria, constipation, weakness, cognitive change, stones, renal dysfunction, or electrical changes, yet mild chronic disease may be asymptomatic. Review calcium/alkali, vitamin D, thiazides, lithium, immobilization, and trajectory. Reference-range PTH can be inappropriately normal. Confirm mild unexpected findings without delaying urgent treatment of symptomatic or marked elevations.

Primary hyperparathyroidism usually reflects autonomous gland activity from an adenoma or multigland disease. Confirm the biochemical pattern, then assess kidney and skeletal consequences rather than assume that an asymptomatic patient has no organ involvement. A 24-hour urine study helps assess calcium excretion and the differential with familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH). [NIDDK primary hyperparathyroidism](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/primary-hyperparathyroidism)

For stable hypercalcemic primary disease, the Fifth Workshop requires paired calcium/PTH abnormalities on two occasions at least two weeks apart. Normocalcemic primary disease requires repeatedly normal total **and ionized** calcium with elevated PTH over 3-6 months, after excluding secondary causes such as CKD, vitamin D deficiency, and malabsorption. It cannot be diagnosed from one elevated PTH. [Fifth Workshop diagnostic criteria](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jbmr.4677)

FHH often produces longstanding mild hypercalcemia with relative hypocalciuria and a family pattern; parathyroid surgery usually does not correct its mechanism. Urine results overlap, and CKD, low intake, vitamin D deficiency, and medicines can distort them. Use family/genetic evaluation when uncertainty would change surgery. [ESE PARAT FHH assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8789028/)

The Fifth Workshop supports surgery for confirmed primary hyperparathyroidism with overt renal or skeletal complications and provides criteria even without overt symptoms. One qualifying feature can be sufficient; absence of all listed features does not prohibit a shared surgical decision. Imaging localizes glands for a planned operation; it does not establish or exclude the biochemical diagnosis.

| Feature in confirmed primary hyperparathyroidism | Fifth Workshop surgical criterion |
| --- | --- |
| Calcium | More than 1 mg/dL above the laboratory upper limit |
| Skeleton | Vertebral fracture or BMD T-score at or below -2.5 at an evaluated site |
| Kidney function | eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or creatinine clearance below 60 mL/min |
| Stones/calcium excretion | Nephrolithiasis/nephrocalcinosis, or hypercalciuria above 250 mg/day in women or 300 mg/day in men |
| Age | Younger than 50 years is itself sufficient |

These criteria concern the named hypercalcemic primary disorder, not automatic surgery for secondary PTH elevation or normocalcemic disease. Interpret bone measurements appropriately for age. [Fifth International Workshop recommendations](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jbmr.4677)

Without surgery, check calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and renal function annually; obtain three-site DXA every 1-2 years unless BMD is normal, with stone/vertebral assessment when indicated. Maintain appropriate calcium intake rather than extreme avoidance. A calcimimetic lowers calcium; an antiresorptive can improve BMD. Neither replaces curative surgery. New stones, fracture, worsening calcium or renal function should reopen the plan. [Fifth Workshop monitoring recommendations](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jbmr.4677)

**Clinical Case: Inappropriately normal PTH.** A 62-year-old has two mildly high calcium values and reference-range PTH. Review medicines and confirm paired interpretation. Negative localization ultrasound cannot exclude biochemical disease; a visible lesion cannot establish it or justify surgery in unrecognized FHH.

### Recognize suppressed-PTH hypercalcemia and acute danger

With suppressed PTH, investigate likely malignancy-related PTH peptide or osteolysis, vitamin D excess, granulomatous/lymphoma-related active vitamin D, and medication or endocrine causes. Use blood count, renal findings, bone/constitutional symptoms, and cancer history to select protein studies, imaging, PTH-related peptide, or vitamin D metabolites. [Society for Endocrinology hypercalcemia guidance](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/4277/emergency-guidance_acute-hypercalcaemia-in-adults.pdf)

Confusion, significant dehydration, AKI, arrhythmia, or severe hypercalcemia requires hospital treatment. Rehydrate with isotonic fluid according to volume status and cardiac/renal reserve; loop diuretics are not routine calcium-lowering treatment in a volume-depleted patient. For hypercalcemia of malignancy, the Endocrine Society recommends an IV bisphosphonate or denosumab; severe disease may require short-term calcitonin alongside antiresorptive treatment. Limit calcitonin to 48-72 hours because response wanes. Renal function, the mechanism, and risk of subsequent hypocalcemia affect treatment choice. Treating the cancer remains essential. [Endocrine Society malignancy-associated hypercalcemia](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/hypercalcemia)

### Separate acute hypocalcemia from chronic hypoparathyroidism

Hypocalcemia can cause perioral tingling, paresthesias, cramps, carpopedal spasm, seizures, laryngospasm, and QT prolongation. Signs elicited on examination are not reliable enough to replace measurement and clinical assessment. Severe symptoms or electrical instability require monitored IV calcium treatment and correction of the cause. Obtain magnesium, phosphate, renal function, and PTH when feasible without delaying emergency care. Calcium gluconate and calcium chloride contain different amounts of elemental calcium and have different access precautions; they are not equal-volume substitutes. [Society for Endocrinology acute hypocalcemia guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5314808/)

Persistent low calcium with inadequate PTH suggests hypoparathyroidism, commonly after neck surgery but also from autoimmune or genetic causes. The revised ESE guideline defines chronic postsurgical disease after more than twelve months, acknowledging possible later recovery. Conventional care uses activated vitamin D and adequate calcium intake, with supplements when needed. Aim to control symptoms while keeping calcium in the lower reference range or slightly below and avoiding hypercalciuria, excess phosphate, and kidney injury. A high-normal serum calcium is not automatically the safest target. [ESE 2025 chronic treatment pathway](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/193/5/G83/8321487)

For stable chronic hypoparathyroidism, ESE recommends serum calcium, phosphate, magnesium, and renal assessment about every 3-6 months and every 1-2 weeks after treatment changes. With conventional treatment, assess 24-hour urine calcium about every 1-2 years; symptoms or instability require closer review. [ESE monitoring recommendations](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/193/5/G83/8321487)

Persistent symptoms or complications despite optimized conventional care warrant specialist consideration of PTH replacement. The U.S. palopegteriparatide label covers adult hypoparathyroidism but was not studied for acute postsurgical disease. Its initiation, supplement adjustment, and interruption plan require close calcium monitoring; it is not emergency calcium rescue. [Current palopegteriparatide labeling](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/216490s011lbl.pdf)

### Distinguish adrenal failure from loss of pituitary stimulation

Cortisol supports vascular responsiveness, fuel availability, and the stress response. In primary adrenal insufficiency, the adrenal cortex fails despite increased ACTH; aldosterone deficiency can produce salt loss, volume depletion, hyperkalemia, and elevated renin. ACTH excess contributes to hyperpigmentation. In central adrenal insufficiency, pituitary or hypothalamic stimulation is inadequate; aldosterone is usually preserved because its principal control is not ACTH. Thus, absence of hyperkalemia or pigmentation does not exclude dangerous cortisol deficiency. [NIDDK adrenal disease mechanisms](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/adrenal-insufficiency-addisons-disease/symptoms-causes)

Ask about fatigue, loss, GI symptoms, salt craving, orthostasis, hypoglycemia, and declining function. Illness, surgery, or poor intake may precipitate crisis. Autoimmune primary disease can accompany thyroid disease or type 1 diabetes; infection, hemorrhage, infiltration, and drugs also cause it. Pituitary injury, surgery/radiation, apoplexy, or hypophysitis suggest central disease. Always assess exogenous glucocorticoid suppression.

In a stable patient, pair an appropriately timed morning cortisol with ACTH and the clinical context. Follow the assay-specific pathway for an intermediate result, often using corticotropin stimulation. A universal historical stimulated-cortisol cutoff is unsafe across newer assays. Recent central disease can retain an initially responsive adrenal gland, so a reassuring stimulation response may not resolve a strongly suggestive pituitary presentation. Acute illness, estrogen-related binding changes, and current glucocorticoids also affect interpretation. [NIDDK adrenal diagnostic testing](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/adrenal-insufficiency-addisons-disease/diagnosis)

After confirmation, investigate autoimmune or structural causes. Replace glucocorticoids and, with aldosterone deficiency, mineralocorticoids; central and glucocorticoid-induced disease generally do not need fludrocortisone. Monitor symptoms, weight, pressure/orthostasis, electrolytes, and overreplacement. Do not increase steroids merely to normalize ACTH. [Endocrine Society adrenal care](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-adrenal-insufficiency)

Typical adult primary-adrenal replacement is hydrocortisone **15-25 mg orally per day in two or three doses**, largest on awakening. With confirmed aldosterone deficiency, fludrocortisone commonly starts at **50-100 mcg orally daily**, adjusted to clinical status, electrolytes, and renin. These are maintenance doses, not crisis treatment; pregnancy and children require separate plans.

### Make steroid discontinuation a planned clinical decision

Glucocorticoids suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis; features of steroid excess can coexist with inadequate endogenous cortisol after withdrawal. Establish preparation, route, dose, duration, repeated courses/injections, and interactions, including outside prescriptions and combined inhaled/topical exposure. Tapering must also prevent recurrence of the treated disease.

The 2024 joint guideline generally does not require an endocrine taper or adrenal testing for a single course shorter than three to four weeks. For longer treatment, attempt withdrawal only when the underlying disease allows it; slow down as dosing approaches physiologic replacement. Withdrawal symptoms, disease relapse, and true adrenal insufficiency overlap and need contextual assessment. Once at a physiologic dose and aiming to stop, clinical tapering or morning cortisol assessment can be used. Its recovery guide is above 10 mcg/dL for recovery, 5-10 for continued physiologic dosing with later retesting, and below 5 for continued dosing with reassessment after months. Sampling requires the guideline's medication-hold and assay instructions, not a random value taken after a dose. These are **recovery thresholds during withdrawal**, not universal diagnostic cutoffs for every adrenal disorder. [ESE/Endocrine Society glucocorticoid guideline](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/glucocorticoid-induced-adrenal-insufficiency)

Physiologic replacement is approximately hydrocortisone 15-25 mg/day or prednisone 4-6 mg/day. For planned recovery testing, collect cortisol at 8-9 a.m. after a clinician-supervised hold of at least 24 hours; dexamethasone requires a different plan. Never improvise a steroid hold during illness. [Joint guideline, recovery-testing rationale](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae250)

**Clinical Case: Interrupted steroids.** After months of prednisone for arthritis, a patient misses refills. Three days later, gastroenteritis prevents intake and hypotension develops: treat possible adrenal crisis and transfer. Later reconcile access, sick-day actions, disease control, and adrenal recovery.

### Prevent and treat adrenal crisis without waiting for confirmation

Suspect crisis with otherwise unexplained hypotension or shock, vomiting, abdominal pain, hypoglycemia, or severe illness in a patient with known or possible cortisol deficiency. Hyponatremia can occur in both primary and central disease; hyperkalemia is not required. Draw cortisol/ACTH before treatment only if this does not delay care. For an adult with suspected crisis, give hydrocortisone 100 mg parenterally immediately, followed in hospital by 200 mg over 24 hours by infusion or divided dosing, alongside resuscitation and treatment of the precipitant. Children require age/body-size-specific dosing. [Endocrine Society crisis treatment](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-adrenal-insufficiency)

Arrange emergency transfer, isotonic fluid according to hemodynamics and comorbidities, glucose correction when needed, and repeated electrolyte/glucose monitoring. An IM dose can be used when IV access is not yet available; do not withhold treatment while arranging access or diagnostic testing. Persistent vomiting means oral stress tablets may not be absorbed. [NICE adrenal emergency care](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG243/chapter/recommendations)

Provide written sick-day instructions, medical identification, an emergency injection kit, and hands-on training. Plan for fever, procedures, trauma, and vomiting. Teach continued replacement despite missed meals and urgent action when oral doses cannot be retained. [NIDDK replacement and illness planning](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/adrenal-insufficiency-addisons-disease/treatment)

For adults with glucocorticoid-induced insufficiency or unconfirmed recovery, the joint guideline's minor-illness example increases hydrocortisone below 40 mg/day to **40 mg/day orally in three doses**, often 20/10/10 mg. Continue for 2-5 days until well, or through antibiotic treatment. Someone already at or above that dose usually needs no increase for minor stress. Hemodynamic instability, persistent vomiting/diarrhea, or inability to absorb tablets requires parenteral coverage and urgent assessment, not repeated ineffective oral doses. [Joint guideline, Table 8](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae250)

### Test suspected cortisol excess for the right reason

Cushing syndrome means glucocorticoid excess; Cushing disease specifically means a pituitary ACTH-secreting cause. Review exogenous exposure first. Progressive proximal weakness, easy bruising, broad violaceous striae, osteoporosis unusual for age, or a child's slowing height growth with increasing weight is more discriminatory than obesity alone. Diabetes, hypertension, infection, thromboembolic risk, mood changes, and reproductive disturbance may accompany the syndrome. [Endocrine Society clinical overview](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/cushings-syndrome-and-cushing-disease)

Choose a validated initial test: two late-night salivary cortisol samples, at least two 24-hour urinary free cortisol collections, or a 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test. Random cortisol and ACTH do not screen for Cushing syndrome. Shift work, pregnancy, renal dysfunction, physiologic hypercortisolism, estrogen, and drugs altering dexamethasone handling affect interpretation. Pregnancy favors urinary testing rather than DST; severe renal failure makes urinary testing less suitable. Confirm an abnormal result with endocrine assessment before ACTH-directed localization; an incidental MRI lesion does not establish the source. [Endocrine Society diagnostic pathway](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/diagnosis-of-cushing-syndrome)

Treat the source, often surgically, with specialist medical/radiation options as needed. Manage infection, glycemia, pressure, bone health, thrombosis, and psychological effects concurrently. Successful treatment may cause temporary adrenal insufficiency requiring replacement and recovery testing. Continue biochemical recurrence surveillance even after appearance improves. [Endocrine Society Cushing treatment guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/treatment-of-cushing-syndrome)

### Recognize aldosterone excess even when potassium is normal

Aldosterone excess causes sodium retention and renin suppression, with potassium loss in more pronounced disease. Many patients with primary aldosteronism have normal potassium. The 2025 Endocrine Society guideline conditionally suggests screening all people with hypertension, with implementation shaped by resources and expertise. Screen with aldosterone and renin, interpreted together and as an assay-specific ratio. Correct hypokalemia and review sodium intake, collection conditions and interfering medicines; unsafe medication withdrawal is not required simply to obtain a test. A ratio is not meaningful without considering the actual concentrations.

Collect the screen in the morning with the patient seated, without restricting sodium in the preceding days. Hypokalemia can falsely lower aldosterone and cause a false-negative result; correct it and repeat when warranted. Interpret beta-blocker-related renin suppression and MRA/diuretic effects using the assay and a safe medication plan. [Endocrine Society screening technical remarks](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-aldosteronism-2)

Further suppression testing is selective, not obligatory after every positive screen. If surgery is contemplated, CT plus adrenal venous sampling usually distinguishes lateralizing from bilateral disease; an incidental unilateral nodule alone is inadequate. Unilateral surgery or mineralocorticoid receptor blockade is selected according to lateralization, patient preference and candidacy. Monitor potassium, renal function and blood pressure during medical treatment. [Endocrine Society 2025 aldosteronism pathway](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-aldosteronism-2)

### Evaluate catecholamine symptoms and adrenal masses deliberately

Consider pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma with episodic headache, sweating, palpitations or hypertension, a compatible mass, hereditary risk, or unusual catecholamine-related cardiovascular events; the full cluster is unnecessary. Test plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines. Posture/rest, illness, and drugs affect false positives. Interpret mild elevations and establish a specialist plan before localization or invasive procedures.

For a functioning tumor, preoperative preparation includes alpha blockade and volume planning. Add a beta blocker for tachycardia only after adequate alpha blockade when indicated; beta blockade first can worsen vasoconstriction. Discuss genetic evaluation and follow-up because hereditary and recurrent disease matter. A suspected catecholamine crisis requires monitored emergency care, not empiric outpatient treatment of palpitations. [Endocrine Society pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/99/6/1915/2537399)

An adrenal incidentaloma raises two separate questions: is it hormonally active, and is its imaging concerning for malignancy? ESE 2023 considers a homogeneous lesion with unenhanced CT attenuation at or below 10 HU benign without further imaging; this rule requires the correct CT technique and does not mean skip hormone assessment. Most patients undergo an overnight dexamethasone suppression assessment, with appropriate frailty/life-expectancy exceptions. Metanephrine testing depends on whether imaging is typical of a benign adenoma; aldosterone testing is indicated with hypertension or unexplained hypokalemia. Mild autonomous cortisol secretion is not synonymous with overt Cushing syndrome. Indeterminate findings need an individualized specialist plan. **Do not biopsy a possible pheochromocytoma before appropriate exclusion and specialist review.** [ESE adrenal incidentaloma guidance](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejendo/lvad066)

After the 1-mg overnight DST, cortisol at or below 1.8 mcg/dL (50 nmol/L) excludes autonomous secretion in this framework. Above that level without overt Cushing features, evaluate for mild autonomous cortisol secretion: confirm ACTH independence and account for test confounders. Measure metanephrines for lesions not typical of a benign adenoma, including unenhanced attenuation above 10 HU; absent an unenhanced CT, do not assume the imaging-based exemption applies.

### Connect multiple endocrine tumors without assuming a syndrome

Young-onset, multigland or familial disease should prompt consideration of inherited syndromes. MEN1 commonly involves parathyroid, pituitary and gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors; one finding can create a reason to investigate the family pattern and arrange genetic counseling. [NIDDK MEN1 overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/multiple-endocrine-neoplasia-type-1)

MEN2 is associated with RET-related medullary thyroid cancer and pheochromocytoma; parathyroid disease is characteristic of MEN2A rather than MEN2B. Mucosal neuromas and a marfanoid habitus can suggest MEN2B. Assess for pheochromocytoma before relevant elective surgery in a suspected syndrome. Surveillance and preventive surgery depend on the specific inherited variant and specialist risk assessment, not the acronym alone. [NCI MEN2 clinical genetics summary](https://www.cancer.gov/publications/pdq/information-summaries/genetics/men2-hp-pdq)

### Recognize pituitary disease from several axes and the visual examination

The anterior pituitary regulates thyroid, adrenal, gonadal, and growth axes; posterior vasopressin regulates water balance. Sellar lesions cause excess, deficiencies, stalk effects, headache, or visual compression. Ask about reproductive/sexual change, hyponatremia, polyuria, hand/shoe growth, visual fields, postpartum hemorrhage with lactation failure, surgery/radiation, and checkpoint therapy. [Endocrine Society hypopituitarism overview](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/hypopituitarism)

Pair target hormones with their regulators rather than assume that a normal TSH excludes pituitary hypothyroidism. Low free T4 with inappropriately low/normal TSH requires central and illness/drug assessment. Evaluate adrenal reserve before starting levothyroxine when central disease is possible; provide glucocorticoid coverage if evaluation cannot safely precede replacement. Thyroid treatment can worsen unrecognized cortisol deficiency. Central thyroid replacement is monitored with free T4 and clinical context rather than a TSH target alone. Multiple deficiencies require coordinated replacement and ongoing surveillance after surgery or irradiation. [Endocrine Society hypopituitarism replacement pathway](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/hormone-replacement-in-hypopituitarism)

Evaluate incidental pituitary lesions with dedicated imaging as indicated, hormone assessment, and vision testing according to symptoms and optic proximity. Small lesions need not explain fatigue. The 2025 Pituitary Society statement individualizes assessment of microlesions, macrolesions, and cysts; new visual or endocrine deterioration requires prompt review. [Pituitary Society incidentaloma consensus](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-025-01134-8)

### Interpret prolactin elevation before diagnosing a prolactinoma

Prolactin excess causes reproductive/sexual dysfunction and sometimes galactorrhea, which is neither necessary nor specific. Pregnancy, hypothyroidism, kidney disease, chest stimulation, and dopamine-blocking drugs can raise prolactin. Repeat mild unexpected elevations and review context; do not abruptly stop essential antipsychotics. Persistent unexplained elevation or mass effects warrant pituitary evaluation. [NIDDK prolactinoma assessment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/prolactinoma)

With clinical-biochemical discordance, ask about macroprolactin, which may elevate the result without typical effects. Conversely, modest prolactin with a large lesion may reflect the high-dose hook effect; request dilution testing. Stalk compression can raise prolactin without a lactotroph tumor.

Cabergoline is commonly used to lower prolactin and shrink a prolactinoma. Surgery is an appropriate option for selected patients depending on anatomy, treatment response, tolerability and preference, rather than a universal last resort. Monitor biochemical and tumor response, reproductive recovery, mood/impulse-control effects and relevant cardiac safety. Pregnancy planning needs specialist coordination; restoration of fertility can precede an expected menstrual change. [Pituitary Society 2023 prolactinoma consensus](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-023-00886-5)

### Confirm hypogonadism and identify its level before replacement

In men, low libido, reduced spontaneous erections, infertility, small testes, reduced body hair, low bone mass or otherwise unexplained anemia can prompt evaluation. Fatigue alone is nonspecific. The Endocrine Society requires compatible symptoms/signs and consistently low testosterone, confirmed with repeat morning fasting testing using an appropriate assay. Consider SHBG/free testosterone when binding changes make total testosterone misleading. LH/FSH help distinguish primary testicular failure from central suppression; assess reversible illness, medication effects, nutritional status and pituitary disease. [Endocrine Society male hypogonadism guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy)

Testosterone suppresses spermatogenesis; discuss fertility before prescribing. Assess hematocrit, prostate risk and monitoring preferences, severe untreated sleep apnea, recent major cardiovascular events, and other contraindications. Follow response, concentrations, hematocrit, pressure, and adverse effects. FDA labeling warns of increased blood pressure; revised cardiovascular labeling does not remove risk. Testosterone is not general treatment for aging, weight gain, or low energy. [FDA testosterone safety update](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-issues-class-wide-labeling-changes-testosterone-products)

With amenorrhea or infertility, first assess pregnancy, medicines, energy availability, and reproductive history. High gonadotropins with low sex steroids suggest primary failure; low/inappropriately normal gonadotropins suggest central or functional suppression. Interpret by cycle, age, and exogenous hormones. Address bone health, fertility, and other pituitary axes; use reproductive/lifespan pathways.

### Look beyond isolated symptoms for growth-hormone and water-balance disease

Suspect acromegaly with progressive ring/shoe growth, facial change, sweating, arthralgia, carpal tunnel, apnea, hypertension, or diabetes; compare prior photographs. Start with IGF-I, not fluctuating random GH. Confirm abnormal/equivocal results with specialist assay-aware testing before attributing them to MRI findings. Surgery, medicine, or radiation requires continuing comorbidity care. [NIDDK acromegaly overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/acromegaly)

The acromegaly consensus permits diagnosis from characteristic features and clearly high age-adjusted IGF-I without mandatory glucose suppression testing. Equivocal results need repeat validated measurement and sometimes OGTT, accounting for IGF-I/GH confounders. Suppression thresholds are assay- and population-dependent. [Acromegaly diagnostic consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10837217/)

True polyuria means excessive urine volume, not merely frequency. Assess measured output, glucose, sodium, calcium, potassium, renal function and urine concentration. Vasopressin deficiency, formerly central diabetes insipidus, and renal resistance can produce large volumes of dilute urine; primary polydipsia and solute diuresis are alternatives. Normal sodium does not exclude a defect when thirst and water access compensate. Formal water-deprivation or stimulated-copeptin testing requires specialist supervision; instructing a patient to stop drinking at home can be dangerous. [NIDDK diabetes insipidus evaluation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/diabetes-insipidus)

Desmopressin replacement needs a fluid/sodium plan: excess water retention can cause hyponatremia, while omitted replacement or lost water access can rapidly cause dehydration and hypernatremia. Hospitalization, fasting, cognitive impairment, and vomiting are high-risk transitions. Communicate the diagnosis and replacement route/timing; correct severe sodium disturbance in monitored care, not through unsupervised dose changes. [AVP-deficiency safety](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/scplexuf/guidelines-for-the-inpatient-management-of-cranial-diabetes-insipidus.pdf)

### Recognize pituitary apoplexy and close the endocrine safety loop

Sudden severe headache with vomiting, visual loss, diplopia, ophthalmoplegia, altered consciousness or hypotension can represent pituitary apoplexy, sometimes without a previously known tumor. Consider subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis and other neurologic emergencies concurrently. Arrange emergency assessment, hemodynamic stabilization, urgent pituitary imaging and endocrine/neurosurgical involvement. Potential acute ACTH failure makes glucocorticoid treatment lifesaving in an unstable patient; diagnostic imaging must not delay resuscitation. Definitive surgical decisions depend on visual and neurologic findings after stabilization. [Society for Endocrinology apoplexy guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5314810/)

| Presentation | Immediate concern and disposition |
| --- | --- |
| Hypotension/vomiting with possible cortisol deficiency | Treat suspected adrenal crisis and transfer; do not await cortisol confirmation |
| Hypocalcemia with seizure, laryngospasm or electrical instability | Monitored calcium treatment and cause correction |
| Hypercalcemia with confusion, dehydration, AKI or arrhythmia | Urgent fluid/etiology-directed inpatient management |
| Acute headache with visual/ocular-motor change | Neurologic emergency including pituitary apoplexy |
| Major water loss or sodium disturbance in vasopressin deficiency | Monitored fluids/replacement with frequent sodium reassessment |

See 04A for diabetic crises/hypoglycemia and 04C for thyroid emergencies. Stabilize threatened circulation, brain, airway, or rhythm before completing outpatient diagnosis. Send exposures, last replacement dose, paired results, and treatment to the receiving team. After recovery, assign pending tests, education, and recurrence prevention.
