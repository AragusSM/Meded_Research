# Medivale Family Medicine

## 04C. Thyroid Disorders

ATA adult guidance and U.S. labels inform treatment; ACR TI-RADS is the named ultrasound/biopsy framework for unbiopsied adult nodules. Pregnancy uses ATA 2026, cancer decisions use ATA 2025, and emergency recognition incorporates the 2026 joint thyroid-society statements. These are distinct clinical pathways, not interchangeable thresholds.

### Begin with the clinical problem and the feedback loop

The thyroid produces mostly thyroxine (T4), which tissues convert to the more active triiodothyronine (T3). Pituitary TSH stimulates thyroid hormone production; circulating thyroid hormone feeds back to reduce TSH release. With an intact axis, a small change in free hormone can produce a much larger TSH change. This makes TSH a useful first test for primary thyroid dysfunction, but not a reliable stand-alone test for pituitary disease or every acute illness. [ATA thyroid-function testing](https://www.thyroid.org/thyroid-function-tests/)

Assess hormone availability, mechanism, and structural disease separately: nodules can occur with normal function. Thyrotoxicosis is excess thyroid-hormone exposure from any source; hyperthyroidism is increased thyroid synthesis. Synthesis-blocking treatment helps only the latter.

Ask about weight, bowel function, temperature intolerance, tremor, palpitations, menstrual change, neck/eye symptoms, pregnancy, and thyroid treatment. Review hormone, amiodarone, lithium, immunotherapy, contrast, biotin, and supplements. Examine rhythm, temperature, mentation, hydration, thyroid, nodes, eyes, strength, skin, and reflexes as indicated. Severe cardiopulmonary or neurologic symptoms require urgent care.

Routine population screening is separate from this diagnostic evaluation. USPSTF finds insufficient evidence to determine the balance of benefits and harms of screening asymptomatic, nonpregnant adults for thyroid dysfunction. An I statement is not a recommendation to ignore symptoms, an abnormal examination, or a high-risk medication exposure. [USPSTF thyroid screening statement](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/thyroid-dysfunction-screening)

### Interpret TSH with free hormone and clinical context

Use the laboratory's reference interval, with appropriate age and pregnancy considerations. When TSH is abnormal, free T4 usually supplies the next distinction. With suppressed TSH and normal free T4, total T3 can identify T3-predominant thyrotoxicosis. T3 is not a sensitive test for hypothyroidism, and reverse T3 has no routine role in deciding whether nonspecific fatigue needs thyroid replacement.

| Laboratory pattern | Initial interpretation, before exceptions |
| --- | --- |
| High TSH, low free T4 | Overt primary hypothyroidism |
| High TSH, normal free T4 | Subclinical hypothyroidism if persistent and appropriately measured |
| Low TSH, high free T4 and/or T3 | Overt thyrotoxicosis; determine its source |
| Low TSH, normal free T4 and T3 | Subclinical hyperthyroidism if persistent; exclude transient/drug effects |
| Low free T4 with low or inappropriately normal TSH | Consider central hypothyroidism, illness, drugs, or assay problems |
| High free hormone with nonsuppressed TSH | Check interference, binding abnormalities and treatment timing before rare pituitary/resistance disorders |

[NIDDK thyroid test interpretation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diagnostic-tests/thyroid)

Severe illness can lower T3 and sometimes T4/TSH; recovery may transiently raise TSH. Interpret inpatient testing cautiously and reassess after recovery when appropriate. Coexisting thyroid disease remains possible when the presentation is compelling. [ATA-commissioned laboratory review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10517335/)

### Resolve discordant results before prescribing a new diagnosis

Binding proteins affect total T4 and T3: estrogen can raise them without hyperthyroidism. Free-hormone assays also have limitations, particularly in pregnancy and altered protein states. Compare symptoms, prior values, medications, collection timing, and assay before interpreting a flagged result.

Biotin can produce misleadingly low TSH and high measured thyroid hormone on susceptible platforms. It does not affect every method identically. Ask the laboratory about the assay and an appropriate interruption interval based on dose and clinical circumstances; prescribed high-dose therapy should not be stopped casually. Other interference includes heterophile antibodies, hormone-binding antibodies, and macro-TSH. Repeat testing on an alternative platform or specialized testing may resolve the discrepancy. **Exclude interference before labeling someone with thyroid-hormone resistance or a TSH-secreting tumor.** [ETA 2026 assay-interference guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13482799/)

**Clinical Case: Supplement interference.** A 46-year-old awaiting surgery develops suppressed TSH and elevated free T4 after starting a high-dose hair supplement, without clinical change. Review ingredients and consult the laboratory before scanning or prescribing methimazole. True disease may be asymptomatic, but a plausible interferent warrants confirmation.

### Recognize primary hypothyroidism and establish its cause

Hypothyroidism can cause gradual fatigue, constipation, cold intolerance, dry skin, menstrual disturbance, muscle symptoms, and poor concentration. Bradycardia, delayed reflexes, or nonpitting edema support but do not confirm it. Older adults may present with functional or cognitive change. Consider anemia, depression, sleep disease, and medication effects. [NIDDK hypothyroidism overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/hypothyroidism)

Hashimoto thyroiditis is autoimmune thyroid injury, common in iodine-sufficient settings. TPO antibodies support etiology and predict risk but may coexist with normal function; do not use serial titers to titrate replacement. A goiter is possible, but an atrophic or apparently normal gland does not exclude disease. [ATA Hashimoto thyroiditis](https://www.thyroid.org/hashimotos-thyroiditis/)

Other causes include thyroidectomy, radioiodine, neck irradiation, medications, and temporary or persistent thyroiditis. Clarify whether surgery removed the whole gland or one lobe and whether prior treatment was for cancer, because intentional TSH suppression is a separate specialist indication. Ultrasound is not routinely needed for abnormal thyroid-function tests alone when the neck examination is normal. A palpable nodule, asymmetric enlargement, or compressive symptom creates a structural indication. [ATA hypothyroidism assessment](https://www.thyroid.org/hypothyroidism/)

### Replace missing hormone without creating excess

Levothyroxine supplies T4 for peripheral conversion and is standard replacement. Desiccated thyroid, liothyronine alone, and routine T4/T3 combinations lack consistently superior outcomes. Investigate persistent symptoms rather than automatically adding T3 or inducing biochemical hyperthyroidism. Alternatives belong in selected specialist-supervised care. [ATA thyroid-hormone replacement guideline](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2014.0028)

Dose by the patient's needs, residual thyroid function, body size, age, cardiac risk, and treatment context. The tablet label gives approximately 1.6 mcg/kg/day as full adult replacement, but some patients need less. Older adults and people with coronary disease or arrhythmia risk require lower starting doses and slower escalation. Do not apply a full weight-based dose mechanically in marked obesity, mild disease, frailty, or long-standing severe hypothyroidism. New angina or palpitations after starting treatment requires reassessment.

For known coronary artery disease, the ATA replacement guideline starts levothyroxine at **12.5-25 mcg orally daily**, with gradual adjustment guided by symptoms and TSH. This is a different starting problem from full replacement in a healthy younger adult. [ATA coronary-disease dosing](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2014.0028)

For ordinary adult primary hypothyroidism, check TSH about six to eight weeks after a dose change; once stable, reassess roughly every six to twelve months and with relevant clinical changes. Small adjustments, often 12.5-25 mcg, are preferable to large reflex jumps. The goal is an appropriate TSH, not suppression. Pregnancy, central disease, and cancer-related suppression need different monitoring. [Levothyroxine tablet prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=f347d92a-7726-4165-bc3c-f5b37ec86f9f)

### Investigate apparent replacement failure before escalating

Take levothyroxine consistently. Conventional tablets are usually taken 30-60 minutes before breakfast; an agreed bedtime schedule at least three hours after the evening meal is an alternative. Separate calcium and iron by at least four hours, and review antacids, binding agents, acid-suppressing medicines, food patterns, and formulation changes. Liquid and soft-gel preparations have their own instructions. A patient who takes tablets with an iron-containing breakfast supplement may need a workable schedule rather than a much higher dose. [Tablet administration label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=f347d92a-7726-4165-bc3c-f5b37ec86f9f), [ATA timing guidance](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2014.0028)

Check missed doses, refill gaps, storage, and bottle strength without accusation; investigate malabsorption, including celiac disease, when indicated. Catch-up dosing before testing can raise free T4 while TSH reflects earlier underexposure. Record collection timing before pursuing rare tumors.

**Clinical Case: An absorption interaction.** A 68-year-old's previously stable TSH rises after calcium is started for a fracture. Correct administration interactions before escalating levothyroxine. Investigate persistent fatigue once TSH normalizes; chronic overreplacement increases atrial-fibrillation and bone-loss risk, especially in older adults. [ATA replacement and treatment-response guidance](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2014.0028)

### Confirm subclinical hypothyroidism and individualize treatment

Subclinical hypothyroidism is a biochemical pattern, not proof that fatigue is thyroid-mediated. Confirm persistent elevated TSH with normal free T4, considering illness recovery, drugs, assay interference, and age-specific interpretation. Assess TPO antibodies when they will help predict progression or clarify etiology. The active AACE/ATA adult framework supports considering replacement when TSH persistently exceeds 10 mIU/L; below that, symptoms, antibodies, progression, cardiovascular context, age, and treatment risk guide an individualized decision. Pregnancy uses the separate 2026 pathway.

Observation needs planned retesting and symptom-triggered review. For a symptom-directed trial, define benefit after TSH normalizes; persistent nonspecific symptoms alone do not justify continued dose escalation. [AACE/ATA adult hypothyroidism guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23246686/)

### Do not miss central hypothyroidism

Low free T4 should normally stimulate TSH. When TSH is low, normal, or insufficiently elevated for that degree of hormone deficiency, consider hypothalamic/pituitary disease after excluding confounding factors. Pituitary surgery, irradiation, trauma, a mass, severe postpartum hemorrhage, headache, visual-field symptoms, or other hormone deficits can supply the clue. A reflex laboratory pathway that stops after a normal TSH can miss the diagnosis.

Assess other pituitary functions and obtain appropriate imaging with endocrine involvement. **Establish adequate cortisol secretion, or provide glucocorticoid coverage when indicated, before starting thyroid replacement if adrenal insufficiency is possible.** Increasing metabolism without adequate cortisol can precipitate crisis. Follow replacement primarily with free T4 and clinical status, not a TSH target that the damaged axis cannot provide. Routine TRH stimulation is not required to distinguish every central case. [ETA central hypothyroidism guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6198777/)

### Separate increased synthesis from hormone leakage

Thyrotoxicosis causes weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, anxiety, proximal weakness, frequent stools, and tachyarrhythmia, with cardiac and skeletal complications. Older adults may instead show anorexia, withdrawal, weakness, or atrial fibrillation. Confirm hormone excess while assessing arrhythmia or heart failure directly. [NIDDK thyrotoxicosis overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/hyperthyroidism)

Graves disease involves stimulating TSH-receptor antibodies, producing diffuse hormone synthesis and often a diffuse goiter. TRAb or TSI testing can establish the cause in an appropriate biochemical setting. Orbitopathy or pretibial dermopathy supports Graves, but neither is necessary, and simple lid retraction is not equivalent to inflammatory eye disease. Autonomous nodules produce hormone without normal TSH regulation; their focal or multinodular pattern differs from diffuse stimulation. [ATA Graves disease](https://www.thyroid.org/graves-disease/)

| Mechanism | Useful discriminating evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Graves disease | TSH-receptor antibodies; diffuse uptake and increased vascularity when imaged |
| Toxic adenoma or multinodular autonomy | Focal or patchy functioning regions, often with suppression of surrounding tissue |
| Destructive thyroiditis | Stored hormone leaks; uptake is low rather than increased synthesis |
| Exogenous thyroid hormone | Suppressed uptake; medication history and selected thyroglobulin interpretation help |
| Recent iodine load | Can alter hormone production and suppress tracer uptake, complicating interpretation |

Choose etiologic testing rather than ordering every modality. Radionuclide uptake/scintigraphy is useful when the cause remains uncertain or autonomy is suspected, but pregnancy excludes its routine use. Recent iodine exposure can make a low-uptake study misleading. Ultrasound characterizes structure and vascularity; it does not directly measure hormone synthesis. [NIDDK thyroid testing](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diagnostic-tests/thyroid) [ATA etiologic evaluation](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2016.0229)

### Treat Graves disease with a sustainable plan

Beta blockers relieve tachycardia and tremor while etiologic treatment takes effect. Assess asthma, conduction disease, pressure, and heart failure; unstable cardiac disease requires monitored dosing. Choose medication, radioiodine, or surgery according to disease, eye involvement, pregnancy plans, goiter, access, and preference.

Methimazole is generally preferred when a nonpregnant adult chooses antithyroid medication. It inhibits new synthesis, so improvement is not instantaneous. ATA recommends free T4 and total T3 assessment about 2-6 weeks after starting, with timing adapted to severity. TSH may remain suppressed for months and should not alone drive early dose escalation. A conventional initial course is often 12-18 months; selected patients continue low-dose treatment longer. Reassess antibodies and the clinical course before stopping. [ATA treatment and monitoring](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2016.0229)

ATA's rough initial methimazole dose guide is 5-10 mg orally daily for free T4 1-1.5 times the upper reference limit, 10-20 mg daily at 1.5-2 times, and 30-40 mg daily at 2-3 times. Clinical severity, T3, gland size, adverse effects, and subsequent tests modify the choice. These are nonpregnant adult Graves doses, not pregnancy, thyroiditis, or storm regimens.

Radioiodine acts gradually and often causes hypothyroidism; pregnancy and breastfeeding contraindicate it, and significant active eye disease may favor alternatives. Surgery offers rapid definitive control, especially for compression or suspected cancer, but risks nerve and parathyroid injury and requires experienced care. Discuss lifelong replacement and arrange biochemical follow-up. [ATA hyperthyroidism treatment overview](https://www.thyroid.org/hyperthyroidism/)

### Teach antithyroid-drug danger signs explicitly

Obtain baseline blood count/differential and liver tests as indicated. Fever or sore throat on methimazole or PTU requires stopping doses pending urgent assessment and a blood count for agranulocytosis. A normal baseline count does not exclude a later abrupt event; never wait for scheduled thyroid testing.

Methimazole can also cause hepatic injury, rash, and rarely vasculitis; jaundice, dark urine, marked abdominal symptoms, hematuria, or pulmonary symptoms need prompt evaluation. Confirmed serious reactions require specialist selection of another treatment strategy rather than an automatic unsupervised switch between related drugs. [Methimazole prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=289ec852-176e-41aa-9f9a-b1f9b4196771)

PTU has a boxed warning for severe liver injury, including liver failure. Its selected roles include early pregnancy and some severe thyrotoxicosis settings; it is not generally preferred for routine adult treatment merely because it also reduces T4-to-T3 conversion. Routine liver testing cannot guarantee prevention of an abrupt severe reaction, so counseling remains essential. [PTU prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=b0c23a2c-b250-4c0d-9b2a-c1483faa2ca0)

### Address persistent low TSH and eye disease separately

Persistently suppressed TSH with normal free T4 and T3 is not always benign. Under the ATA adult pathway, TSH below 0.1 mIU/L supports treatment in adults at least 65 years old and in those with important cardiac or bone risk or symptoms. Lesser suppression prompts a more individualized decision. Confirm persistence, exclude overreplacement and transient causes, and treat the underlying mechanism rather than the TSH in isolation. [ATA subclinical hyperthyroidism recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2016.0229)

Thyroid eye disease can evolve separately from hormone levels. Ask about pain, pressure, diplopia, light sensitivity, exposure symptoms, and changes in vision. Reduced visual acuity, color desaturation, severe exposure, or concern for optic neuropathy warrants urgent ophthalmology assessment. Smoking cessation, stable thyroid control, and local eye care are important, but immunomodulatory treatment and surgery depend on activity and severity. Normalizing TSH does not itself resolve every orbital problem. [ATA thyroid eye disease](https://www.thyroid.org/thyroid-eye-disease/)

### Follow thyroiditis through its changing phases

In destructive thyroiditis, damaged follicles release stored hormone, then may enter a hypothyroid phase before recovery. Antithyroid synthesis inhibitors do not correct that leakage. Beta blockade may help thyrotoxic symptoms; temporary levothyroxine may be appropriate for symptomatic or significant hypothyroidism. Retest through the transition rather than assuming that the first abnormal phase defines a lifelong state.

Subacute painful thyroiditis often follows an inflammatory illness and can produce anterior neck tenderness with elevated inflammatory markers. Treat pain with an appropriate anti-inflammatory medicine; severe or refractory pain may require a supervised glucocorticoid course. Focal severe pain, fever, toxicity, or an abscess concern requires evaluation for infection rather than routine steroid treatment. [ATA thyroiditis](https://www.thyroid.org/thyroiditis/)

Postpartum thyroiditis is autoimmune destruction in the year after pregnancy, with thyrotoxic and/or hypothyroid phases that can resemble anxiety or depression. Distinguish Graves disease by timeline, examination, antibodies, and testing. Permanent hypothyroidism can follow; reassess recovery biochemically and review temporary replacement under supervision. [ATA postpartum thyroiditis](https://www.thyroid.org/postpartum-thyroiditis/)

**Clinical Case: Changing phases.** After postpartum palpitations, a patient returns with constipation and fatigue. Repeat thyroid testing rather than increasing a beta blocker. Assess mental health and other causes alongside the evolving thyroid pattern.

### Recognize medication-related disease without abruptly withdrawing essential care

Amiodarone alters tests without necessarily causing disease, but can also cause hypothyroidism or thyrotoxicosis. Type 1 reflects increased synthesis, often with Graves or nodular autonomy; type 2 is destructive, and mixed forms occur. Specialists may use thionamides, glucocorticoids, or both. Coordinate with cardiology: stopping this persistent drug does not immediately remove exposure. Overt hypothyroidism often permits continued amiodarone. [ETA amiodarone-associated thyroid dysfunction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5869486/)

Lithium, iodine exposure, and some cancer therapies can also affect thyroid function. Check the actual drug and clinical trajectory. Immune checkpoint treatment can produce thyroiditis, but pituitary inflammation can instead cause central hypothyroidism with adrenal insufficiency. A low free T4 without a compensatory TSH rise should therefore trigger broader endocrine evaluation. Treating the number with levothyroxine alone may miss the more urgent cortisol problem. [Society for Endocrinology checkpoint-inhibitor emergency guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6013692/)

### Evaluate nodules by function, ultrasound risk, and context

A newly recognized nodule needs a focused history/examination, TSH, and appropriate diagnostic ultrasound of the thyroid and cervical nodes. Rapid growth, fixation, hoarseness, abnormal nodes, childhood radiation exposure, or a relevant familial cancer syndrome increases concern. Most nodules are benign; normal TSH does not exclude cancer. A goiter describes enlargement, not its cause or functional state. Dysphagia, dyspnea, or voice change deserves attention even when hormone values are normal. [ATA thyroid nodules](https://www.thyroid.org/thyroid-nodules/)

If TSH is low, scintigraphy can identify autonomy when not contraindicated. A corresponding hyperfunctioning nodule usually does not need cytologic evaluation; ultrasound must still assess other nonfunctioning nodules and cervical nodes. If TSH is normal or high, a radionuclide scan is not the routine first imaging test. Do not biopsy only the largest lesion when another nodule has more concerning features. [ATA nodule evaluation recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4739132/)

ACR TI-RADS scores composition, echogenicity, shape, margins, and echogenic foci. The score and size together determine routine FNA or follow-up, not diameter alone. Use the radiologist's actual category; do not mix ACR labels with thresholds from another system.

| ACR category | Routine FNA threshold | If below FNA size but eligible for ultrasound follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TR1-TR2 | None | No routine TI-RADS follow-up |
| TR3 | At least 2.5 cm | At least 1.5 cm: ultrasound at years 1, 3, and 5 |
| TR4 | At least 1.5 cm | At least 1.0 cm: ultrasound at years 1, 2, 3, and 5 |
| TR5 | At least 1.0 cm | At least 0.5 cm: annual ultrasound for five years |

These are ordinary unbiopsied-adult nodule thresholds, not rules for ignoring suspicious nodes, invasion, symptoms, pediatric disease, or known cancer. A nodule below the listed follow-up threshold ordinarily does not need routine TI-RADS surveillance. [ACR TI-RADS assessment and management](https://edge.sitecorecloud.io/americancoldf5f-acrorgf92a-productioncb02-3650/media/ACR/Files/RADS/TI-RADS/TI-RADS-Ultrasound-Reporting-Template.pdf)

**Example.** A 1.8-cm TR3 nodule with normal TSH and no clinical concerns generally needs surveillance; a 1.2-cm TR5 nodule meets FNA criteria. Assign follow-up and explain symptoms requiring earlier assessment.

### Interpret cytology as the next decision, not the end of follow-up

The Bethesda system distinguishes nondiagnostic, benign, atypia of undetermined significance, follicular neoplasm, suspicious for malignancy, and malignant samples. Nondiagnostic is not benign; it often requires repeat ultrasound-guided sampling or another planned evaluation. Indeterminate cytology may lead to repeat sampling, molecular testing, observation, or diagnostic surgery depending on category, ultrasound, clinical risk, and preferences. Molecular results modify risk rather than supplying infallible yes/no answers. Follicular carcinoma requires assessment of capsular or vascular invasion, which routine FNA cannot establish. [Bethesda 2023 cytopathology system](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37427847/)

After benign biopsy, use the cytology-informed pathway, not the unbiopsied-nodule table. Suspicious ultrasound features, growth, or clinical change warrant reassessment. Focal FDG uptake also needs contextual evaluation; competing cancer prognosis and life expectancy may change investigation's value. [ACR TI-RADS clinical FAQ](https://radssupport.acr.org/support/solutions/articles/11000071474-acr-ti-rads-faq)

### Know what a cancer diagnosis does and does not imply

Papillary and follicular cancers are differentiated follicular-cell cancers, but not every patient needs total thyroidectomy and radioiodine. The ATA 2025 framework permits less extensive surgery and selected active surveillance for appropriately evaluated low-risk disease. Histology, size, extension, nodes, distant spread, patient priorities, and reliable follow-up determine treatment. TSH suppression and postoperative surveillance are risk-adapted specialist decisions, not the target for ordinary hypothyroidism. [ATA 2025 differentiated thyroid cancer guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13090833/)

Medullary cancer arises from C cells and raises different hereditary and biochemical questions, including possible MEN2. Anaplastic cancer and thyroid lymphoma can present through rapid enlargement or compression and require expedited evaluation. These are not interchangeable with an incidental low-risk papillary microcarcinoma. Stridor or threatened airway requires emergency care regardless of the suspected histology. [ATA medullary cancer](https://www.thyroid.org/medullary-thyroid-cancer/) [ATA anaplastic cancer](https://www.thyroid.org/anaplastic-thyroid-cancer/) [ATA thyroid lymphoma](https://www.thyroid.org/primary-thyroid-lymphoma/)

### Use the pregnancy pathway, not nonpregnant cutoffs

Pregnancy changes TSH physiology and binding proteins; use laboratory- and trimester-specific intervals when available. In established overt hypothyroidism, anticipate increased levothyroxine requirements and arrange an early planned adjustment and monitoring. Do not automatically apply the same increase to preconception subclinical disease. Mild abnormalities need confirmation and the current shared-decision pathway, not an assumption that every TSH above 2.5 requires treatment.

For Graves disease requiring medication, PTU is preferred in the first trimester when available. **ATA 2026 makes no preferred-drug recommendation after 16 weeks**, so continued PTU versus switching to methimazole is individualized with specialist input. Use the lowest effective treatment, follow free hormone as well as TSH, and assess TRAb/TSI-related fetal risk even after previous definitive maternal treatment. A positive pregnancy test warrants prompt contact, not unsupervised cessation of all therapy.

During continued antithyroid treatment in pregnancy, check TSH and free T4 as soon as pregnancy is confirmed and every 2-4 weeks thereafter. Distinguish Graves disease from hCG-mediated gestational transient thyrotoxicosis, often associated with hyperemesis and lacking Graves-specific findings; transient disease usually needs supportive care rather than antithyroid medication. Antibody testing helps when the distinction is uncertain. [ATA pregnancy evaluation and monitoring](https://doi.org/10.1177/10507256261445624)

Radionuclide scanning and radioiodine treatment are avoided in pregnancy. Ultrasound and indicated FNA can be used. A cancer diagnosis does not automatically require immediate surgery; urgency depends on disease behavior and maternal/fetal circumstances. [ATA 2026 preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum guideline](https://doi.org/10.1177/10507256261445624)

### Recognize thyroid emergencies clinically

Thyroid storm is decompensated thyrotoxicosis with systemic dysfunction, such as fever, major tachyarrhythmia, heart failure, agitation/delirium, or gastrointestinal/hepatic disturbance. No hormone concentration alone separates it from uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis. Infection, surgery, iodine exposure, or treatment interruption can precipitate deterioration. Arrange emergency transfer and monitored multidisciplinary care; do not wait for a score or send the patient home because the free T4 is only moderately elevated.

Treatment addresses airway/circulation, temperature, the precipitant, hormone synthesis/release, peripheral effects, and possible relative adrenal insufficiency. It commonly includes a thionamide, iodide, glucocorticoid, and carefully selected adrenergic control. Give synthesis blockade before iodide in the usual synthesis-driven pathway. Beta blockade requires particular caution in low-output heart failure or shock; cardiovascular collapse can follow an inappropriate dose. Etiology and contraindications modify the regimen. [2026 joint thyroid-storm consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13506520/)

Myxedema coma is severe decompensated hypothyroidism and need not include literal coma. Think of it with hypothermia, altered mental status, hypoventilation, bradycardia, hypotension, hyponatremia, or hypoglycemia, often after an intercurrent illness or interruption of treatment. TSH may not be markedly high with central disease or severe illness. Hospital treatment includes early glucocorticoid coverage, cautious thyroid replacement usually initially intravenous, respiratory/circulatory support, and treatment of the precipitant. Do not delay care for confirmatory testing or assume thyroid disease explains away coexisting sepsis. [2026 joint myxedema-coma consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13452032/)
