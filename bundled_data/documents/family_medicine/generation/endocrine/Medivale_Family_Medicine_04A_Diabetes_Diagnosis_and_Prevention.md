# Medivale Family Medicine

## 04A. Diabetes Diagnosis and Prevention

ADA 2026 is the main diagnostic/prevention pathway. Adult acute-care principles use the multisociety hyperglycemic-crisis consensus; pediatric and pregnancy protocols remain distinct. Longitudinal treatment, monitoring targets, and complication surveillance follow in 04B.

### Recognize hyperglycemia and decide how urgently to act

Diabetes is sustained hyperglycemia from inadequate insulin secretion, impaired insulin action, or both. Insulin restrains hepatic glucose production and supports fuel use and storage. Excess filtered glucose causes osmotic loss of water and electrolytes. Renal thresholds vary with pregnancy, kidney function, and glucose-wasting medicines; glucosuria alone is not diagnostic.

Polyuria, polydipsia, blurred vision, fatigue, recurrent candidiasis, and unintentional weight loss can prompt diagnostic testing. Catabolic weight loss, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep breathing, dehydration, or altered thinking increases concern for insulin deficiency and a crisis. An older person may present through falls, infection, functional decline, or dehydration rather than report classic thirst. Type 2 diabetes can also be asymptomatic for years. **A relatively well appearance does not establish that the glucose abnormality is mild.** [NIDDK diagnostic overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/tests-diagnosis)

Assess vital signs, mental status, intake, perfusion, breathing, weight change, and access to fluids and medicines. Ask about infection, ischemia, glucocorticoids, antipsychotics, cancer therapy, alcohol, pregnancy possibility, and prior diabetes. For insulin users, establish missed or rationed doses, pump interruption, vomiting, and the last effective basal dose.

Check glucose promptly when clinically indicated, but do not postpone emergency transfer to complete a routine diagnostic panel in an unstable person. Vomiting with ketosis, circulatory compromise, abnormal breathing, or altered consciousness requires hospital assessment. A symptomatic new diagnosis with suspected type 1 diabetes needs an urgent insulin-capable pathway even if the patient is not yet acidotic; waiting several weeks for routine follow-up can be dangerous.

### Apply diagnostic criteria to the right patient and specimen

The following ranges describe nonpregnant individuals under the ADA framework. They are alternative tests that assess different aspects of glycemia, not numbers that must all be abnormal. A1C does not require fasting. Fasting plasma glucose requires at least eight hours without caloric intake, and a diagnostic oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) uses a standardized 75-g load with the specified two-hour sample. Pregnancy testing uses separate protocols.

| Laboratory measure | Prediabetes range | Diabetes threshold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1C | 5.7-6.4% | At least 6.5% |
| Fasting plasma glucose | 100-125 mg/dL | At least 126 mg/dL |
| Two-hour plasma glucose after 75-g OGTT | 140-199 mg/dL | At least 200 mg/dL |

Classic hyperglycemic symptoms or a hyperglycemic crisis plus **random plasma glucose at least 200 mg/dL** establishes diabetes without waiting for routine repeat confirmation. An asymptomatic random value at that level instead needs appropriate confirmatory evaluation; it is not the same clinical situation. Do not confuse a diagnostic cutoff with an individualized treatment target. [ADA 2026 diagnosis and classification](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690183/)

Use validated assays and correct sample handling: delayed processing lowers glucose through cellular consumption. Illness, activity, diet, fasting duration, and medicines affect results. OGTT can reveal postchallenge hyperglycemia missed by fasting testing, but an ordinary meal or uncertain glucose drink is not a substitute.

Before a diagnostic OGTT, ensure at least 150 g carbohydrate daily for the preceding three days, followed by the specified overnight fast. Carbohydrate restriction can distort the response. Check for recent illness, vomiting, or mistimed sampling that could invalidate the test.

A1C should use an NGSP-certified, DCCT-standardized assay. ADA restricts diagnostic point-of-care use to FDA-approved diagnostic devices operated by trained staff in CLIA-certified laboratories performing moderate-complexity or higher testing. Monitoring clearance alone is insufficient. A home meter or CGM may reveal an urgent concern but does not replace laboratory diagnostic criteria in a stable patient.

### Confirm an unexpected result rather than average away the diagnosis

Without unequivocal symptomatic hyperglycemia, promptly obtain two abnormal results: the same test on separate occasions, or different qualifying tests from the same or separate samples. If tests disagree, repeat the one above its diagnostic threshold and assess interference. A lower result on another test does not cancel a reproducibly diagnostic result.

**Clinical Case: Discordant tests.** An asymptomatic 57-year-old has fasting glucose 131 mg/dL and A1C 6.2%. Repeat fasting glucose of 134 mg/dL confirms diabetes. An unconfirmed marginal elevation instead requires follow-up; near-threshold results often merit reassessment within three to six months, sooner for symptoms.

Prediabetes indicates increased risk, not inevitable progression. Higher or rising values, prior gestational diabetes, and other risk factors guide prevention intensity; one cutoff cannot establish personal probability. Record the test, context, confirmation status, and next action.

### Understand when A1C is misleading

A1C reflects red-cell glucose exposure, weighted toward recent exposure. Abrupt insulin deficiency can cause dangerous hyperglycemia before A1C rises; A1C can remain elevated after glucose improves. Its estimated average glucose neither describes daily swings nor excludes hypoglycemia. [NIDDK A1C explanation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diagnostic-tests/a1c-test)

Shortened red-cell survival from hemolysis or recovery after blood loss tends to lower A1C relative to actual glucose exposure. Iron deficiency can raise it; transfusion, erythropoietin treatment, advanced kidney disease, and pregnancy can complicate interpretation. Hemoglobin variants cause method-dependent interference, while some diseases also change cell survival. Check the assay and clinical mechanism rather than applying a race-based correction or assuming every hemoglobin trait invalidates every method. [NGSP interference guidance](https://ngsp.org/factors.asp)

When A1C and glucose are substantially discordant, review sample quality, recent changes, red-cell disorders, drugs, and the assay with the laboratory. Use plasma glucose criteria for diagnosis when the A1C-glycemia relationship is unreliable. Fructosamine and glycated albumin may help selected monitoring problems, but protein turnover and other limitations matter; they are not automatic replacements with interchangeable diagnostic cutoffs. [NIDDK hemoglobin-variant interpretation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/professionals/clinical-tools-patient-management/diabetes/sickle-cell-trait-hemoglobinopathies-diabetes)

### Classify diabetes by mechanism, not age or body size alone

Autoimmune type 1 diabetes involves progressive destruction of pancreatic beta cells and can begin in childhood or adulthood. Absolute or severe insulin deficiency permits lipolysis and ketone production, so insulin is needed not only to lower glucose but to suppress dangerous catabolism. Weight loss and rapid deterioration heighten suspicion; obesity or a family history of type 2 diabetes does not exclude coexisting autoimmune disease. Adult-onset, slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes is often called LADA, but remains autoimmune diabetes. [NIDDK type 1 overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/type-1-diabetes)

Type 2 diabetes combines insulin resistance with inadequate beta-cell function. It occurs across body sizes and is not a failure of willpower. Insulin may be needed temporarily or permanently without reclassification as type 1. Children can develop type 2 diabetes; adults can develop type 1. [NIDDK type 2 overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/type-2-diabetes)

When the phenotype overlaps, standardized islet autoantibodies help assess autoimmune disease. GAD is a common initial antibody, with IA-2 or ZnT8 and other testing selected as appropriate. Insulin antibodies are harder to interpret after exogenous insulin exposure. A negative panel does not always exclude type 1 diabetes; false positives and nonvalidated assays can also mislead. Tests refine classification but must not delay insulin in a catabolic, insulin-deficient patient.

C-peptide reflects endogenous insulin secretion and is interpreted with concurrent glucose, meal timing, kidney function, illness, and treatment context. A value obtained during hypoglycemia or immediately after a hyperglycemic emergency may not represent stable beta-cell reserve. The ADA adult classification pathway advises against testing within two weeks of a hyperglycemic emergency. Do not discontinue insulin on the basis of age, body habitus, or one poorly contextualized value; establish whether severe insulin deficiency is present first.

### Look for a specific cause when the usual labels do not fit

Ketosis-prone diabetes can present with DKA, then show substantial beta-cell reserve and a nonautoimmune phenotype. Treat acutely with insulin and reassess under supervision. Possible later insulin independence never justifies withholding initial treatment; neither DKA nor obesity alone establishes permanent classification.

Monogenic diabetes becomes more likely with very early onset, a compatible multigenerational pattern, or specific syndromic findings. Diabetes diagnosed before six months of age warrants genetic evaluation rather than an automatic type 1 label. Selected GCK variants produce stable mild hyperglycemia, whereas other gene defects have different treatment implications. Genetic counseling and an appropriate test can change care for the patient and relatives; do not assume all familial diabetes is MODY or prescribe a gene-specific treatment before confirming the diagnosis. [NIDDK monogenic diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/monogenic-neonatal-mellitus-mody)

Pancreatitis, pancreatic surgery, cystic fibrosis, hemochromatosis, and other pancreatic disease can impair insulin secretion. Exocrine insufficiency, malnutrition, and impaired glucagon responses may complicate management. Consider steroid-associated hyperglycemia, immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated insulin deficiency, other drug effects, and endocrine hormone excess when history suggests them. A fasting value can miss afternoon steroid-related elevations. Coordinate with the prescribing team rather than abruptly stopping necessary cancer treatment or long-term glucocorticoids.

Hyperglycemia first recognized in pregnancy must be evaluated within the pregnancy pathway. Some patients have previously unrecognized overt diabetes rather than gestational diabetes alone. Pregnancy changes test interpretation, targets, and medication choices; the routine nonpregnant prediabetes table does not define gestational disease. Prior gestational diabetes also matters years later, even if postpartum glucose initially normalized.

### Reassess glucose after illness and pregnancy

Infection, surgery, trauma, and high-dose glucocorticoids can reveal limited metabolic reserve or cause transient stress hyperglycemia. Review prior glucose and a reliable A1C while treating the acute illness. Document diagnostic uncertainty, medication changes, and planned reassessment rather than automatically labeling permanent diabetes or dismissing the elevation. [ADA hospital assessment and continuity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690180)

Prednisone-related hyperglycemia may peak later in the day despite normal fasting glucose or a recently normal A1C. Follow the time-of-day pattern and adjust treatment as steroid doses change. Requirements can fall rapidly during tapering or recovery, causing hypoglycemia. Coordinate necessary steroid changes; do not stop abruptly.

After gestational diabetes, the ADA pathway uses a fasting 75-g OGTT at 4-12 weeks postpartum, interpreted with nonpregnant criteria. Early postpartum A1C can be distorted by pregnancy-related red-cell turnover and delivery blood loss and is less suitable for this assessment. A normal result does not remove future risk: maintain lifelong screening, generally every one to three years, and revisit preconception planning before another pregnancy. Practical follow-through includes arranging the test around infant care and confirming that the result reached the primary care team. [ADA 2026 pregnancy and postpartum care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690181/)

### Make prevention a supported longitudinal intervention

Under the ADA pathway, assess type 2 diabetes risk and test earlier in adults with overweight/obesity and additional risk factors; routine testing otherwise begins at age 35. Normal results generally allow a minimum three-year interval, shortened for symptoms or increased risk. Prediabetes usually merits at least annual reassessment. The preventive chapter separately identifies the USPSTF screening population; do not combine its age range with another organization's eligibility rules. Symptomatic testing is diagnostic, not a screening eligibility debate.

For risk-based adult testing, ADA uses BMI at least 25 kg/m2, or 23 with Asian ancestry, plus another risk factor such as PCOS, hypertension, a first-degree family history, or acanthosis nigricans. In youth, consider testing at puberty or age 10, whichever comes first, with BMI at least the 85th percentile plus an additional risk factor; adult BMI cutoffs do not apply. [ADA screening populations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690183/)

Offer a structured prevention program rather than a brief instruction to lose weight. ADA 2026 supports a sustained initial weight reduction of at least 5-7% in eligible high-risk adults with overweight/obesity, alongside at least 150 minutes weekly of moderate activity. Individualize for frailty, disability, eating-disorder risk, pregnancy, and comorbid illness. A workable plan includes nutrition, activity progression, self-monitoring, problem solving, and ongoing support rather than a promise that one particular diet prevents every case. [ADA 2026 prevention guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690170/)

Tailor prevention support to food preferences, cost, work, sleep, transportation, childcare, mobility, and language. Offer suitable telehealth or in-person programs and confirm enrollment. Weight regain warrants reassessment of barriers and treatment, not blame. [CDC lifestyle change program](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes-prevention/lifestyle-change-program/index.html)

**Example.** At 100 kg, a 5-7% reduction is 5-7 kg, reaching about 93-95 kg. This is a discussion goal, not a guarantee of normal glucose. Also track glycemia, function, blood pressure, lipids, tobacco exposure, and well-being.

Consider metformin for selected adults at high risk, especially with BMI at least 35 kg/m2, age 25-59, higher fasting glucose or A1C, or prior gestational diabetes. It supplements rather than replaces behavioral support. Review kidney function, tolerability, other risks, and periodic B12 assessment, particularly with anemia or neuropathy. Do not treat every isolated borderline value pharmacologically, and distinguish guideline-supported prevention from the product's diabetes-treatment indication. [ADA prevention medication recommendations](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S50/163924/3-Prevention-or-Delay-of-Diabetes-and-Associated)

Choose weight-management and cardiovascular/kidney therapies by their own indications and safety criteria, not a universal prediabetes drug ladder. Maintain lifelong follow-up after gestational diabetes, including completed postpartum testing. Address smoking, blood pressure, and lipids directly.

### Recognize presymptomatic autoimmune diabetes as a different pathway

Type 1 diabetes can be identified before overt hyperglycemia. Stage 1 involves confirmed multiple islet autoantibodies with normoglycemia; stage 2 involves autoimmunity and dysglycemia short of overt diabetes. Stage 3 meets overt diabetes criteria and requires prompt clinical care. A positive antibody result warrants metabolic evaluation, education, and stage-appropriate monitoring, not reassurance that normal fasting glucose excludes future progression. Offer appropriate autoantibody screening to those with family history or known elevated genetic risk under the ADA pathway.

Refer confirmed high-risk findings for specialist staging and discussion of approved treatment or trials. A single antibody does not establish stage; antibody type, persistence, age, and glycemia guide monitoring. Teach thirst, polyuria, weight loss, vomiting, and escalation signs to reduce DKA presentation risk. Type 2 lifestyle advice does not replace this pathway.

Teplizumab is labeled to delay stage 3 onset in stage 2 autoimmune diabetes from age one. Its separate accelerated-approval indication in recently diagnosed patients aged 8-17 with stage 3 disease is to delay the decline in endogenous insulin production, not eliminate insulin treatment. It does not treat type 2 prediabetes. Immunocompromise or active viral infection is a contraindication. The boxed warning requires assessment for EBV/CMV infection, lymphocyte monitoring, and surveillance for reactivation during treatment and for at least two months afterward. Selection also addresses blood counts, liver tests, vaccines, and pregnancy; the two indications do not use identical courses. [FDA teplizumab label, sections 1-5](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/761183s010lbls014lbl.pdf)

### Distinguish ketoacidosis from hyperosmolar illness

Severe insulin deficiency releases fatty acids for hepatic ketone production while counterregulatory hormones increase glucose production. DKA combines diabetes/hyperglycemia, ketonemia, and metabolic acidosis. HHS more often involves enough residual insulin to limit major ketogenesis but not enough to control glucose; osmotic diuresis produces profound water loss, hyperosmolality, and often neurologic dysfunction. Both can occur in type 2 diabetes, and mixed DKA/HHS is important. They are not mutually exclusive boxes determined by age. [Active multisociety adult crisis consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272983/)

For adult DKA, the consensus requires glucose at least 200 mg/dL **or a history of diabetes**, plus beta-hydroxybutyrate at least 3.0 mmol/L (or urine ketones at least 2+), and pH below 7.3 and/or bicarbonate below 18 mmol/L. All three domains are required. Urine strips measure acetoacetate rather than the dominant early ketoacid beta-hydroxybutyrate and can misrepresent both early severity and recovery. A ketone result without acid-base assessment is incomplete.

HHS requires glucose at least 600 mg/dL, hyperosmolality, absence of significant ketonemia, and absence of substantial acidosis. The consensus uses effective osmolality above 300 mOsm/kg or total osmolality above 320, beta-hydroxybutyrate below 3.0 mmol/L (or urine ketones below 2+), and pH at least 7.3 with bicarbonate at least 15 mmol/L. Mild ketonemia can occur. Mixed findings or another cause of acidosis require a broader assessment rather than forcing the patient into a pure HHS label.

Using sodium in mmol/L and glucose/BUN in mg/dL, estimated effective osmolality is **2 x sodium + glucose/18**; estimated total osmolality adds **BUN/2.8**. State the units and formula. Urea contributes to total measured osmolality but not the same sustained effective osmotic gradient as sodium and glucose. Do not use an unconverted glucose value or confuse calculated osmolality with the anion gap.

**Example.** Sodium 148 mmol/L, glucose 720 mg/dL, and BUN 56 mg/dL yield effective osmolality 336 mOsm/kg and total osmolality 356 mOsm/kg. With pH 7.36, bicarbonate 22 mmol/L, and beta-hydroxybutyrate 0.7 mmol/L, the pattern is hyperosmolar rather than ketoacidotic. Dehydration or altered thinking still requires emergency treatment and full assessment.

### Do not miss ketoacidosis because glucose is only modestly elevated

SGLT2-associated DKA can occur with glucose below the levels expected in a classic presentation. Reduced intake, dehydration, acute illness, insulin reduction, and pregnancy can also alter the glucose picture. A patient with diabetes taking an SGLT2 inhibitor who develops vomiting, abdominal pain, malaise, or abnormal breathing needs ketone and acid-base assessment, not reassurance from a near-normal glucose reading. Stop the implicated medicine and arrange urgent evaluation when ketoacidosis is suspected. [FDA SGLT2 ketoacidosis warning](https://www.fda.gov/media/94822/download)

Other explanations for an elevated anion gap or ketones include starvation, alcohol-related ketoacidosis, lactate accumulation, kidney failure, and toxins. Mixed acid-base disorders can obscure a typical pH. Assess infection and ischemia as potential triggers without assuming every leukocytosis proves bacterial infection. Obtain glucose, electrolytes, renal measures, ketones, venous blood gas, and osmolality or other testing as appropriate; ECG and trigger-directed studies often matter. Do not delay stabilization while investigating a rare cause.

### Understand the priorities of hospital treatment

Assess DKA severity from acid-base disturbance, ketones, mental status, comorbidity, and monitoring needs, not glucose alone. Stupor, shock, severe acidosis, a critical precipitant, or substantial HHS overlap can require intensive care without meeting every severe-category criterion.

| Clinical picture | Implication for monitored care |
| --- | --- |
| Uncomplicated mild DKA with preserved alertness and stable physiology | Selected observation/regular-unit pathways may be appropriate when frequent assessment and protocol treatment are available. |
| Greater acidosis, drowsiness, or increasing treatment/monitoring needs | Consider intermediate or step-down care according to the full assessment and local resources. |
| Severe acidosis, stupor/coma, circulatory compromise, or critical precipitant | Intensive care assessment and treatment. |

Selected uncomplicated mild/moderate DKA may receive frequent subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin under a monitored hospital protocol, never an unsupervised home regimen. Severe/complicated DKA and HHS require different pathways. Safe placement depends on staffing, serial laboratories, oral tolerance, and prompt recognition of deterioration. [Adult consensus severity and care-setting guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272983/)

DKA/HHS needs protocol-based monitored care. Restore perfusion with appropriate IV crystalloid, assess potassium and renal function, give insulin at the correct stage, replace electrolytes, and treat the precipitating problem. Older adults and patients with HF or kidney failure need individualized fluid delivery and frequent reassessment rather than an indiscriminate large-volume prescription.

Selected adult protocol anchors are below; they are not outpatient orders or pediatric/pregnancy regimens. [Multisociety adult treatment pathway](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00125-024-06183-8)

| Decision | Adult consensus anchor |
| --- | --- |
| Initial fluid without cardiac/renal compromise | Isotonic saline or balanced crystalloid, 500-1,000 mL/hour for the first 2-4 hours; then reassess |
| DKA or mixed DKA/HHS, once potassium permits | IV short-acting insulin, commonly 0.1 units/kg/hour |
| Glucose below 250 mg/dL during DKA treatment | Add 5-10% dextrose and reduce insulin to 0.05 units/kg/hour; continue until ketoacidosis resolves |
| HHS without acidosis, with absent/mild ketonemia | IV insulin 0.05 units/kg/hour; bicarbonate below 18 mmol/L or significant ketosis changes the treatment branch |
| Transition from IV insulin | Give subcutaneous basal insulin 1-2 hours before stopping the infusion |

Check bedside glucose every 1-2 hours and relevant laboratories about every four hours; reassess sooner when unstable. HHS osmolality should fall by only 3-8 mOsm/kg/hour. Fluid overload risk requires smaller reassessed volumes, not automatic application of the first row.

Serum potassium may initially be normal or high despite substantial whole-body depletion. Osmotic losses remove potassium, while insulin deficiency and acid-base changes shift it between compartments. Insulin and recovery can then lower serum potassium rapidly. Under the adult consensus, potassium below 3.5 mmol/L requires replacement and delaying insulin until it rises above 3.5. This is a safety sequence requiring monitoring, not a reason to leave a patient untreated outside hospital.

As glucose falls during DKA treatment, dextrose-containing fluid allows insulin to continue clearing ketones without inducing hypoglycemia. A normalizing glucose does not establish resolution of acidosis. Bicarbonate and phosphate are not routinely administered to every patient; selected indications and harms require the protocol. In HHS, avoid overly rapid osmotic shifts and monitor neurologic status, fluids, electrolytes, glucose, and osmolality. The pediatric pathway has different fluid and cerebral-injury considerations.

Resolution of adult DKA requires beta-hydroxybutyrate below 0.6 mmol/L plus recovery of acid-base status (venous pH at least 7.3 or bicarbonate at least 18 mmol/L); glucose should ideally also be below 200 mg/dL. Neither anion-gap closure alone nor a negative urine strip is the preferred endpoint. Persistent hyperchloremic acidosis after treatment can confuse interpretation. Transition to subcutaneous insulin requires an appropriate overlap so effective insulin delivery does not lapse. [Current ADA hospital-care framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690180)

### Recognize and treat hypoglycemia without creating another emergency

Insulin, sulfonylureas, and other insulin secretagogues are important causes of treatment-related hypoglycemia. Missed food, unexpected activity, alcohol, kidney dysfunction, dosing errors, and impaired awareness raise risk. Sweating, hunger, tremor, and palpitations may precede confusion, behavior change, weakness, seizure, or unconsciousness. Older adults may present through delirium or a fall. A neurologic emergency can coexist; glucose testing is an early action, not the end of the differential.

| Hypoglycemia level | Clinical meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Level 1: below 70 but at least 54 mg/dL | Actionable low glucose, including without symptoms. |
| Level 2: below 54 mg/dL | Clinically significant low glucose requiring immediate action. |
| Level 3: severe event requiring another person's assistance because of altered function | Defined by the event, not a particular measured glucose. |

For a conscious adult who can swallow safely, use rapid glucose, commonly 15 g, then recheck after 15 minutes and repeat if still low. Individual plans differ, including for children and automated insulin-delivery users. Fat-rich foods act too slowly for initial rescue, and glucose is particularly important when carbohydrate digestion is inhibited. Severe symptoms or inability to swallow require assistance and emergency care rather than food or fluid by mouth. [ADA 2026 hypoglycemia guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690178/)

Use prescribed glucagon when severe hypoglycemia prevents oral self-treatment, with IV dextrose in a medical setting as appropriate. Teach household members how to use the actual nasal or injectable product and call for emergency help. Check availability and expiry rather than documenting only that a prescription exists. Recurrent or prolonged hypoglycemia, especially from long-acting insulin or secretagogues, may need observation even after an initial response; specialist toxicology management may be necessary. [NIDDK hypoglycemia rescue](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/preventing-problems/low-blood-glucose-hypoglycemia)

### Turn an acute event into a safer follow-up plan

After hypoglycemia rescue, review food access, renal function, injection technique, concentration, duplicate dosing, alcohol, activity, cognition, and caregiver support. Recurrent lows require regimen changes; impaired awareness needs a prevention plan and reassessment of monitoring and targets in 04B. Avoid driving until safe recovery and clinical assessment.

After DKA/HHS, verify insulin and monitoring supplies, a sick-day plan, ketone-testing access where indicated, education, and early clinical contact. Ask about cost and rationing without blame. A person with severe insulin deficiency should not simply stop all insulin because meals are missed; individualized basal/correction instructions and escalation thresholds are essential. Persistent vomiting, inability to hydrate, worsening ketones, or altered breathing/mentation requires urgent reassessment.
