# Medivale Family Medicine

## 13B. Newborn and Infant Problems

Young infants express many diseases through poor feeding, lethargy, tachypnea, vomiting or faltering growth. Assess stability first. A normal birth examination does not exclude disease emerging with ductal closure, changing intake or infection. Establish gestational/chronological age, birth/discharge weights and symptom timing.

Bilirubin treatment follows the AAP 2022 framework and the 2025 biliary-atresia report. Infant-fever decisions use the named CHOP age bands below; hip screening uses AAOS guidance. Gestational age, renal function and the care setting can change neonatal treatment, so these principles do not replace a complete neonatal protocol.

### Begin with stability and the birth-to-home transition

Observe alertness, movement, mucosal color, breathing and caregiver interaction before undressing; obtain reliable vital signs and unclothed weight. Apnea, central cyanosis, grunting, poor perfusion, marked lethargy, seizures or inability to feed requires emergency care. Hypothermia may signal serious illness without fever. Support airway, breathing, circulation and glucose while arranging neonatal-capable assessment.

Review pregnancy/delivery, maternal infections and treatment, membrane rupture, gestation, resuscitation, nursery complications and exposures. Confirm vitamin K, eye prophylaxis, vaccines and screening records. Ask about home support, feed preparation and urgent-care access; use an interpreter as needed. Caregiver reports of changed behavior may precede obvious examination findings.

Blood-spot screening, hearing screening and pulse oximetry answer different questions. HRSA describes usual blood-spot collection at 24-48 hours; local programs determine panels, repeat specimens and special circumstances such as prematurity or transfusion. A collection entry is not proof of a normal result. Track outstanding or unsatisfactory results to completion. A symptomatic infant needs diagnostic evaluation even if the screening result was normal, because screening neither covers every disorder nor detects every affected child. [HRSA newborn screening process](https://newbornscreening.hrsa.gov/newborn-screening-process)

### Evaluate jaundice with age, fraction and clinical context

Newborn bilirubin production is high and conjugation immature; poor intake/stooling increases enterohepatic recycling. Elevated unconjugated bilirubin can enter the brain, especially in susceptible infants. Conjugated elevation suggests impaired bile flow or liver disease. Total bilirubin alone cannot distinguish them; an abnormal direct fraction may require urgent investigation despite a modest total.

Assess jaundice onset/progression, feeding effectiveness, output, weight, bruising, hemolysis/severe-jaundice family history and oxidant/drug exposures. Examine eyes/skin, hydration, tone, activity, liver and spleen. Visual assessment cannot quantify bilirubin. First-24-hour jaundice needs prompt measurement and hemolysis assessment. Poor arousal/feeding, high-pitched cry, arching or seizures requires emergency evaluation for encephalopathy and other illness.

For infants born at **35 or more weeks**, use the AAP hour-specific treatment framework: exact postnatal age, gestational age and additional neurotoxicity risks determine the appropriate threshold. Do not use one bilirubin cutoff for every newborn or apply these curves to infants born before 35 weeks. Total serum bilirubin, not visual color or a transcutaneous estimate, determines treatment. Obtain a serum value when transcutaneous bilirubin is at least 15 mg/dL, at or above the phototherapy threshold, or within 3 mg/dL below that threshold. [AAP 2022 bilirubin guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/150/3/e2022058859/188726/)

Gestational age is already represented in the curves. In a 35-37-week infant, do not automatically select the additional-risk curve solely because gestation is below 38 weeks; additional neurotoxicity risks include hemolytic disease, sepsis and significant recent clinical instability. A DAT or albumin level is not automatically necessary in every jaundiced infant. Order targeted testing when the history, trajectory or treatment context warrants it. A negative DAT does not exclude nonimmune hemolysis such as G6PD deficiency. [AAP bilirubin implementation FAQ](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/hyperbilirubinemia/frequently-asked-questions-about-the-2022-aap-guideline-on-the-management-of-hyperbilirubinemia/)

Review the actual predischarge bilirubin, its collection time and prior treatment. Follow-up after birth hospitalization is based on the distance below the relevant phototherapy threshold, together with intake, weight, examination and reliable access to reassessment. It is not based on an old percentile-risk label alone. Use the AAP follow-up diagram to assign the repeat interval; a closer result generally requires earlier reassessment. The diagram's untreated-birth-hospitalization population differs from infants receiving phototherapy or those older than five days, who need additional clinical judgment. Tell caregivers exactly where and when testing will occur and who will act on the result. [AAP bilirubin implementation FAQ](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/hyperbilirubinemia/frequently-asked-questions-about-the-2022-aap-guideline-on-the-management-of-hyperbilirubinemia/)

Phototherapy changes bilirubin into forms that can be eliminated more readily. Effective treatment requires an appropriate device, sufficient exposure and monitoring of response, temperature and hydration; ordinary household light is not equivalent. Continue feeding support, with supplementation when medically indicated rather than routinely interrupting breastfeeding. Infants approaching exchange transfusion need escalation before reaching that threshold; the AAP escalation threshold is 2 mg/dL below the exchange threshold. Acute neurologic signs or a dangerous trajectory requires urgent neonatal management. After treatment stops, repeat testing depends on rebound risk; early treatment and hemolysis warrant particular attention. [AAP phototherapy technical report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/154/3/e2024068026/199038/Phototherapy-to-Prevent-Severe-Neonatal)

**Clinical Case: poor intake and jaundice.** A four-day-old born at 37 weeks weighs 2.84 kg versus 3.20 kg at birth, a loss of 11.25%. Six brief feeds and two wet diapers accompany jaundice and reduced arousal. Arrange urgent assessment for inadequate intake, dehydration/hypernatremia and bilirubin risk using the correct curve and targeted metabolic tests. Observe feeding and establish safe supplementation while investigating lethargy.

### Do not dismiss prolonged jaundice as breast milk jaundice

Early suboptimal-intake jaundice reflects insufficient milk transfer, often with excessive weight loss. Breast milk jaundice is persistent unconjugated elevation in a thriving infant, not a diagnosis from feeding type alone. Ask about pale/white stools, dark urine and prior direct bilirubin. Biliary atresia may initially occur with adequate growth and no hepatomegaly.

The AAP 2025 report targets recognition by the **2-4-week visit**: assess eye/skin jaundice, stool color and previous direct or conjugated results to decide on fractionated testing, including the report's exceptions for reassuring prior results. Before two weeks, interpret the direct/conjugated result against the laboratory's own reference interval, not a fixed ratio. At 2-4 weeks, a direct or conjugated value **at least 1 mg/dL** warrants urgent pediatric gastroenterology consultation. Do not delay that consultation while waiting for liver enzymes or an ultrasound that might falsely reassure. [AAP early biliary-atresia identification](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/155/3/e2024070077/201020/Guidance-for-the-Primary-Care-Provider-in)

**Clinical Case: a modest total bilirubin.** A thriving 23-day-old remains icteric with newly chalk-colored stools. Total bilirubin is 6.1 mg/dL and direct 2.0 mg/dL. Arrange urgent pediatric gastroenterology assessment for cholestasis, including biliary atresia; adequate growth and a modest total do not establish benign breast milk jaundice.

### Determine whether feeding is effective, not merely frequent

Observe a complete feed: latch, suck-swallow-breathe coordination, swallowing, milk transfer, caregiver pain, fatigue and respiratory symptoms. For bottles, assess nipple flow, position, volume/frequency and exact preparation. Frequent feeds do not prove transfer or correct concentration. Choking, color change or respiratory distress suggests swallowing, airway or structural disease beyond spit-up.

Breastfed newborns commonly feed 8-12 times daily, but sleepy infants with inadequate intake may need an actively supported feeding plan rather than waiting indefinitely for cues. Evaluate maternal supply and practical barriers as well as the infant. Temporary expressed milk, donor milk when available and appropriate, or formula supplementation can support safety while preserving breastfeeding goals. Pair supplementation with skilled assistance and milk expression when indicated. Do not prescribe water or dilute formula to manage jaundice or weight loss. [AAP newborn feeding assessment, updated February 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/newborn-infant-and-early-childhood-nutrition/newborn-and-infant-health-assessment-and-promotion/newborn-visit/)

Plot serial weights and calculate change from birth. Weight loss greater than 10% requires evaluation; it is a trigger to investigate, not a complete diagnosis or a reason to ignore smaller losses in an ill infant. Birth weight is often regained by 7-14 days. By day four or five, expect increasing dilute urine and transition away from meconium as intake improves. Persistent urate staining, scant output, ongoing weight loss or ineffective feeding needs prompt reassessment. Continued urination does not reliably exclude dehydration in a newborn. [AAP first office visit, updated February 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/newborn-infant-and-early-childhood-nutrition/newborn-and-infant-health-assessment-and-promotion/first-office-visit-3-5-days/)

Prematurity and cardiac, neurologic or craniofacial disease can limit feeding endurance despite apparent latch. Prolonging exhausting feeds may increase expenditure without adequate nutrition. Coordinate pediatric, feeding and lactation support with intake/growth monitoring. Investigate GI, infectious, cardiopulmonary or metabolic clues; provide formula-access support without equating poverty with neglect.

### Separate uncomplicated reflux from disease and obstruction

Small effortless regurgitation after feeds in a comfortable, growing infant is common. Gastroesophageal reflux becomes disease when it produces troublesome symptoms or complications; crying or back arching alone is not specific evidence of acid injury. Without alarm features, review feeding volume/technique and avoid overfeeding rather than ordering routine imaging or acid suppression. Persistent troublesome visible regurgitation may justify supervised thickening. Cereal does not reliably thicken expressed human milk because its amylase breaks it down; commercial thickeners have age/product restrictions, particularly important in preterm infants. Do not improvise a thickening recipe. [NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN infant reflux summary, revised January 2024](https://naspghan.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/PedGERD-Summary-revised-January-2024.pdf)

If appropriate feeding measures fail, consider a **2-4-week trial of extensively hydrolyzed formula**, with amino-acid formula for selected circumstances, or supervised maternal cow's-milk exclusion when breastfeeding. Lactose-free ordinary formula is not the same intervention. Reassess symptoms, growth and nutritional adequacy, then plan reintroduction to establish whether cow's-milk protein was responsible rather than continuing an unproven restriction indefinitely. Alarm findings require investigation, not this trial-and-wait sequence.

Never use prone sleep, wedges or raised sleep surfaces for reflux. Acid suppression is not routine for thriving infants with spit-up, and response does not establish diagnosis. Suspected esophagitis or refractory symptoms may need specialist assessment. Hematemesis, dysphagia, poor growth, fever, neurologic findings, forceful vomiting or sudden deterioration requires diagnostic reassessment. [NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN reflux guideline](https://www.naspghan.org/files/Pediatric_Gastroesophageal_Reflux_Clinical.33.pdf)

| Vomiting pattern | Important interpretation | Immediate direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effortless milk-colored spit-up, comfortable infant, appropriate growth | Uncomplicated reflux is possible | Feeding review, reassurance and follow-up if no alarm findings |
| Progressively forceful nonbilious vomiting in early infancy | Gastric outlet obstruction, including pyloric stenosis | Assess hydration and electrolytes; urgent pediatric assessment and ultrasound |
| Green emesis, with or without distention | Intestinal obstruction or malrotation with volvulus must be excluded | Emergency surgical-capable assessment; do not wait for repeated episodes |
| Vomiting with poor perfusion, lethargy or abnormal temperature | Serious infection, metabolic illness or other systemic disease | Stabilization and urgent diagnostic evaluation |

Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is a progressively obstructed gastric outlet, usually presenting in the first weeks rather than immediately at birth. Vomit is typically nonbilious because the obstruction precedes entry of bile into the intestine. Persistent gastric losses can cause hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, but early disease may have normal electrolytes and no palpable mass. Ultrasound is the usual diagnostic study; a nondiagnostic early examination may need repetition if suspicion remains. Initial priorities are stopping feeds, IV access and correction of dehydration and acid-base disturbances, followed by definitive pyloromyotomy. An isolated pyloric obstruction generally allows stabilization before anesthesia; untreated alkalosis increases postoperative respiratory risk. [RCH pyloric stenosis clinical guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Pyloric_Stenosis/)

Green vomiting is different: malrotation can permit the bowel and its blood supply to twist, causing ischemia before the abdomen looks dramatic. Keep the infant without oral intake, stabilize, decompress when indicated and involve pediatric surgery urgently. In a stable infant with suspected malrotation, an upper GI series is usually appropriate; local expert ultrasound pathways may also contribute. A normal plain abdominal radiograph does not exclude malrotation. Shock, peritonitis or other evidence of compromised bowel takes priority over completing a routine imaging sequence. [ACR infant vomiting imaging criteria](https://acsearch.acr.org/list/TopicNarrativePdf?topicId=103)

**Clinical Case: two vomiting patterns.** A five-week-old with progressive forceful nonbilious vomiting, scant urine, chloride 89 mEq/L and bicarbonate 35 mEq/L needs ultrasound and monitored correction of dehydration/alkalosis before pyloric surgery. A nine-day-old with a first green vomit needs emergency obstruction assessment despite alertness and a soft abdomen; neither presentation is routine reflux.

### Treat fever in a young infant as a separate risk problem

A documented rectal temperature **at least 38.0 C**, including a reliable measurement at home, needs prompt medical assessment in a young infant. Becoming afebrile before arrival or improving after an antipyretic does not erase the history. Consider urinary infection, bacteremia and meningitis, but also HSV and noninfectious illness. Young infants may have few localizing signs. Respiratory symptoms or a positive viral test do not independently exclude bacterial coinfection. Ill appearance requires stabilization and prompt empiric treatment; do not postpone antibiotics in an unstable infant to obtain CSF. [CPS young-infant fever principles, updated May 2026](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/management-of-well-appearing-febrile-young-infants-aged-90-days)

AAP's infant-fever framework covers otherwise well-appearing term infants aged 8-60 days with exclusions. **CHOP's August 2026 US pathway** below ends at **56 days**, not 60 or 90. Apply its eligibility and cutoffs together, without mixing protocols. [AAP infant fever resource and scope](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/infant-fever/)

CHOP obtains urine testing/culture, blood culture and inflammatory markers across these groups. Its procalcitonin and ANC abnormal thresholds are greater than 0.5 ng/mL and greater than 4000/mcL. These values belong to this pathway; do not substitute a cutoff from another prediction rule. Prematurity, prolonged neonatal admission, complex illness or focal bacterial infection makes marker-only risk stratification inappropriate.

| CHOP age band | CSF decision emphasis | Safety implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-21 days | Perform lumbar puncture as part of the infection evaluation | Admit and give empiric parenteral antibiotics; obtain cultures first when feasible without delaying urgent treatment |
| 22-28 days | Abnormal urinalysis or an abnormal inflammatory marker prompts LP; normal results permit a more selective approach | Follow the complete pathway for observation, treatment and missing/uninterpretable CSF |
| 29-56 days | Normal markers can allow no LP, including in selected infants with abnormal urinalysis | Treat suspected UTI appropriately; abnormal markers and other concerns change management |

[CHOP maintained febrile-infant pathway](https://www.chop.edu/clinical-pathway/febrile-infant-emergent-evaluation-clinical-pathway)

Omitting LP does not omit urine evaluation or indicated treatment. Obtain a reliable specimen and arrange culture-directed care; bag culture alone cannot confirm UTI. AAP's September 2026 UTI guidance covers eight days to five years but does not replace young-infant sepsis assessment. Use the age-specific fever pathway before outpatient neonatal treatment; see 11C for broader UTI treatment/imaging. [AAP 2026 UTI guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/4/e2026078565/209460/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis-and)

Consider neonatal HSV with vesicles, seizures, hepatitis, CSF abnormalities, sepsis-like illness or relevant exposure. Absence of vesicles or known maternal HSV does not exclude infection. Obtain blood/surface/CSF testing as feasible and start **IV acyclovir promptly**, without waiting for confirmation. CPS uses **20 mg/kg per dose every eight hours with normal renal function**; prematurity and renal dysfunction require neonatal dose/interval review. Monitor hydration, renal function and blood counts. Oral acyclovir is not adequate acute treatment. Confirmed skin/eye/mouth disease usually needs 14 days IV; CNS or disseminated disease needs at least 21 days, with CSF PCR near the end of CNS treatment and extension if still positive. Specialist-led suppression and developmental/hearing/eye follow-up follow acute care. [CPS neonatal HSV guidance, updated 2025 and reaffirmed January 2026](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/prevention-management-neonatal-herpes-simplex-virus-infections)

Beyond a pathway's age limit, use the appropriate age-specific protocol. Outpatient care requires adequate feeding, stability, reliable communication/transport, timed reassessment and assigned culture review. If these or urgent-return arrangements are unreliable, observation/admission may be safer despite reassuring initial tests.

**Clinical Case: fever documented at home.** A 17-day-old had rectal temperature 38.2 C at home but is now 37.3 C, alert and feeding, with a sibling's cold. Arrange immediate hospital-based young-infant infection evaluation. Current afebrile appearance and viral exposure do not erase age-related risk.

### Make sleep safety feasible in the actual household

For every sleep, place infants on their backs on a firm, flat, noninclined approved surface with a fitted sheet and no loose bedding, pillows, toys or positioners. Room-share with a separate infant sleep space for at least the first six months. A couch or armchair is especially dangerous when an adult falls asleep holding the infant. Reflux does not justify prone sleeping or an inclined device. A car seat is essential for transport but is not the routine sleep destination after arrival. [NICHD Safe to Sleep recommendations](https://safetosleep.nichd.nih.gov/reduce-risk/reduce)

Stop swaddling when the infant shows attempts to roll; do not wait for a fixed birthday. Avoid weighted swaddles or blankets. If an infant can independently roll both ways, continue placing the infant supine but do not repeatedly force repositioning after self-rolling; keep the sleep area empty. Avoid overheating, smoke exposure and caregiver impairment. An offered pacifier may help, once direct breastfeeding is established when applicable, but should not be forced or attached by a cord. Supervised awake tummy time supports development and is not permission for prone sleep. Home monitors do not substitute for a safe environment or establish protection from SIDS. [AAP safe-sleep policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/150/1/e2022057990/188304/Sleep-Related-Infant-Deaths-Updated-2022-Recommendations)

Ask where the infant slept and how nighttime feeds occur. Address crib access, crowded housing, reflux fears and caregiver exhaustion, including another alert caregiver when possible. If feeding in an adult bed, clear soft items and return the infant to a separate surface; after accidental sleep, do so immediately on waking. This reduces harm without endorsing bed-sharing. [NICHD sleep and feeding safety](https://safetosleep.nichd.nih.gov/reduce-risk/reduce)

**Clinical Case: recliner feeding.** A two-month-old's caregiver falls asleep feeding in a padded recliner, believing upright sleep treats reflux. The infant grows normally with effortless spit-up. Explain the recliner hazard, arrange an approved bassinet and a feasible feeding/support plan, and continue reflux follow-up.

Use a rear-facing restraint until the manufacturer's rear-facing height or weight limit is reached, with correct installation and harness fit. Do not place it in front of an active airbag. Moving from an infant-only seat to a rear-facing convertible seat is different from turning the child forward. Remove bulky material that prevents a snug harness. [NHTSA rear-facing restraint guidance](https://www.nhtsa.gov/car-seats-and-booster-seats/how-install-all-one-car-seat-rear-facing)

Anticipate mobility before it arrives: guard elevated surfaces and stairs, secure furniture and cords, lock medicines and chemicals, and keep small objects, batteries and magnets inaccessible. Never leave an infant unattended in water, including a bath with a bath seat. Reduce scald hazards and test water temperature. Discuss safe handling when crying becomes overwhelming: put the child in a safe place, take a brief break and obtain help; shaking is never safe. Suspected ingestion, injury or abuse needs prompt clinical assessment rather than assuming a normal initial appearance excludes harm. [CPSC home-safety measures](https://www.cpsc.gov/safety-education/safety-guides/kids-and-babies/Childproofing-Your-Home?language=en)

### Recognize heart disease despite a reassuring nursery screen

After birth, pulmonary resistance falls and the ductus closes; ductal-dependent lesions may deteriorate after a reassuring examination. Outflow obstruction may cause shock/weak pulses, while reduced pulmonary flow or poor mixing causes cyanosis. Compare limb perfusion and femoral pulses without waiting for a loud murmur. Evaluate infection and cardiopulmonary causes together when necessary.

The current CDC algorithm reflects the AAP's 2025 revision. Screen healthy-appearing newborns at least 24 hours after birth, or as late as possible before earlier discharge, using the **right hand and a foot** after supplemental oxygen has been discontinued. Passing requires at least 95% in **both** sites and an absolute difference no greater than three percentage points. Any value below 90% fails immediately. Values below 95% at either site, or a difference above three points, fail if persistent on two measurements separated by an hour. This is not the older algorithm with extra repeat cycles. A failed screen requires prompt evaluation of hypoxemia before routine discharge. [CDC current CCHD screening algorithm](https://www.cdc.gov/heart-defects/hcp/screening/index.html)

| Infant presentation | Circulatory problem to consider | What should not falsely reassure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gray, poorly perfused infant with weak femoral pulses | Systemic outflow obstruction or ductal-dependent systemic flow | A prior passing oxygen screen or no prominent murmur |
| Central cyanosis with abnormal saturations | Reduced pulmonary flow, impaired mixing, or another cardiopulmonary cause | Warm hands or a normal prenatal scan |
| Fast breathing, sweating and fatigue during feeds with poor growth | Pulmonary overcirculation or heart failure | Labeling all feeding-associated symptoms reflux |

Large left-to-right shunts can become symptomatic as pulmonary resistance falls. A substantial VSD may cause tachypnea, sweating/fatigue during feeds and poor growth without cyanosis; small VSDs may be asymptomatic. Assess breathing, feeding, liver size and growth and obtain cardiac evaluation. Murmur loudness does not reliably grade severity. [CHOP VSD overview, reviewed June 2026](https://www.chop.edu/conditions-diseases/ventricular-septal-defect-vsd)

Suspected ductal-dependent disease needs immediate neonatal/cardiology consultation, monitored stabilization and possible transfer. Prostaglandin E1 may maintain/restore ductal flow before anatomy is established; this requires intensive-care monitoring and airway readiness for apnea. Tailor resuscitation to physiology rather than automatically giving large fluid volumes. [Current neonatal alprostadil labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=dea460fa-d05e-4191-8461-f185a711ad89)

**Clinical Case: shock after a passing screen.** A six-day-old who passed pulse oximetry develops poor feeding, tachypnea and cool legs, with brachial but barely palpable femoral pulses. Arrange emergency neonatal-capable transport and cardiac assessment for systemic obstruction, including coarctation, while investigating sepsis and other shock. Normal saturation does not establish systemic perfusion.

### Examine the whole newborn and maintain hip surveillance

Perform a structured examination even when the visit begins with feeding or jaundice. Assess head shape and swelling, fontanelles, eyes and red reflexes, palate, neck, clavicles, chest, abdomen, cord, genitalia, anus, spine, limbs, tone, symmetry and reflexes. Observe breathing and behavior between maneuvers. A persistent asymmetry, absent spontaneous movement or abnormal tone needs an explanation. A brief normal suck during examination does not negate reported choking, cyanosis or exhaustion over a full feed. [Stanford newborn examination](https://med.stanford.edu/newborns/clinical-rotations/students/students-newborn-exam.html)

Caput is superficial edema that may cross sutures; cephalohematoma is subperiosteal and bounded by bone. Blood breakdown can worsen jaundice. Expanding/fluctuant swelling crossing sutures, especially after assisted delivery with pallor or poor perfusion, suggests subgaleal hemorrhage and requires urgent care. Suture crossing alone does not distinguish benign from dangerous swelling. [Stanford neonatal scalp findings](https://www.med.stanford.edu/newborns/professional-education/photo-gallery/head)

An absent, asymmetric or white red reflex requires prompt specialist assessment; do not wait for a later visual-acuity test. Look for cleft palate or micrognathia when feeding or airway function is abnormal. Confirm that the anus is present and review urine and meconium passage; distention, persistent vomiting or delayed passage needs evaluation for obstruction rather than repeated reassurance. Inspect genital anatomy and locate testes; atypical genital development with vomiting or dehydration can indicate an endocrine emergency. Spreading cord erythema, tenderness, purulent discharge or systemic illness warrants urgent assessment, not routine cord-care advice. [AAPOS infant screening and referral](https://www.aapos.org/syndicated/vision-screening)

Developmental dysplasia of the hip includes an inadequately formed acetabulum, instability, subluxation and dislocation. A hip may look normal externally, and a normal birth examination does not eliminate later dysplasia. Gently assess each hip separately: Barlow tests whether an unstable hip can be displaced; Ortolani detects reduction of a displaced hip. A true instability clunk differs from an isolated soft-tissue click. With increasing age, limited abduction and leg-length asymmetry become important. Do not force the examination or use symmetric skin folds as proof that the joints are normal.

AAOS supports repeated clinical examination and **selective**, not universal, ultrasound screening. Breech presentation, family history or a history of clinical instability warrants an imaging plan before six months even when today's examination is normal. Ultrasound is useful before substantial femoral-head ossification; the AAOS evidence supports selected ultrasound at 2-6 weeks or an AP pelvis radiograph at four months. Exact scheduling should follow the local pediatric orthopedic pathway. A clearly unstable or dislocated hip needs referral rather than waiting for the routine risk-screening date. [AAOS 2022 infant DDH guideline](https://www.aaos.org/globalassets/quality-and-practice-resources/pddh/pddhcpg.pdf)

DDH treatment depends on age and stability. Early bracing, often a Pavlik harness, supports femoral-head positioning and acetabular development; later/resistant disease may need reduction, casting or surgery. Orthopedics manages fit and follow-up; caregivers should not adjust straps. Teach skin/movement checks and hip-flexion-friendly swaddling. Residual dysplasia can impair later gait and joint health. [AAOS DDH clinical overview](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/developmental-dislocation-dysplasia-of-the-hip-ddh/)

**Clinical Case: breech history.** A seven-week-old born breech moves both legs freely without apparent length discrepancy. Cesarean birth does not remove DDH risk. Arrange selective imaging and serial examinations; limited abduction or definite instability warrants orthopedic referral without waiting for screening imaging.

| Examination or record issue | Required follow-through |
| --- | --- |
| Positive or missing newborn-screen result | Obtain the actual result and arrange the program-specific confirmatory response |
| Abnormal red reflex, concerning neurologic finding or cord infection | Prompt evaluation appropriate to the finding; do not defer to routine screening |
| Definite hip instability or dislocation | Pediatric orthopedic referral with coordinated imaging when indicated |
| Normal hip examination with important risk history | Selective imaging plus continued clinical surveillance |

### Close the loop on prevention and reassessment

Confirm receipt of intramuscular vitamin K and explain that it prevents bleeding caused by the newborn's limited vitamin K stores; it is not a vaccine. Unexplained bleeding, bruising, pallor or neurologic change requires urgent evaluation, particularly after prophylaxis was declined or when cholestasis may impair absorption. A feeding history or maternal diet does not establish adequate protection. Record refusal and counseling without allowing a signed form to substitute for a follow-up plan. [CDC vitamin K deficiency bleeding](https://www.cdc.gov/vitamin-k-deficiency/about/index.html)

Document eye prophylaxis and maternal infection results. Erythromycin ophthalmic prophylaxis targets gonococcal disease; purulent neonatal conjunctivitis still needs urgent etiologic assessment and systemic treatment when indicated. Ointment is not treatment for established invasive infection. It also does not prevent chlamydial ophthalmia or pneumonia; prevention depends on maternal screening and treatment, and affected infants need appropriate evaluation. Avoid inferring the organism solely from the day symptoms begin. [CDC neonatal gonorrhea guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-neonates.htm), [CDC neonatal chlamydia guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/chlamydia.htm)

Before discharge, assign the next weight/bilirubin check, culture review, imaging or referral and explain uncertainty and return precautions. Demonstrate formula preparation and medicines. Fever, poor feeding, worsening lethargy, green vomiting, breathing difficulty, abnormal color/perfusion or seizures requires immediate reassessment.
