# Medivale Family Medicine

## 13A. Well-Child Care and Development

Preventive care follows growth, development, feeding, family circumstances, sensory function and immunization history over time. Loss of skills, faltering growth or persistent caregiver concern needs assessment now, regardless of the next screening date.

AAP/Bright Futures provides the preventive-visit framework; newer disease-specific guidance governs faltering weight and iron screening. Pediatric vaccination follows the AAP 2026 schedule and September respiratory-season addendum. Newborn illness follows in volume 11B and older-child/adolescent care in 11D.

### Organize continuity around the child and family

Review birth/gestational history, neonatal illness, screening results, admissions, medicines, allergies, family history, growth and vaccine records. Elicit family priorities and involve the child in an age-appropriate way, using an interpreter when needed.

Bright Futures includes evaluation after birth, an early visit at 3-5 days, a visit by one month, visits at 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24, and 30 months, then annual visits from age three through adolescence and young adulthood. The early postdischarge visit may need to occur sooner according to discharge timing and feeding, jaundice, or other risks. A late first visit should trigger reconciliation of missed services, not a decision to wait until the next age column. [AAP periodicity framework and current linked schedule](https://www.aap.org/periodicityschedule)

At each encounter, assess interval concerns, growth, development, psychosocial needs, oral health, and immunizations; track earlier abnormal results and referrals. Arrange additional assessment when a complicated symptom cannot safely share a routine visit. Minor illness need not displace preventive care.

Ask about food/housing security, transport, childcare, caregiver stress, smoke and medicine access without assuming risk from household structure. Screen maternal depression at the infant's 1-, 2-, 4- and 6-month visits and connect positive findings to care. Assess other distressed caregivers. Suicidality, psychosis or inability to provide safe care requires urgent action. [AAP perinatal depression in pediatric practice](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/143/1/e20183259/37241/)

Anticipate emerging mobility and skills: discuss safe sleep, seat height/weight limits, water supervision, choking, burns, medicines and firearms. Support interaction, reading, routines and positive discipline with feasible household priorities. Media advice should be age-specific. See 11B for infant safety and 10G for maltreatment safeguards.

### Measure growth accurately before interpreting it

Measure weight, infant recumbent length, developmentally appropriate standing height and head circumference through the first two years, longer when indicated. Use calibrated equipment and correct positioning; repeat implausible results. Length error can distort weight-for-length/BMI. Interpret serial measurements and methods, not one percentile.

Use WHO standards from birth to age two for both breastfed and formula-fed infants, then CDC charts for older children in US practice. Weight-for-length is the usual proportionality measure before two; BMI-for-age becomes useful from two onward. Changing from recumbent length to standing height or changing chart systems can create an apparent shift. Disease-specific charts may add context but do not remove the need to assess nutritional status and general health. [CDC growth-chart selection and interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/growth-chart-training/hcp/using-growth-charts/who-using.html)

Percentiles locate a child within a reference distribution; they are neither percentages of ideal weight nor disease probabilities. A z score measures standard deviations from the mean and better distinguishes extreme values. Interpret velocity, proportionality, parental height, puberty and clinical course together. Genetic potential does not explain progressive weight loss or new neurologic findings.

For a child born preterm, corrected age helps interpret early developmental expectations and the relevant growth assessment. Subtract the number of weeks before 40 weeks' gestation from chronological age; use an appropriate preterm-chart and follow-up pathway before transitioning to standard infant charts. Correction is generally useful during the first two years, but it is not permission to dismiss asymmetry or regression. Most routine immunizations use chronological age, with specific newborn/product exceptions addressed separately. [AAP explanation of corrected age](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/preemie/pages/Corrected-Age-For-Preemies.aspx)

**Example: corrected age.** An infant born at 34 weeks is now 20 weeks old: corrected age is 20 - (40 - 34) = 14 weeks. Interpret skills with neonatal history and examination rather than term expectations at 20 weeks, but investigate persistent asymmetry. This correction does not move vaccination six weeks later.

### Evaluate faltering weight without an indiscriminate workup

The AAP/NASPGHAN **2026 faltering-weight guideline**, directed to children younger than five, uses z-score criteria rather than relying only on crossing percentile lines. Its criteria include weight-for-length or BMI-for-age below -1.65; weight-gain velocity below -2 for children younger than two; or a decline of at least 1 z score in weight, weight-for-length, or BMI. These findings identify a problem requiring assessment, not its cause or a diagnosis of neglect. [AAP/NASPGHAN 2026 clinical guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/4/e2025075764/206901/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-Diagnosis-and)

Assess intake, absorption/loss and energy needs. Review feeding frequency, formula preparation, milk transfer, textures, meal duration, vomiting, stools, pain, breathing, development and food access; observe feeding. Check hydration, vital signs, tissue stores and focal findings. Dysphagia, cardiac/lung disease, celiac disease or restrictive intake requires cause-directed care, not calories alone.

| Growth pattern or associated finding | What it may suggest | Important next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weight deceleration before linear growth is affected | Inadequate intake, losses, or increased demand | Detailed feeding and illness assessment; verify measurements |
| Reduced linear velocity with relatively preserved weight | Endocrine, skeletal, genetic, or other chronic disease | Review height trajectory, puberty, and focused diagnostic clues |
| Coughing, sweating, or exhaustion during feeds | Swallowing, cardiac, pulmonary, or neuromuscular difficulty | Assess feeding safety and the suspected organ system |
| Persistent diarrhea, blood, or bulky stools | Inflammatory, infectious, or malabsorptive disease | Targeted evaluation rather than calories alone |
| Edema, severe wasting, lethargy, or dehydration | Potentially severe nutritional or systemic illness | Urgent assessment and an appropriate supervised setting |

The 2026 guideline recommends against routine initial diagnostic panels without focal findings; test selectively when disease clues emerge. Establish feeding/nutrition support, address access, involve dietetics or feeding therapy and reassess growth. Severe malnutrition may require supervised rehabilitation with refeeding precautions. Formula concentration requires a prescribed preparation plan.

**Clinical Case: diluted formula.** An alert, hydrated five-month-old has declining weight with preserved length and no focal illness. After losing nutrition benefits, the family began diluting formula. Assess immediate needs and feeding, correct preparation, restore access and arrange early growth review. Poverty alone does not establish neglect, and resource support does not replace checking recovery.

### Assess high BMI as a health problem, not a character judgment

From age two, calculate BMI as kilograms divided by height in meters squared and interpret it by age and sex, not adult cutoffs. Underweight is below the fifth percentile; healthy-weight classification is fifth to below 85th; overweight is 85th to below 95th; obesity is at or above the 95th. BMI is a screening/clinical classification measure rather than a complete assessment of body composition, nutrition, or fitness. Use an extended chart when necessary for very high values. [CDC pediatric BMI categories](https://www.cdc.gov/bmi/child-teen-calculator/bmi-categories.html)

Assess food patterns, activity, sleep, medicines, mood, bullying, eating-disorder symptoms and social constraints. Check properly measured pressure and complications. Reduced height velocity, unusually early severe obesity or endocrine/genetic clues changes the workup; high BMI alone rarely warrants a broad endocrine panel. Use permission-based, person-first discussion.

In AAP's disease-management pathway, from **age 10 with obesity**, assess fasting lipids, glucose metabolism (fasting glucose, HbA1c or an indicated glucose-tolerance test) and ALT. With overweight, obtain fasting lipids and add glucose/liver assessment when risk factors warrant; lipid testing may also be considered at ages 2-9 with obesity. Measure BP at every visit from age three in children with overweight/obesity, not only at the annual preventive visit. Snoring or other disordered-breathing symptoms warrants sleep evaluation, not an assumption that daytime inattention is ADHD. [AAP obesity guideline, KAS 3-8](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/151/2/e2022060640/190443/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Evaluation-and)

Treatment should be family-centered and sufficiently intensive to provide actual help. USPSTF supports providing or referring children six and older with BMI at or above the 95th percentile to comprehensive behavioral intervention, with at least 26 contact hours associated with benefit. This is not a minimum age for measuring BMI or helping younger families. [USPSTF high-BMI intervention framework](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/obesity-in-children-and-adolescents-screening)

AAP recommends offering appropriate **pharmacotherapy from age 12 with obesity**, alongside behavioral treatment, after checking the particular drug's indication, contraindications and monitoring. At **13 or older with severe obesity, BMI at least 120% of the age/sex-specific 95th percentile**, offer referral to a comprehensive pediatric metabolic/bariatric center for evaluation. Referral does not mean automatic surgery, and neither age threshold is a license to use an adult dose or BMI cutoff. [AAP obesity guideline, KAS 12-13](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/151/2/e2022060640/190443/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Evaluation-and)

### Distinguish developmental surveillance, screening, and diagnosis

**Surveillance** combines caregiver concerns, skills, observation, risks/strengths and longitudinal change. **Screening** uses validated instruments to identify children needing assessment. **Diagnostic evaluation** establishes the disorder and cause. A normal screen does not override convincing concern; an abnormal score does not establish a diagnosis.

AAP recommends standardized general developmental screening at **9, 18, and 30 months**, with screening whenever surveillance raises concern. Assess gross motor, fine motor/adaptive, language, cognitive, social-emotional, and everyday functional skills. Tool selection must match age, language, culture, and accessibility. Parent milestone lists and a clinician's impression are useful observations but do not replace validated screening. Document the instrument, result, interpretation, and resulting action. [AAP developmental surveillance and screening](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/developmental-surveillance-and-screening-patient-care/)

CDC milestone checklists describe skills most children, at least 75%, demonstrate by the listed age; they are not average-age tables or diagnostic tests. Selected examples are independent sitting and varied babbling by nine months, independent walking and pointing to share interest by 18 months, and combining at least two words by two years. Failure to demonstrate a listed skill invites discussion and assessment, not a diagnosis from one unchecked box. [CDC nine-month landmarks](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html), [18-month landmarks](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html), and [two-year landmarks](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)

Distinguish delayed acquisition from loss of skills. Review hearing, vision, neurologic/birth history, sleep, feeding, exposures and opportunities for interaction. Examine head growth, tone, strength, reflexes, symmetry, gait and relevant dysmorphic/neurocutaneous findings. Lost words, hand use or new seizures warrants prompt evaluation rather than waiting for a screening birthday.

| Developmental concern | Important distinctions | Initial response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Limited expressive language | Hearing, receptive language, social communication, and skills in all languages | Audiology and language/developmental assessment; early support |
| Gross motor delay | Weakness versus abnormal tone, symmetry, pain, or limited opportunity | Focused neurologic/musculoskeletal assessment and appropriate therapy/workup |
| Delays across several domains | Global developmental difficulty, sensory barriers, systemic or genetic disease | Comprehensive developmental evaluation and indicated etiologic assessment |
| Reduced joint attention or social reciprocity | Autism, hearing impairment, language disorder, and broader developmental context | Autism-focused evaluation while addressing other possibilities |
| Loss of established skills | Neurologic, metabolic, sensory, or other acquired/progressive disorder | Prompt medical evaluation; urgent care for acute deterioration |

Refer significant developmental concerns to early intervention without waiting for a specialist diagnosis. US infant/toddler services generally cover children under three; school-based preschool pathways take over from three. Eligibility is local and families can often request evaluation directly. Track completion and address forms, transport and follow-up. [CDC early-intervention pathways](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/early-intervention/index.html)

### Recognize autism without explaining away other needs

Autism combines persistent social-communication/interaction differences with restricted or repetitive behavior, interests or sensory responses. Signs may emerge with rising social demands or be masked by learned strategies. Language delay alone is insufficient; stereotyped hand movements are not required. Eye contact, affection or apparent intelligence does not exclude autism.

AAP recommends autism-specific screening at **18 and 24 months**, alongside general developmental surveillance and the scheduled general screens. Refer when surveillance is concerning even outside those ages. Hearing impairment, language disorder, intellectual disability, anxiety, and other neurologic or developmental conditions may coexist. No laboratory test or imaging study replaces clinical developmental assessment. Etiologic genetic evaluation and testing for associated disorders depend on the clinical assessment and specialist pathway, not an indiscriminate panel for every late talker. [AAP autism clinical resources](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/developmental-behavioral-pediatrics-a-resource-guide-for-general-pediatricians/autism-spectrum-disorder-resources-for-pediatricians/)

For M-CHAT-R/F, use the current complete instrument and its authorized language version. Scores of 0-2 usually require continued surveillance, with referral if other concerns remain; 3-7 calls for the structured follow-up interview; two or more persistently elevated items after follow-up constitutes a positive screen. An initial score of 8-20 supports immediate diagnostic and early-intervention referral without that interview. The screen estimates likelihood; it does not determine severity or confirm autism. Do not replace the validated procedure with selected favorite questions. [Instrument authors' scoring instructions](https://www.mchatscreen.com/mchat-rf/scoring/)

**Clinical Case: language regression.** A bilingual 23-month-old has lost words and stopped bringing objects to share. Assess both languages, arrange audiology and developmental/autism evaluation, and begin early services without waiting for a positive screen. Bilingualism does not explain regression; reacting to loud sounds does not exclude hearing loss.

Support communication, daily function, participation, learning, and family priorities. Address sleep, feeding selectivity, constipation, pain, safety, coexisting ADHD/anxiety, and caregiver stress. Medicines for associated problems do not replace developmental and educational support. Avoid promised cures, unsupported restrictive diets, and harmful treatments. Explain concerns without stigma and begin practical help while diagnostic evaluation is pending.

### Support feeding, complementary foods, and micronutrients

Assess food, volume/frequency, preparation, feeding interaction and cues. AAP supports exclusive breastfeeding for approximately six months and continued breastfeeding with complementary foods for two years or beyond as mutually desired. Offer lactation and workplace/access support. Formula or combination feeding may be necessary or chosen. [AAP breastfeeding clinical guidance](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/newborn-infant-and-early-childhood-nutrition/newborn-and-infant-breastfeeding/)

Use commercial infant formula prepared exactly as directed. Powder is not sterile; high-risk infants may need extra precautions or ready-to-feed formula. Never dilute feeds or use homemade recipes. Review safe water, preparation and storage. Vomiting, scant urine, lethargy or poor growth requires assessment despite apparently adequate reported volumes. [CDC formula preparation and storage](https://www.cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/formula-feeding/preparation-and-storage.html)

Introduce developmentally appropriate complementary foods around six months while maintaining human milk or formula as the principal milk source through the first year. Assess head/trunk control and ability to handle textures safely. Offer varied nutrient-dense foods, including iron-rich choices, and encourage responsive feeding rather than pressure to finish a bottle or plate. Do not replace infant milk feeds with cow milk or plant beverages before 12 months. After the first year, excessive milk can displace iron-containing foods; nutritional equivalence should not be assumed across plant beverages.

Prevent choking through seated supervision, texture modification, and avoidance of whole nuts, whole grapes, and other hazardous shapes. Do not give honey before 12 months because of infant botulism risk. Delay of allergenic foods is not an allergy-prevention strategy: introduce age-appropriate forms when ready. Severe eczema or egg allergy calls for a specific early peanut-introduction assessment, potentially at 4-6 months, rather than unsupervised whole peanuts or a broad screening panel for every infant. [NIAID peanut-prevention framework](https://www.niaid.nih.gov/sites/default/files/addendum-peanut-allergy-prevention-guidelines.pdf)

Breastfed and partially breastfed infants generally need **400 IU vitamin D daily** beginning shortly after birth; account for formula intake and other sources. Total intake needs rise to 600 IU daily after the first birthday, with diet and supplements considered together. Check the product concentration: a dose expressed in IU is not automatically one drop or one dropper. Sun exposure is not a reliable or risk-free replacement. Malabsorption, prematurity, kidney/liver disease, or suspected rickets requires a separate assessment, not automatic escalation of routine prophylaxis. [CDC current vitamin-D guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/diet-micronutrients/vitamin-d.html)

Under AAP's June 2026 iron report, exclusively breastfed term infants should receive **1 mg/kg/day elemental iron by four months**; an individualized discussion may defer supplementation until six months when iron-containing complementary foods begin. **Preterm infants receiving full enteral feeds need fortification or supplementation by two weeks to provide a total of 2-3 mg/kg/day elemental iron.** Count iron already supplied by feeds and coordinate the neonatal plan, especially after transfusions; this is not an instruction to add that amount on top of adequate fortified intake. Prevention and treatment doses differ. After 12 months, keep cow/plant-milk intake below 24 ounces daily and maintain iron-rich foods. [AAP 2026 iron report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/1/e2026077414/207901/Prevention-Screening-Diagnosis-and-Treatment-of)

### Check sensory function and oral health with meaningful follow-up

Obtain newborn hearing results. EHDI targets screening by one month, diagnosis by three and early intervention by six when loss is identified; assess sooner when possible. A passed newborn screen does not exclude progressive/later loss. Caregiver concern, language delay or risk exposures warrants audiology even if the child responds to some sounds. [CDC EHDI benchmarks](https://www.cdc.gov/hearing-loss-children/articles/baby-hearing-screening-infographic.html)

Inspect eyes/red reflex, alignment and age-appropriate visual behavior. Photoscreening detects selected amblyopia risks, not all eye disease. Test each eye separately. Abnormal red reflex/leukocoria, persistent strabismus or other concerning findings warrants ophthalmic evaluation without waiting for school screening.

AAPOS age-specific acuity criteria include correctly identifying most optotypes at 20/50 for 36-47 months, 20/40 for 48-59 months, and 20/32 from age five. Use the protocol for the actual method and refer a failed screen. An untestable child is not automatically a pass; arrange a reliable repeat or appropriate referral according to risk. Visual development can be harmed even when the child has no complaint. [AAPOS screening and referral guidance](https://www.aapos.org/syndicated/vision-screening)

**Clinical Case: monocular impairment.** A four-year-old recognizes pictures binocularly but has left-eye acuity of 20/80 without caregiver concern. Refer for eye evaluation; binocular performance can conceal unilateral impairment. Establish the cause and treatment plan before patching.

Establish a dental home by the first birthday and assess caries, trauma, pain and access. Brush twice daily with a rice-grain smear of fluoridated toothpaste before three and a pea-sized amount at three to six, with assisted brushing and minimal swallowing. Avoid sugary-drink exposure and bedtime bottles. Pain, facial swelling or spreading infection needs clinical care. [AAPD fluoride therapy](https://www.aapd.org/research/oral-health-policies--recommendations/fluoride-therapy/)

Apply fluoride varnish from tooth eruption in children younger than five, repeating according to risk. **Swallowed fluoride supplements differ from toothpaste, varnish and community water fluoridation.** FDA recommends limiting ingestible drug products to children aged **at least three** with **high caries risk**, after assessing water deficiency and total fluoride exposure. Its enforcement priorities do not confer product approval. AAPD retains exposure/risk-based supplementation from six months through 16 years, including selected children under three. Discuss the disagreement with dental care rather than automatically prescribing infant drops or stopping topical prevention. [USPSTF topical prevention](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/prevention-of-dental-caries-in-children-younger-than-age-5-years-screening-and-interventions1), [FDA ingestible-product position](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/public-health-focus/ingestible-fluoride-drug-products), and [AAPD response](https://www.aapd.org/statement-on-recent-fda-announcement-to-fluoride-supplements/)

### Select laboratory screening according to age, risk, and purpose

Lead exposure may come from deteriorating paint or renovation dust, contaminated soil/water, imported products, or take-home occupational exposure. Ask about all places the child spends time. Medicaid-enrolled children require blood testing at 12 and 24 months, and catch-up testing at 24-72 months if not previously screened; other testing follows local plans and exposure risk. A capillary elevation requires appropriate venous confirmation. CDC's **3.5 micrograms/dL** reference value identifies children needing additional action; it is not a safe-versus-toxic boundary. [CDC lead assessment and follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Remove lead exposure, assess development/nutrition and coordinate public health. Higher values or symptoms need faster confirmation; chelation never substitutes for exposure control. **Example: capillary lead.** A 26-month-old exposed to renovation dust has capillary lead 7.6 micrograms/dL. Confirm venously within three months while addressing exposure and follow-up now.

AAP's 2026 iron pathway recommends CBC and ferritin screening at **9-12 months when human milk was the primary infant nutrition**, and **15-18 months after predominantly iron-fortified formula feeding**, with additional testing for symptoms or continuing risk. If both studies cannot be obtained, hemoglobin is the minimum initial assessment. Risk review continues beyond that first screen. A normal hemoglobin does not exclude iron depletion; ferritin interpretation is affected by inflammation. Pallor, pica, fatigue, poor intake, or bleeding warrants diagnostic assessment rather than waiting for the routine window.

In the AAP pathway, ferritin **20 ng/mL or lower through age 10**, or **30 ng/mL or lower from age 11 or in a menstruating patient**, supports iron deficiency; anemia is a separate age/sex-adjusted hemoglobin finding. Inflammation may elevate ferritin and conceal deficiency. For uncomplicated young-child iron deficiency, with or without anemia, use oral ferrous sulfate supplying **3 mg/kg elemental iron once daily**, while correcting the cause. Check elemental concentration rather than the mass of ferrous sulfate: for a 12-kg child, 36 mg elemental iron is 2.4 mL only if the preparation contains 15 mg elemental iron/mL. Store securely because overdose is dangerous. [AAP diagnosis and treatment report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2026-077414/207901/Prevention-Screening-Diagnosis-and-Treatment-of)

For uncomplicated iron-deficiency anemia, repeat CBC/reticulocytes at one month. Mild anemia should normalize; moderate anemia should show a hemoglobin rise of at least 2 g/dL by four weeks. Continue a successful therapeutic course for at least three months total, then reassess CBC and ferritin before stopping; persistent depletion needs further treatment and cause review. Poor response calls for checking dose, administration, ongoing blood loss, malabsorption and alternatives such as thalassemia, not simply doubling iron indefinitely. Severe anemia, instability or ongoing bleeding needs urgent assessment and closer follow-up rather than this routine schedule. Adolescent screening and dosing appear in 11D.

Other preventive tests have their own populations. AAP/Bright Futures includes universal lipid assessment once at 9-11 years and once at 17-21, with earlier selective assessment when indicated. TB testing is risk-based, not an annual test for every child. Blood pressure is routinely measured annually from age three, and earlier or more often for specified risk conditions. Adolescent infectious, depression, suicide-risk, and substance-use assessment belongs in an age-appropriate confidential process. An insufficient-evidence preventive recommendation does not mean that symptoms should be ignored. [AAP current periodicity sheet and footnotes](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/periodicity_schedule.pdf)

### Reconcile immunizations instead of restarting a late series

Use **AAP's 2026 schedule, updated September 2**, with catch-up intervals, medical indications, notes, contraindications and addendum. AAP and federal age recommendations differ; keep each framework intact. School requirements, coverage, availability and clinical recommendations are separate. [AAP current schedule hub](https://publications.aap.org/redbook/resources/15585/AAP-Immunization-Schedule)

Obtain dated practice/registry records, including vaccines given abroad. Check product, administration age, dose number, and interval. An extended gap generally **does not require restarting** a valid series. Distinguish missing records, invalid early doses, combination products, and missing doses; age can change catch-up indications and counts. [AAP catch-up guidance](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/AAP-Immunization-Schedule.pdf)

The following are **routine series for children without special-risk indications**, not the complete immunocompromised, travel or outbreak schedules. Combination products must satisfy every included antigen's age and interval rules. [AAP antigen notes and catch-up table](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/AAP-Immunization-Schedule.pdf)

| Vaccine | Routine series | Important catch-up rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HepB | Birth, 1-2 months, 6-18 months | Minimum gaps: 4 weeks between doses 1-2; final dose at least 8 weeks after dose 2 and 16 weeks after dose 1, at age at least 24 weeks |
| DTaP, under seven | 2, 4, 6, 15-18 months, 4-6 years | Minimum gaps 4 weeks, 4 weeks, 6 months, 6 months; fifth dose unnecessary if dose 4 was at age at least four and at least 6 months after dose 3 |
| IPV | 2, 4, 6-18 months, 4-6 years | Final dose at age at least four AND at least 6 months after the preceding dose; early doses do not remove that requirement |
| Hib | ActHIB/Hiberix/Pentacel/Vaxelis primary doses at 2, 4, 6 months, then booster 12-15 months; PedvaxHIB at 2, 4, then 12-15 months | Do not use Vaxelis for the booster. Healthy unvaccinated children 15-59 months need one dose; first dose at 12-14 months needs one further dose at least 8 weeks later. Earlier or mixed histories need AAP Table 2 (page 3) and Hib notes (page 7) in the linked schedule |
| PCV15 or PCV20 | 2, 4, 6, 12-15 months | Healthy children 24-59 months with an incomplete age-appropriate series need one catch-up dose; younger children and risk conditions require history-specific assessment |
| Rotavirus | Rotarix at 2 and 4 months; RotaTeq at 2, 4 and 6 months | If any dose was RotaTeq or unknown, use three doses. Minimum gap 4 weeks; first dose no later than 14 weeks 6 days and final dose no later than 8 months 0 days |
| MMR | 12-15 months and 4-6 years | Two postbirthday doses can be completed at least 4 weeks apart; MMRV requires a 3-month planned interval |
| Varicella | 12-15 months and 4-6 years | Plan 3 months between doses under 13 years, at least 4 weeks from age 13; check evidence of immunity |
| HepA | Two doses starting at 12-23 months | At least 6 months apart; offer catch-up through age 18 |
| Tdap | One adolescent booster at 11-12 years | From age seven, incomplete DTaP histories use Tdap, then Td/Tdap as indicated. A previously unvaccinated adolescent needs dose 2 at least 4 weeks later and dose 3 at least 6 months after dose 2 |
| HPV | Start at 9-12 years under AAP | If first dose before 15 and immunocompetent: two doses at 0 and 6-12 months, minimum gap 5 months. If first dose at least 15 or immunocompromised: three at 0, 1-2 and 6 months; minimum gaps 4 weeks, 12 weeks and 5 months from first to last |
| MenACWY | 11-12 years and booster at 16 | First dose at 13-15: booster at 16-18, at least 8 weeks later. First dose at 16 or older: no routine healthy-adolescent booster |

HepB's birth dose is time-sensitive. If maternal HBsAg is positive, give monovalent vaccine and HBIG in separate limbs within 12 hours regardless of birth weight; infants below 2000 g then need three additional vaccine doses. If maternal status is unknown, test immediately: infants below 2000 g receive vaccine plus HBIG within 12 hours; those at least 2000 g receive vaccine within 12 hours, adding HBIG as soon as possible, no later than day seven, if maternal testing is positive. Other evidence of maternal HBV infection warrants the positive-status pathway. For an HBsAg-negative mother, medically stable infants at least 2000 g receive vaccine within 24 hours; those below 2000 g receive it at one month or discharge, whichever comes first. Exposed infants require HBsAg/anti-HBs testing at 9-12 months, or 1-2 months after a delayed final dose, never before nine months.

MenB is separate from MenACWY: for healthy adolescents it is shared decision-making at 16-23 years, preferably 16-18, using the same brand for two doses at least six months apart. Faster protection or high-risk conditions may require a three-dose course. A Tdap catch-up dose at 7-9 years does not replace the 11-12-year booster; one given at age 10 does. HPV is deferred during pregnancy, and Tdap is recommended in each pregnancy, preferably early in weeks 27-36.

| Situation | Principle | Important safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long interval after a valid dose | Continue the indicated series | Do not restart solely because time elapsed |
| Dose given too early | Check minimum age, interval, and permitted grace rules | Routine target age is not the same as minimum valid age |
| Several vaccines due | Appropriate same-day administration often avoids missed protection | Check product-specific combinations and live-vaccine timing |
| Mild respiratory illness | Usually not a contraindication | Moderate/severe acute illness and true contraindications need separate assessment |
| Older child with incomplete infant vaccines | Age and risk can change catch-up needs | Do not copy an infant dose count into every older age |
| Seasonal or risk-based product | Use current season, history, and indication | Do not assume last season's product or number of doses still applies |

Injected live vaccines such as MMR and varicella can generally be given on the same day; if not simultaneous, the usual minimum separation is 28 days. Antibody-containing blood products can interfere with selected live vaccines, requiring product-specific intervals. Severe immunodeficiency, pregnancy, prior anaphylaxis, and vaccine-specific contraindications must be checked; a family history of nonspecific reactions is not equivalent to the child's anaphylaxis. [CDC timing and spacing principles](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-best-practices/timing-spacing-immunobiologics.html)

Prior intussusception or severe combined immunodeficiency contraindicates rotavirus vaccine. Prebirthday MMR for travel/exposure does not replace the routine postbirthday series. Developmental delay or autism concern does not justify deferring indicated vaccination.

### Apply the 2026-2027 respiratory-season recommendations

Give age/health-appropriate influenza vaccine annually from six months. At ages six months through eight years, fewer than two prior influenza doses before **July 1, 2026**, or unknown history, means **two this season at least four weeks apart**; otherwise give one. From age nine, give one. Egg allergy alone does not require a special vaccine. Live intranasal vaccine is not for children under two, pregnancy, immunocompromise, or ages 2-4 with asthma/recent wheezing; review its other contraindications. [AAP 2026-2027 influenza policy](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078778)

AAP recommends 2026-2027 COVID-19 vaccination for all children 6-23 months and, at ages 2-18, those at increased risk of severe disease, living in congregate care, never previously vaccinated, or living with a high-risk contact; offer it also when other families desire protection. For a nonimmunocompromised, previously unvaccinated child 6-23 months, use **two Moderna Spikevax doses 4-8 weeks apart**. After one prior Moderna dose, give one 4-8 weeks later; after one Pfizer dose, give two Moderna doses at least four weeks apart, starting 3-8 weeks after Pfizer; after two Pfizer doses, give one Moderna at least eight weeks later. After a completed initial series, give one current-season Moderna dose at least eight weeks later. From age two, eligible nonimmunocompromised children generally need one age-appropriate current-season dose, at least eight weeks after any previous dose; mNEXSPIKE from age 12 uses a 12-week interval. Immunocompromise needs its separate multidose notes. Use the current formulation, not last year's volume. AAP-recommended vaccination without an FDA-labeled high-risk condition can be off-label: distinguish the recommendation from product approval, counsel and document accordingly. [AAP schedule COVID-19 notes](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/AAP-Immunization-Schedule.pdf), [current Spikevax indication](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/spikevax)

RSV antibodies are **passive immunization, not vaccines**. In the AAP pathway, eligible infants **under eight months** entering their first season receive nirsevimab or clesrovimab if maternal vaccination was absent/unknown or birth occurred less than 14 days afterward. Give during the birth hospitalization when born in season, otherwise shortly before/within the season, usually October-March in the continental US. Most infants born at least 14 days after maternal vaccination need neither antibody; selected high-risk exceptions and antibody-depleting procedures require specific review.

For children **8-19 months entering a second season**, AAP's September update recommends **nirsevimab**, not clesrovimab, for these high-risk groups: birth before 32 weeks regardless of ongoing support; chronic lung disease from prematurity or another significant neonatal condition needing oxygen, diuretics or chronic steroids within the preceding six months; hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease; high-risk pulmonary anatomy or neuromuscular disease; severe immunocompromise; Down syndrome or another chromosomal condition increasing severe-RSV risk; cystic fibrosis with severe lung disease or weight-for-length below the 10th percentile; and American Indian/Alaska Native children. This expanded AAP list must not be silently narrowed to the older CDC list. [AAP September schedule/addendum](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/AAP-Immunization-Schedule.pdf)

Maternal protection uses **Abrysvo at 32 weeks 0 days through 36 weeks 6 days**, during **September 1-March 1** under specialist 2026-2027 guidance, adjusted locally. Prior-pregnancy vaccination is not repeated; plan infant antibody protection instead. Coordinate alternatives rather than routinely giving both. CDC's concurrent September-January window is a separate framework; see Immunization Across the Lifespan. [SMFM seasonal guidance](https://www.smfm.org/rsv-toolkit)

**Clinical Case: DTaP catch-up.** A child aged exactly three has valid DTaP doses at two and four months only. Give dose 3 now, dose 4 at least six months later, then dose 5 at age 4-6 years and at least six months after dose 4. If dose 4 is delayed until at least age four and at least six months after dose 3, omit dose 5. Reconcile other antigens separately: at age three, a healthy child with no Hib or PCV doses generally needs one of each. Mild coryza need not delay vaccination.
