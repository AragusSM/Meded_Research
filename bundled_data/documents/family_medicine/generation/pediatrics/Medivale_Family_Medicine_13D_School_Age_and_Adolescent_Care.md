# Medivale Family Medicine

## 13D. School-Age and Adolescent Care

School decline may reflect sleep, bullying or learning difficulty; sports visits may uncover syncope, restrictive eating or concealed injury. Hear patient and caregiver, connect symptoms to function and safety, and obtain a private history. Screening scores organize assessment but do not establish diagnoses.

Use age-specific pathways for ADHD, puberty, mental health and sports participation. AAP confidentiality guidance does not replace current consent/access law, and a screening recommendation does not determine treatment of a symptomatic patient.

### Build a visit that can identify the real problem

Ask what has changed, when it began and what the patient can no longer do. Review growth, sleep, school attendance, relationships, medicines and chronic conditions. Longstanding difficulty decoding words differs from deteriorating performance after trauma or new illness. Ask about strengths and supports as well as difficulties.

Routinely offer adolescents private time, explaining confidentiality and safety limits before sensitive questions. Explore home, education, activities, nutrition, peers, sexuality, substances, emotional safety and online experiences. Ask about coercion/violence without appearance-based assumptions, preferred forms of address and safe communication. Involve caregivers when safe. [AAP adolescent confidentiality policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/153/5/e2024066326/197124/Confidentiality-in-the-Care-of-Adolescents-Policy)

### Investigate school difficulties before assigning ADHD

For poor school performance, establish timeline, subject pattern, attendance, instruction, language and observations across settings. Review hearing, vision, sleep, mood, trauma, bullying, illness and medicines. Questionnaires cannot distinguish ADHD from these alternatives alone. Obtain school information with permission and assess coexisting problems. [CDC diagnostic evaluation](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/diagnosis/index.html)

ADHD requires developmentally inappropriate symptoms persisting at least six months, several symptoms before age 12, impairment and symptoms in at least two settings. The symptom-count threshold is at least six in the relevant inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity domain through age 16, and at least five from age 17. A child need not be visibly hyperactive to have the inattentive presentation. Establish that another disorder does not better account for the pattern. A teenager's new concentration complaint is not sufficient evidence of childhood-onset ADHD. [CDC clinical diagnostic criteria](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

| School pattern | Additional possibilities | Useful next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistent difficulty across activities and settings | ADHD, often with another condition | Multi-informant developmental and functional assessment |
| Reading or mathematics disproportionately difficult | Specific learning or language difficulty | School-based educational evaluation and targeted instruction |
| Recent decline with withdrawal or missed school | Mood, anxiety, trauma, bullying or illness | Private history and safety/medical assessment |
| Daytime sleepiness, snoring or very short sleep | Sleep-disordered breathing or insufficient sleep | Sleep history and indicated sleep/airway evaluation |

Learning disorders and ADHD may coexist; improved attention does not replace targeted instruction. An individualized education program addresses qualifying special-education needs; Section 504 plans may provide access accommodations. Schools determine eligibility; clinicians supply functional information and check implementation. [AAP ADHD guideline, school-support scope](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/144/4/e20192528/81572/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis)

### Treat ADHD as a monitored longitudinal condition

The CDC/AAP implementation combines medication with behavioral and school interventions from age six. For **ages 4-5**, evidence-based parent behavior-management training and/or behavioral classroom intervention comes first. Methylphenidate can be considered when those measures do not produce sufficient improvement and moderate-to-severe functional disturbance persists; balance risks and seek experienced support. Children under four fall outside the AAP diagnostic/treatment guideline's age scope, although behavioral help and developmental assessment should not wait. Agree on functional goals such as completing work, safer driving or improved relationships; organizational coaching and classroom strategies supplement medication rather than proving that a child simply needs stricter discipline. [CDC treatment implementation](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/treatment/index.html), [AAP ADHD clinical guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/144/4/e20192528/81572/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis)

Stimulants are often effective; nonstimulants may better fit particular risks, preferences or comorbidities. Individualize formulation/timing. Review cardiovascular/psychiatric history and monitor appetite, sleep, growth, pulse, pressure and function. Titrate to tolerable benefit, checking access, adherence and duration of effect rather than grades alone. Teach secure storage and assess diversion. [AAP ADHD guideline, monitoring and adolescent scope](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/144/4/e20192528/81572/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis)

**Clinical Case: difficulty decoding.** A nine-year-old disrupts reading but participates in science and follows multistep chores at home. Slow word recognition and reading avoidance warrants educational assessment plus sensory, sleep and emotional review. Obtain observations across settings before diagnosing ADHD; treat coexisting reading difficulty with targeted instruction.

### Identify mood and anxiety problems without confusing screening with diagnosis

Youth depression may present with irritability, withdrawal, anhedonia, sleep change, somatic symptoms or school refusal. Assess duration, function, previous episodes, mania, substances, medical illness and family history. Anxiety may cause abdominal pain, avoidance, reassurance seeking or separation/evaluation distress. Normal examination or academic success does not exclude either.

USPSTF recommends depression screening at 12-18 and anxiety screening at 8-18 for patients without recognized symptoms or established diagnosis. Positive results need diagnostic assessment and treatment/follow-up access. Assess symptomatic younger children regardless of screening eligibility. Interview directly using developmentally and linguistically appropriate tools. [USPSTF depression screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/screening-depression-suicide-risk-children-adolescents), [USPSTF anxiety screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/screening-anxiety-children-adolescents)

Choose psychotherapy, medication or both by diagnosis, severity, preference and support, with school/sleep/family planning. Antidepressants require a pediatric indication or supported specialist plan, bipolar assessment and early monitoring for activation, worsening mood and suicidality after initiation/dose changes. Risk warnings require monitoring and shared decisions, not blanket withholding. [NIMH teen depression](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/teen-depression), [NIMH depression treatment and monitoring](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression)

### Ask directly about suicide and respond to the level of risk

Use the AAP clinical workflow for suicide-risk screening: universal screening from age 12 when developmentally and medically appropriate, and screening at ages 8-11 when clinically indicated. Under eight, routine screening is not recommended, but warning signs still require direct clinical assessment. Use an instrument validated for the child's age; the linked NIMH outpatient ASQ safety-assessment guide is for ages **10-24**, not automatically every younger child. A depression tool alone does not assess every suicidal patient. Asking clearly does not create suicidal thoughts. A positive screen calls for a safety assessment, not automatic hospitalization or dismissal because the patient denies a plan. This workflow differs from USPSTF's insufficient-evidence conclusion about population suicide screening. [AAP suicide-screening workflow](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/blueprint-for-youth-suicide-prevention/strategies-for-clinical-settings-for-youth-suicide-prevention/screening-for-suicide-risk-in-clinical-practice/)

Assess current thoughts, intent, past behavior, access to lethal means, supports and ability to remain safe. Current imminent risk requires continuous supervision and emergency psychiatric evaluation; do not send the patient away alone to arrange an appointment. For nonimminent risk, make a collaborative safety plan, involve a safe caregiver and restrict lethal means. In its age scope, NIMH advises a check-in within **48 hours when possible** for positive screens; when further evaluation is needed, arrange it promptly, preferably within **72 hours**, rather than handing over an untracked referral. A promise not to self-harm is not a safety intervention. Explain necessary disclosure to those responsible for protection. [NIMH outpatient youth safety assessment](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-conducted-at-nimh/asq-toolkit-materials/youth-outpatient/youth-outpatient-brief-suicide-safety-assessment-guide)

### Assess eating and substance use across body sizes and backgrounds

Ask about food restriction, bingeing, purging, exercise, body image and fear of weight gain; restrictive intake may also arise from sensory aversion or fear of choking without weight-shape concerns. Review the growth trajectory and rate of weight change rather than relying on today's BMI. Bradycardia, orthostatic symptoms, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance or inability to eat may require hospital assessment even at a normal or high weight. Stable outpatient care needs medical, nutritional and psychological follow-up; family-based treatment is a first-line psychological approach for adolescent anorexia nervosa. [SAHM restrictive-eating-disorder position paper](https://adolescenthealth.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Medical-Management-of-Restrictive-ED-in-AYA.pdf)

Normal BMI or initial chemistry does not establish medical stability or remove refeeding risk. Section 12B (reading volume 10E) develops the worked case and monitored-restoration principles.

Ask privately about nicotine/vaping, alcohol, cannabis, nonprescribed pills and other substances. S2BI, BSTAD or CRAFFT supports assessment, not diagnosis. Explore frequency, consequences, driving, overdose exposure and reasons for use. Offer brief intervention and age-appropriate treatment, including indicated medicines and overdose/naloxone counseling. Intoxication, dangerous withdrawal or overdose requires immediate medical care. [CDC adolescent substance-use clinical tool](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/media/pdfs/2025/09/Adolescent-Tool_Clinician-Final.pdf)

### Interpret puberty through growth and progression

Record pubertal timing, progression, growth velocity and family pattern. Breast/testicular development reflects gonadal puberty; hair/body odor alone may reflect adrenarche. Review nutrition, illness, treatment and neurologic symptoms. Explain examination, obtain consent/assent and use a chaperone.

The **2026 Endocrine Society guideline** traditionally defines precocious puberty as secondary sexual development before eight in girls or nine in boys, but not every early sign needs immediate imaging. For Tanner B2 breast development at ages 7-8 without CNS findings, reassess growth/staging every 4-6 months. It also suggests a 4-6-month observation period for isolated initial B2 before seven to establish tempo. Tanner B3 or higher, progression to B3, growth acceleration or CNS findings warrants prompt evaluation rather than this observation pathway. Distinguish glandular breast tissue from lipomastia. [Endocrine Society recommendations 1-2](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/central-precocious-puberty)

When evaluation for central precocious puberty is indicated, begin with **basal LH using an ultrasensitive assay**; low basal LH does not exclude early disease and may require repeat morning testing or stimulation testing. Brain MRI is not routine for otherwise neurologically normal girls aged 6-8 or boys aged 8-9 with established central precocious puberty; younger patients or CNS symptoms require a different assessment. GnRH-agonist treatment is individualized by progression, expected benefit and family priorities, not prescribed for every isolated early sign. [Endocrine Society recommendations 3-6](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/central-precocious-puberty)

Evaluate absent breast development by 13 or testicular enlargement by 14. Consider constitutional delay, chronic illness, low energy availability, hypothalamic/pituitary and gonadal disease. Review height, bone age and appropriately timed endocrine tests/referral. Headache, visual symptoms, growth deceleration or systemic findings increases urgency. Specialist-directed sex steroids may suit constitutional delay. [Pediatric Endocrine Society: delayed puberty in girls](https://pedsendo.org/patient-resource/delayed-puberty-girls/), [Pediatric Endocrine Society: delayed puberty in boys](https://pedsendo.org/patient-resource/delayed-puberty-boys/)

### Take menstrual symptoms seriously

Ask about menarche, pattern, amount, pain, missed school, pregnancy possibility, medicines and nutrition/exercise. Explain pregnancy testing when biologically possible without relying on identity or a caregiver's account. **No menses by 15**, or absent breast development by 13, merits evaluation. ASRM defines secondary amenorrhea requiring investigation as **more than three months without menses after regular cycles, or six months after irregular cycles**; pregnancy may need exclusion much earlier. Evaluate hypothalamic suppression, thyroid/prolactin disorders, ovarian dysfunction and anatomic causes with history, examination and selected tests. Do not label every irregular early cycle as a chronic ovarian disorder. [ASRM 2024 amenorrhea evaluation](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/current-evaluation-of-amenorrhea/)

Assess stability, anemia/iron status, possible pregnancy, anovulation and bleeding disorders. Bleeding since menarche, bruising, epistaxis or procedural/family bleeding suggests a hemostatic disorder. Normal ferritin during inflammation may not exclude iron deficiency. Unstable bleeding needs urgent care. Stable treatment may use hormonal or antifibrinolytic therapy with contraindication review and indicated hematology input. Treat iron deficiency and arrange reassessment. [ACOG adolescent heavy-bleeding guidance](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2019/09/screening-and-management-of-bleeding-disorders-in-adolescents-with-heavy-menstrual-bleeding)

Do not reserve iron testing for heavy bleeding. AAP's **June 2026 pathway adds universal CBC and ferritin screening for menstruating adolescents at least one year after menarche, no later than age 14**, then symptom/risk-based testing after annual review. Fatigue, pica, low-iron diet, blood donation or abnormal bleeding warrants testing now, even outside a screening window. Ferritin **30 ng/mL or lower** supports deficiency in adolescents/menstruating patients; normal hemoglobin does not exclude it, and inflammation can conceal it. [AAP 2026 iron report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/1/e2026077414/207901/Prevention-Screening-Diagnosis-and-Treatment-of)

For uncomplicated adolescent iron deficiency, AAP recommends oral ferrous sulfate supplying **65 mg elemental iron once daily**, with treatment of the cause. The elemental amount is not 65 mg of ferrous sulfate salt; verify the actual preparation. For iron-deficiency anemia, reassess CBC/reticulocytes at one month and CBC/ferritin at three months to guide duration and recovery of stores. Hemodynamic instability, ongoing major bleeding or severe symptoms requires urgent care rather than routine supplementation alone. The young-child dose and interpretation are in 11A; do not apply adult hematology regimens indiscriminately to children. [AAP treatment/follow-up report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2026-077414/207901/Prevention-Screening-Diagnosis-and-Treatment-of)

**Clinical Case: heavy bleeding and family history.** A 14-year-old soaks protection repeatedly and misses school with fatigue. Prolonged epistaxis and a sibling's dental bleeding suggests a bleeding disorder alongside possible anovulation. Assess stability, iron/anemia, pregnancy when relevant and hemostasis; begin appropriate treatment while coordinating specialty care.

Primary dysmenorrhea often responds to timed NSAIDs or suitable hormonal treatment. Persistent disabling/progressive pain, pain outside menses or poor response requires reassessment. ACOG's 2026 update permits clinical diagnosis and empiric treatment of suspected endometriosis during evaluation, without mandatory prior surgery. Normal imaging does not exclude all disease. [ACOG painful-period overview](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/dysmenorrhea-painful-periods), [ACOG 2026 endometriosis diagnostic update](https://www.acog.org/news/news-releases/2026/02/acog-publishes-new-endometriosis-clinical-guidance-aiming-shorten-time-diagnosis-improve-access-care)

### Offer sexual health care without coercion

Discuss partners, practices, pregnancy goals, protection, consent and exposure sites in the patient's language. Choose contraception by preference, effectiveness, contraindications and access, including optional long-acting methods, condoms and timely emergency contraception. Anticipatory counseling need not await sexual activity. Avoid unnecessary examination barriers and provide appropriate bridging when the preferred method is unavailable. [AAP 2025 adolescent contraception policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2025-072217/202144/Contraception-for-Adolescents-Policy-Statement)

Choose STI tests by anatomy and exposure. CDC recommends annual chlamydia/gonorrhea screening for sexually active females under 25; other populations/sites follow risk. Arrange indicated HIV testing, prevention, treatment, partner care and result review. Pain, fever, pregnancy concern, assault or possible HIV exposure may require time-sensitive care rather than routine screening. [CDC adolescent STI guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/adolescents.htm)

### Protect privacy within the actual law and care system

Consent authority, confidentiality, parental access and mandatory reporting are related but different questions. A minor's ability to consent to a particular service does not create identical rules for every other service or every jurisdiction. Check current state/federal requirements and institutional legal guidance. Under HIPAA, parents usually act as personal representatives, with important exceptions involving legally minor-consented care, court-directed care, an agreed confidential relationship or individualized abuse/endangerment circumstances. There is no nationwide birthday at which all records automatically become private from parents. [HHS December 2025 parental-access clarification](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-letter-hipaa-privacy-rule-and-parental-access-to-minor-childrens-medical-records.pdf)

Check proxy permissions, released results, prescription messages, summaries and insurance explanations of benefits. Ask who controls the phone and portal. Use lawful segmentation and approved workflows, not false documentation, blanket deletion or unsupported promises. The AAP's 2026 technology policy addresses patient access and unauthorized disclosure. Sports forms should not reproduce the private interview. [AAP 2026 health-information technology policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/3/e2025075747/206452/Principles-for-Health-Information-Technology-to)

| Decision | Question to resolve | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permission to treat | Who may legally consent to this service? | Confirm authority, capacity and applicable exceptions |
| Information access | Who may see this specific information? | Apply legal access rules, not a universal age cutoff |
| Safety disclosure | Is protection or reporting legally required? | Explain necessary disclosure and involve a safe team |
| Communication | Where will results and billing information go? | Confirm a lawful safe route and document the plan |

Assess coercion, exploitation and abuse separately from consensual activity. Age differences, threats, dependence and jurisdiction determine safeguarding/reporting. Protect immediate safety, document objectively and involve the appropriate team without confrontational investigation. Caregiver involvement must be safe; neither universal secrecy nor universal reporting promises are appropriate.

**Clinical Case: pharmacy disclosure.** A 17-year-old requests legally minor-consented care, but pharmacy alerts reach an unsafe household member. Confirm confidentiality/access rules and arrange an approved safer communication/dispensing route. Assess immediate danger as well as downstream disclosure.

### Make sports eligibility a clinical decision

AAP's sixth-edition approach favors medical-home evaluation early enough to resolve concerns before the season. Review symptoms, family history, prior restrictions/injury, asthma, heat illness, nutrition/supplements, chronic disease, mental health and emergency plans. Adapt participation to disability and disease; share eligibility information without unnecessary private details. [AAP current preparticipation framework](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/preparticipation-evaluation/)

Exertional syncope/chest pain, unexplained palpitations, suspicious murmur or premature sudden-death family history warrants cardiovascular assessment before clearance. Clarify events before, during and after exercise; collapse during activity differs from faintness after stopping, but neither establishes dehydration. Normal resting examination does not exclude arrhythmia/coronary disease. Target testing/referral; routine ECG/laboratory panels are not universal AAP requirements. [AAP cardiac warning signs](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/sports-injuries/Pages/Sudden-Cardiac-Death.aspx)

| Finding during sports evaluation | Why it matters | Initial response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Syncope during exercise or concerning cardiac history | Potential cardiac disease | Defer clearance pending appropriate evaluation |
| Suspected concussion | Further head injury may worsen risk | Remove from play and assess; no same-day return |
| Focal bone pain, rest pain or persistent limp | Stress injury or another bone/joint disorder | Restrict the provoking activity and investigate |
| Restrictive intake, menstrual change or recurrent stress injury | Possible low energy availability | Assess medical/nutritional risk, not only fitness |

### Manage concussion with gradual recovery, not prolonged isolation

A concussion may follow a blow to the head or body and need not involve loss of consciousness. Remove the athlete from play when suspected, assess for cervical injury and dangerous intracranial features, and prohibit same-day return. Worsening headache, repeated vomiting, focal neurologic change, seizure or declining alertness needs emergency assessment. Concussion is a clinical diagnosis; most uncomplicated cases do not require CT or MRI. Imaging, when indicated, assesses dangerous structural injury rather than proving or disproving every concussion. [CDC immediate sports response](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/response/index.html), [CDC concussion assessment and recovery](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/guidelines/recovery-from-concussion.html)

After a brief period of relative rest, reintroduce tolerable everyday and light physical activity without contact or fall risk. Avoid prolonged bed rest, complete social isolation and an indefinite ban on all cognitive work. Most children can return to school within one or two days with symptom-based adjustments, even if mild symptoms remain. Coordinate breaks, reduced workload, testing adjustments and a point of contact; increase demands as tolerated. Persistent or worsening symptoms deserve reassessment, including sleep, mood, headache and vestibular/ocular concerns. [CDC return-to-school guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/guidelines/returning-to-school.html)

Return to contact sport is a separate progression under clinical supervision. CDC's six-step framework moves from ordinary activities through increasing exercise, sport-specific noncontact work, controlled contact and competition, with each step generally taking at least 24 hours. Recurring symptoms mean stop, contact the treating clinician and resume at an appropriate previous stage after recovery. Full-contact clearance requires the appropriate recovery and authorization, not only the athlete's desire to compete or a normal scan. Return to school and early sport rehabilitation can overlap, but regular nonsport activities should be restored without accommodations before completing the sport protocol. [CDC return-to-sports progression](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/guidelines/returning-to-sports.html)

**Clinical Case: school before match clearance.** A 13-year-old goalkeeper has headache and photophobia after collision. Two days later, shortened school sessions with breaks are tolerable but running provokes symptoms. Continue school support, adjust activity and reassess before unrestricted sport clearance. New neurologic deterioration requires urgent evaluation.

Investigate training pain through load changes, specialization, recovery, sleep and nutrition. Apophysitis causes attachment-site pain; stress injury may cause localized bone or rest pain. Avoid analgesia to conceal unexplained limp. Modify load, rehabilitate and graduate return. Energy deficiency affects any sex/body size; menstrual suppression is not evidence of healthy training. [AAP overuse, overtraining and burnout report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/153/2/e2023065129/196435)

### Examine the hip when a child reports knee pain

Observe gait safely and examine spine, abdomen, hip, adjacent joints and limb. Review trauma, fever, infection, duration, night pain, weight and baseline function; compare motion, tenderness, swelling and neurovascular findings. Acute non-weight-bearing, severe limitation, systemic illness or concerning injury needs prompt assessment. Minor trauma does not exclude infection, malignancy or abuse; low-risk observation requires arranged reassessment. [RCH October 2025 limp guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/The_limping_or_non-weight_bearing_child/)

Normal early radiographs do not exclude a stress fracture, osteomyelitis or early Perthes disease. An effusion on ultrasound does not distinguish infection from transient synovitis. Select blood counts, inflammatory markers, cultures, targeted imaging or MRI for the clinical question. Normal markers do not absolutely exclude infection, and a prediction score is not a universal rule-out test. Persistent pain or failure to improve should reopen the differential rather than extend a provisional benign label indefinitely. [Queensland pediatric limp assessment](https://www.childrens.health.qld.gov.au/for-health-professionals/queensland-paediatric-emergency-care-qpec/queensland-paediatric-clinical-guidelines/limp)

| Pattern | Important consideration | What not to miss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fever, marked pain with passive motion, refusal to bear weight | Septic arthritis or bone infection | Urgent specialist-capable assessment; absence of fever does not exclude infection |
| Well child after a viral illness with improving hip symptoms | Transient synovitis is possible | Worsening, persistent symptoms or systemic change needs reassessment |
| Gradual limp in a younger school-age child | Perthes disease | Hip pathology may present as thigh or knee pain |
| Adolescent with hip/thigh/knee pain and restricted internal rotation | Slipped capital femoral epiphysis | Stop weight bearing and obtain urgent orthopedic assessment |
| Persistent night/rest pain, systemic or hematologic findings | Malignancy, inflammation or other serious disease | Do not label as growing pains without an appropriate evaluation |

### Separate septic arthritis from a reassuring postviral story

Septic arthritis threatens cartilage and adjacent structures. Arrange urgent pediatric/orthopedic care, blood cultures and imaging. Stable observed children may undergo joint sampling before antibiotics; ill or rapidly deteriorating children need immediate empiric therapy after blood cultures if feasible, without awaiting aspiration. Coordinate drainage and targeted treatment. [PIDS/IDSA pediatric bacterial-arthritis guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/acute-bacterial-arthritis-in-pediatrics2/)

Consider transient synovitis only after serious causes, with a compatible improving course in a comfortable, well child. Provide analgesia, activity adjustment and review. Fever, worsening/persistent pain, inability to walk despite analgesia or caregiver concern warrants reassessment. Normal tests or low prediction scores do not guarantee a sterile joint.

Perthes disease involves impaired femoral-head blood supply and remodeling that may leave deformity. A gradual limp, limited hip motion or hip/thigh/knee pain, often at ages four to eight, warrants orthopedic assessment; early imaging may be normal. Management depends on age, extent, motion and containment, using activity modification, motion-preserving care or surgery. [AAOS Perthes disease overview](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/perthes-disease/)

### Treat suspected SCFE as non-weight-bearing until evaluated

In slipped capital femoral epiphysis, displacement occurs through the growth plate. Suspect it with adolescent limp or hip/thigh/knee pain, reduced internal rotation or obligatory external rotation during flexion. Increased weight is a risk factor, not a requirement. Stop weight bearing, avoid forceful manipulation and arrange urgent orthopedic care. For suspected acute/unstable slips, the RCH pathway uses AP pelvis and cross-table lateral imaging, not forced frog-leg positioning. Orthopedics directs stabilization and assessment of the other hip. [RCH SCFE emergency guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/fractures/Slipped_upper_femoral_epiphysis_SUFE_-_Emergency_Department/)

**Clinical Case: knee pain from the hip.** A 12-year-old has weeks of atraumatic knee pain, no knee swelling, limp and outward hip rotation on flexion. Suspect SCFE, stop weight bearing and arrange urgent imaging/orthopedics without repeatedly testing gait. Lack of obesity does not exclude it.

### Make transition a process, not a birthday discharge

Begin transition preparation in early adolescence. Got Transition covers policy, tracking, readiness, planning, transfer and confirmation rather than a fixed age. Even with the same family physician, adult consent, privacy and self-management roles change. Apply disease-specific planning and current law separately. [Got Transition six-element framework](https://gottransition.org/six-core-elements/index.cfm)

Teach medicine identification, urgent-symptom recognition, refills, appointments and care explanations, adapted to language, cognition, sensory needs and access. Supported decision-making preserves autonomy; intellectual disability does not eliminate all capacity. Review caregiver/proxy roles under current law and patient preferences. [Joint transition report, self-management principles](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/142/5/e20182587/38577/Supporting-the-Health-Care-Transition-From)

Transfer diagnoses, procedures, treatment, allergies, monitoring, emergency plans, pending results and contacts. Resolve insurance, transport, accommodations and prescriptions; confirm receiving-clinician acceptance and attendance. Include disease-specific specialist plans and assign unfinished tests/refills. [Joint transition report, transfer principles](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/142/5/e20182587/38577/Supporting-the-Health-Care-Transition-From)

**Clinical Case: refill readiness.** An 18-year-old with epilepsy is moving for vocational training with only days of medicine left; a caregiver handles refills. Secure treatment continuity, practice refill/appointment skills with chosen support, transfer emergency/monitoring plans and confirm acceptance. Preserve useful assistance while building independence.

### Close the loop on the plan

Assign school communication, screen/confidential-result review, injury reassessment and transfer confirmation. Use teach-back, maintain prevention/immunization through 11A and provide an immediate route for urgent concerns.
