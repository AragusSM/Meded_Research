# Medivale Family Medicine

## 13C. Common Childhood Illness

Cough may reflect viral illness, pneumonia, asthma, aspiration or respiratory failure; vomiting may reflect gastroenteritis, UTI or obstruction. Assess severity and dangerous alternatives alongside the likely diagnosis, and arrange reassessment when the course changes.

UCSF antimicrobial guidance, GINA 2026 asthma recommendations, NICE bronchiolitis/gastroenteritis pathways and AAP 2026 UTI guidance provide the principal pathways. The IDSA/PIDS 2026 pneumonia update concerns pleural complications. See 11B for neonatal illness.

### Establish severity before assigning a diagnosis

Assess changes in activity, interaction, breathing, intake, urine, pain, sleep and walking/play. Take impaired arousal seriously despite reassuring isolated vital signs. Examine airway, breathing, circulation, hydration and mental status. Use age-specific respiratory/heart rates and interpret saturation with the examination.

Apnea, cyanosis, markedly reduced air entry, exhaustion, altered consciousness, poor perfusion, severe dehydration or signs of obstruction require urgent care. A decreasing respiratory rate in an exhausted child may indicate failure rather than recovery. Young infants, immunocompromised children and those with complex cardiopulmonary disease need a lower threshold for assessment. Ask about immunization, medicines, recent antibiotics, sick contacts, travel, exposures and access to follow-up without using demographic labels as biological risk substitutes.

Prescribe from current kilogram weight, distinguishing mg/kg/day from mg/kg/dose and checking maximum dose and liquid concentration. Establish the actual allergy reaction. Organ dysfunction, recent therapy, local resistance and ability to take/obtain medicine affect selection. Explain expected improvement and escalation.

### Diagnose ear and upper respiratory infections precisely

A red tympanic membrane during crying is not sufficient evidence of acute otitis media. Look for middle-ear fluid with convincing inflammatory findings: substantial bulging, or new otorrhea not explained by otitis externa; mild bulging needs compatible recent pain or marked inflammation. Pneumatic otoscopy or tympanometry can support the assessment. Otitis media with effusion is fluid without the acute inflammatory syndrome and does not routinely need antibiotics. Treat pain and reassess persistent hearing or language concerns rather than repeatedly prescribing for residual fluid. [CDC pediatric diagnostic and stewardship summary](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/pediatric-outpatient.html)

Using UCSF's AOM pathway, observation for 48-72 hours is an option for an immunocompetent child aged 6-23 months with unilateral, nonsevere illness and no otorrhea, or from age two with nonsevere illness and no otorrhea. Ensure reassessment is feasible. Bilateral AOM before two, otorrhea, moderate/severe pain, pain lasting at least 48 hours, or temperature at least 39 C favors treatment. First-line oral **amoxicillin is 45 mg/kg per dose twice daily, maximum 1000 mg per dose**. Amoxicillin in the preceding 30 days, purulent conjunctivitis or selected failures favors amoxicillin-clavulanate at the same **amoxicillin-component** dose. Use 10 days under two or for severe illness, 7 days at 2-5 years, and 5 days above five for nonsevere illness. [UCSF AOM pathway](https://idmp.ucsf.edu/content/acute-otitis-media)

For high-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate in a child under 40 kg, UCSF favors the 600 mg/42.9 mg per 5 mL ES formulation to limit clavulanate exposure; other concentrations are not mL-for-mL substitutes. A 20-kg child prescribed 900 mg amoxicillin per dose needs 7.5 mL of that formulation twice daily, not 900 mg of each ingredient. These routine regimens assume suitable renal function and no relevant beta-lactam allergy; renal impairment, immediate allergy or failed therapy needs a separate plan. Provide analgesia whether antibiotics are used or not. [UCSF formulation and maximum-dose guidance](https://idmp.ucsf.edu/content/maximum-dosing-for-amoxicillin-and-amoxicillin-clavulanate-0)

For AOM worsening or persisting at 48-72 hours, re-examine diagnosis, dose, adherence/access and alternative fever sources. Postauricular redness/swelling, pinna displacement, neurologic findings or persistent serious illness needs urgent mastoid/intracranial assessment. Recurrent AOM or persistent effusion needs hearing/ENT follow-up, not continuous antibiotic prophylaxis.

Colored nasal mucus alone does not establish bacterial sinusitis. A bacterial course is more likely with symptoms persisting more than 10 days without improvement, worsening after initial recovery, or fever at least 39 C with purulent discharge for at least three consecutive days. Uncomplicated disease usually does not need imaging. A short observation option applies to selected persistent, nonsevere cases, not severe or worsening disease. Periorbital swelling with painful/restricted eye movement, reduced vision or neurologic symptoms needs urgent evaluation for extension. Ordinary viral colds need supportive care, not antibiotics or routine multidrug cough/cold preparations. [CDC pediatric sinusitis and viral-URI guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/pediatric-outpatient.html)

For sore throat, clear viral features such as cough, coryza, hoarseness or oral ulcers make routine group A streptococcal testing unnecessary. In compatible illness without clear viral features, test rather than diagnosing by exudates alone. A negative rapid antigen test in a symptomatic child aged three or older needs backup culture and a plan to act on it; this is not automatically the rule for every molecular assay. Treat confirmed GAS with oral **amoxicillin 50 mg/kg once daily, maximum 1000 mg, for 10 days**, or 25 mg/kg per dose twice daily, maximum 500 mg per dose, for 10 days. A positive test can reflect carriage during another illness. Trismus, drooling, muffled voice, neck limitation or asymmetric swelling requires evaluation beyond routine pharyngitis. [CDC GAS guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/strep-throat.html)

**Clinical Case: backup culture.** A nine-year-old with fever, sore throat and tender anterior nodes but no cough/rhinorrhea has a negative rapid antigen test. Obtain backup culture and assign contact if positive. A sibling with cough, oral ulcers and congestion does not automatically need testing; viral symptoms and carriage affect interpretation.

### Distinguish wheeze, stridor and lower-airway obstruction

Wheeze usually reflects narrowed intrathoracic airways and is often more apparent during expiration. Stridor suggests a larger-airway problem and may be inspiratory or biphasic. Sudden coughing or unilateral breath-sound change during eating or play suggests an aspirated object; a normal radiograph does not exclude it. Persistent suspicion requires airway-capable assessment, often bronchoscopy, rather than reassurance from the film.

If coughing is effective, allow coughing and observe. **Severe choking with an ineffective cough or inability to vocalize requires immediate emergency activation.** Under AHA/AAP 2025 guidance, a conscious infant receives cycles of **five back blows and five chest thrusts**, never abdominal thrusts; a conscious child from age one receives **five back blows and five abdominal thrusts**. If unresponsive, start CPR beginning with compressions and inspect for a visible object before breaths; never perform a blind finger sweep. Do not wait for imaging or for a minute of failed maneuvers before summoning help. [AHA/AAP pediatric basic life support, foreign-body obstruction](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/pediatric-basic-life-support)

Keep significant upper-airway obstruction calm and in a position of comfort while arranging expert care. Avoid forced throat examination or imaging that delays stabilization. Toxic appearance, fever, drooling or rapid deterioration suggests epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis or deep-neck infection rather than uncomplicated croup. [RCH acute upper-airway obstruction guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Acute_upper_airway_obstruction/)

| Presentation | Useful distinction | Immediate emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coryza followed by diffuse crackles/wheeze in an infant | Bronchiolitis is possible; assess intake and apnea risk | Support breathing and hydration; do not automatically prescribe asthma medicines |
| Recurrent variable wheeze, exercise or nighttime symptoms | Asthma becomes more likely, especially with interval symptoms | Assess control and document diagnostic evidence when feasible |
| Barking cough, hoarseness and stridor | Croup is likely if the course and examination are typical | Steroid treatment; monitored epinephrine for more substantial obstruction |
| Abrupt onset after choking or focal breath-sound abnormality | Aspirated object may be present despite a normal film | Urgent airway-capable assessment |

### Support bronchiolitis without unnecessary treatment

Bronchiolitis typically follows coryza with cough, increased effort and diffuse wheeze/crackles, often in the first year. Feeding may decline; young infants may present with apnea. One episode does not establish asthma. Focal findings, unusual fever, recurrence or an atypical course warrants reconsidering pneumonia, heart disease or airway pathology.

The named NICE bronchiolitis pathway prioritizes clinical assessment, oxygen when indicated and adequate fluids. It does not recommend routine radiographs, blood tests, antibiotics, albuterol, nebulized epinephrine or corticosteroids for uncomplicated bronchiolitis. Consider gentle upper-airway suction when secretions interfere with feeding or breathing rather than routine deep suction. Admission and oxygen decisions include persistent room-air saturation below 90% from six weeks, or below 92% under six weeks or with relevant underlying disease, plus apnea, respiratory distress and intake. These treatment/admission thresholds are not a rule to delay referral until a child crosses them. [NICE bronchiolitis recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng9/chapter/Recommendations)

Feeding failure, recurrent apnea or exhaustion may require admission despite adequate oxygenation. Check probe placement and borderline readings against the examination. Explain early worsening and return signs; monitor feeding/hydration and escalate respiratory support when effort becomes unsustainable.

### Recognize asthma and treat both inflammation and obstruction

Asthma causes variable respiratory symptoms with variable expiratory airflow limitation. Recurrent wheeze, cough or breathlessness outside colds, exercise symptoms and nocturnal symptoms support the diagnosis, but none is sufficient alone. Obtain spirometric evidence when age and cooperation permit, preferably before long-term treatment when safe. A normal examination between episodes does not exclude asthma. Assess symptom frequency, activity restriction, previous severe attacks, steroid courses, inhaler technique, adherence and environmental exposure. Do not simply escalate medicines when the inhaler never reaches the lungs.

GINA 2026 recommends ICS-containing treatment for children aged 6-11 rather than SABA-only care. Infrequent symptoms may use an age-appropriate anti-inflammatory reliever strategy or ICS whenever SABA is taken; more frequent symptoms often need daily controller treatment. Device, formulation, local approval and access determine the usable regimen. Give a written action plan and demonstrate spacer use. A long-acting bronchodilator is not asthma monotherapy. [GINA 2026 summary, pediatric scope](https://ginasthma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/GINA-Summary-Guide-2026-WEB-WMS.pdf)

Preschool wheeze needs a separate assessment because spirometry is often unavailable and not every child with viral-associated wheeze has persistent asthma. Consider repeated episodes, interval symptoms, response to an appropriately supervised treatment trial and alternative diagnoses. The GINA 2026 strategy supports daily low-dose ICS for young children with poor control or a severe exacerbation history, with reassessment of benefit and technique. Do not copy an older child's combination-inhaler dose or device into a toddler's plan. [GINA 2026 full strategy, young-child section](https://ginasthma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GINA-2026-Strategy-Report-WMS.pdf)

An acute asthma attack requires assessment of speech/feeding, effort, air entry, oxygenation and mental status. A quiet chest with exhaustion is dangerous. For a **moderate attack from age six**, GINA's primary-care pathway uses **albuterol 4-6 puffs by pMDI/spacer**, up to three treatments 20-30 minutes apart if needed, with repeated assessment. These counts use 100 mcg metered per puff, commonly 90 mcg delivered on a US label. Give one puff into the spacer at a time. Significant attacks need early systemic steroid; for ages 6-11, **prednisolone 1-2 mg/kg/day, maximum 40 mg/day, for 3-5 days** is a GINA regimen. This is not the preschool schedule. Severe or deteriorating illness requires emergency transfer while treatment starts, not repeated unsupervised home doses. Add oxygen/ipratropium as indicated and arrange review within **2-5 days** after a child's attack, with an ICS-containing prevention plan and usable supplies. Detailed devices and guideline-versus-US-label distinctions are in 03B. [GINA 2026 acute-care and follow-up recommendations](https://ginasthma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/GINA-Summary-Guide-2026-WEB-WMS.pdf)

### Treat croup while watching for dangerous alternatives

Typical croup produces a barking cough, hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor after a viral prodrome. Stridor only with agitation is less concerning than persistent stridor at rest with retractions, but appearance and air entry remain essential. The CPS pathway uses **dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg orally once**. For moderate/severe obstruction, monitored nebulized epinephrine provides faster temporary relief: **5 mL of 1 mg/mL L-epinephrine** (the concentration historically called 1:1000) is a standard CPS dose. This is a nebulized regimen, not an IV or IM prescription. It does not replace dexamethasone. Observe for **2-4 hours after epinephrine** and discharge only if relief persists without recurrence and follow-up is safe. Persistent obstruction or repeated doses needs reassessment and possible admission. [CPS croup guidance, updated March 2026](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/acute-management-of-croup)

**Clinical Case: observation after epinephrine.** A 20-month-old with barking cough, rest stridor and retractions improves after monitored nebulized epinephrine and dexamethasone. Observe for sustained relief before discharge. Marked drooling, muffled voice, high fever or little barking cough instead requires diagnostic reassessment and airway-capable care.

### Diagnose pneumonia in context and identify complications

Pneumonia may cause fever, tachypnea, distress, hypoxemia, focal crackles or reduced air entry; lower-lobe disease may cause abdominal pain. Findings overlap viral illness/asthma and do not identify the organism alone. Assess age, immunization, comorbidity and trajectory. Young infants need separate pathways; hypoxemia, major distress, toxicity, dehydration or inability to take treatment favors hospital care.

Stable uncomplicated outpatient pneumonia generally needs no routine radiograph, blood culture or broad laboratory panel. Image severe disease, admission, diagnostic uncertainty, suspected effusion or poor response. Interstitial appearances do not prove atypical pathogens, and viral positivity does not exclude bacterial coinfection. [UCSF pediatric CAP assessment and imaging principles](https://medconnection.ucsfbenioffchildrens.org/pneumonia-guidelines)

Respiratory viruses account for many preschool cases; antibacterial treatment is not routinely needed when the clinical assessment supports a viral cause. For suspected uncomplicated typical bacterial CAP from three months, UCSF's maintained antimicrobial pathway uses amoxicillin, 45 mg/kg per dose twice daily, maximum 1000 mg per dose, with a five-day outpatient course. This is a named institutional regimen, not a universal duration for every pneumonia. Severe or complicated disease, unusual hosts, inadequate response and resistant pathogens require a different plan. Do not routinely add a macrolide to every preschool child's amoxicillin; atypical disease is less common in this age group. [UCSF current pediatric CAP antimicrobial guidance](https://idmp.ucsf.edu/guidelines-empiric-therapy-categories/pneumonia-community-acquired)

Arrange reassessment if the child worsens or fails to improve over the expected 48-72 hours; deterioration warrants earlier action. Reconsider adherence, diagnosis, pathogen and complications rather than repeatedly extending the same prescription. Persistent fever, pleuritic pain, asymmetric reduction in breath sounds or increasing distress can indicate parapneumonic fluid or empyema. The IDSA/PIDS 2026 update favors ultrasound to characterize moderate/large effusions when available. Small uncomplicated effusions may be observed, whereas large, purulent or moderate effusions with respiratory compromise generally need drainage and inpatient specialty care. Do not transfer adult pleural regimens automatically to children. [IDSA/PIDS 2026 pediatric pneumonia update](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/community-acquired-pneumonia-in-infants-and-children/)

**Clinical Case: worsening pneumonia.** A four-year-old develops increasing chest pain, persistent fever, harder breathing and reduced basal air entry after two treatment days. Reassess urgently for effusion or another complication, with imaging/hospital care as indicated. Worsening breathing, rather than fever at an arbitrary hour, changes the decision.

### Replace gastrointestinal losses and reassess the diagnosis

Gastroenteritis commonly produces acute vomiting and loose stools, but assess hydration and the possibility of another cause. Ask about duration, blood, urine, exposures, travel, antibiotics, intake and whether vomiting is green. Examine mental status, perfusion, mucosa, tears and serial weight when available. No single sign precisely measures the deficit. Shock, altered consciousness, severe focal pain, distention, bilious vomiting or an unexpectedly ill appearance requires a broader evaluation. A diarrheal episode does not rule out intussusception, and a child with DKA or urinary infection may initially be described as having a stomach virus.

For clinical dehydration without shock in a child under five, the named NICE gastroenteritis pathway uses correctly prepared low-osmolarity oral rehydration solution. Its deficit component is 50 mL/kg over four hours, **in addition to maintenance**, with frequent small amounts and repeated assessment. Continue breastfeeding. A 12 kg child therefore has a 600 mL deficit-replacement component, not a 600 mL total allowance for the whole illness. Ongoing losses and maintenance remain separate. Persistent vomiting may require supervised nasogastric delivery; deterioration, shock or failed enteral rehydration requires IV treatment. Significant electrolyte abnormalities need an individualized monitored plan. [NICE childhood gastroenteritis hydration guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg84/chapter/Recommendations)

After rehydration, resume age-appropriate food and full-strength milk. Use correctly mixed ORS, not juice, soda, sports drinks or diluted formula. Reassess persistent losses despite initial drinking; hospital care may be needed if the caregiver cannot deliver or monitor rehydration safely.

Most uncomplicated gastroenteritis does not require stool testing or antibiotics. Bloody diarrhea, severe systemic illness, immunocompromise, an outbreak or a prolonged/unusual course changes the investigation. Avoid routine antimotility drugs in children. When Shiga-toxin-producing E coli is possible, antibiotics may increase risk and should not be given reflexively while the cause is being clarified. Pallor, bruising or reduced urine after diarrhea raises concern for hemolytic uremic syndrome and needs urgent blood counts, kidney assessment and supportive care. [IDSA infectious-diarrhea guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/infectious-diarrhea/)

**Clinical Case: deficit plus ongoing needs.** An alert, perfused two-year-old weighing 12 kg has vomiting and watery diarrhea. Small frequent supervised ORS doses are tolerated. Give 600 mL over four hours for the deficit component, plus maintenance and ongoing losses. Lethargy, poor perfusion or inability to retain fluids requires escalation.

### Separate functional constipation from a surgical abdomen

Painful defecation promotes withholding, hard retained stool, rectal distension and overflow soiling, sometimes mistaken for diarrhea or deliberate behavior. Ask about consistency, size, withholding, fissures, toileting, medicines and urinary symptoms; daily stools do not exclude retention. Assess growth, abdomen and indicated perianal, spinal and neurologic findings with privacy/consent.

Symptoms from birth, delayed meconium beyond 48 hours in a term infant, persistent vomiting with distention, ribbon-like stools or leg/neurologic abnormalities need evaluation rather than automatic treatment as functional constipation. A typical uncomplicated presentation is diagnosed clinically; routine abdominal radiographs and indiscriminate thyroid/celiac testing are unnecessary. Disimpact before maintenance when significant retention is present. The NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN functional-constipation guideline uses oral **PEG 1-1.5 g/kg/day for 3-6 days** for impaction, then **0.4 g/kg/day initially for maintenance**, titrated within the usual 0.2-0.8 g/kg/day range to soft comfortable stools. These are grams, not milligrams or a fixed number of adult capfuls. Check formulation, mixing and age suitability; NICE specifically uses PEG 3350 with electrolytes. Obstruction, dehydration, very young infants or significant renal/electrolyte disease requires individualized assessment, not an unsupervised clean-out. [NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN recommendations 31-38 and dose table](https://naspghan.org/files/documents/pdfs/position-papers/Constipation_Feb_2014.pdf), [NICE constipation pathway](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg99/chapter/Recommendations)

Combine medication with unhurried post-meal toilet sitting, foot support, encouragement and ordinary fluid/fiber intake. Avoid punishment or diet-only treatment of significant retention. Review response within about two weeks; maintenance generally continues at least two months, with symptoms resolved for at least one month before gradual reduction. Continue support through toilet training. Refractory symptoms require review of diagnosis, dose, adherence and toileting, then specialist assessment. The 2025 NASPGHAN refractory-constipation paper does not replace every first-line functional-constipation recommendation. [NASPGHAN refractory-constipation recommendations](https://naspghan.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/J-pediatr-gastroenterol-nutr-2025-Kilgore-Evaluation-and-management-of-pediatric-refractory-constipation-.pdf)

Evaluate focal/progressive pain separately for appendicitis, incarcerated hernia, urinary disease or gonadal torsion. Early appendicitis may include vomiting/loose stools. Repeat examination when needed. Ultrasound is often first-line, but nondiagnostic imaging does not settle a high-risk presentation. Peritonitis, obstruction or shock requires surgical-capable care.

Intussusception is telescoping bowel that can compromise venous drainage and eventually arterial flow. It may cause episodic pain, pallor, vomiting or unexplained lethargy, with periods of normal behavior between episodes. Bloody mucus and a palpable mass are not required, and early loose stools can mislead. Obtain urgent ultrasound and coordinate emergency, radiology and surgical care. In a stable child, image-guided enema reduction may be diagnostic and therapeutic. Shock, peritonitis or perforation makes routine enema reduction inappropriate; stabilize and obtain surgical management. [RCH intussusception guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/intussusception_guideline/)

**Clinical Case: episodic pallor and pain.** A 14-month-old has sudden pallor with legs drawn up, then resumes play. One loose stool, absent blood and no palpable mass do not exclude intussusception. Arrange urgent ultrasound and stabilization as needed. After reduction, follow observation and recurrence precautions.

### Ask about dangerous ingestions in unexplained vomiting

Drooling, painful swallowing, unexplained vomiting or abrupt feeding refusal may reflect an unwitnessed ingestion. Suspected **button battery** ingestion needs immediate emergency assessment and radiographs locating the object through neck, chest and abdomen; an esophageal battery requires emergency removal, not a trial of stool observation. Look for the AP double rim and lateral step-off rather than calling every round object a coin. **Multiple magnets, or a magnet plus metal**, require urgent specialist assessment even without symptoms; apparent single magnets can overlap on a film. [RCH foreign-body ingestion pathway](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Foreign_body_ingestion/)

For suspected recent lithium coin-cell ingestion, the National Capital Poison Center permits **10 mL honey every 10 minutes, up to six doses**, only when the child is **at least 12 months**, can swallow and ingestion was **within 12 hours**. Do not delay transport/removal to give honey; otherwise keep the patient without oral intake until the battery's location is established. Never induce vomiting. Medication or chemical exposure requires poison-center/toxicology advice and stabilization, not empiric emesis or home charcoal. [National Capital Poison Center battery guideline](https://www.poison.org/battery/guideline)

### Diagnose pediatric UTI using symptoms and a reliable specimen

Infants may present with unexplained fever or poor feeding; older children may describe dysuria, frequency, urgency or new wetting. Ask about flank pain, vomiting, previous cultures, anatomy and bowel/bladder symptoms. Consider vulvovaginal irritation and other causes of dysuria. The AAP 2026 framework addresses children from eight days to five years, primarily with low medical complexity. Young-infant fever still needs volume 11B's age-specific infection assessment; a positive urine result does not exclude bacteremia or meningitis.

Obtain urine before antibiotics when feasible without delaying treatment of an unstable child. Use a properly collected clean-catch sample when possible, or catheterization when required. A bag sample may help decide whether a better specimen is needed but does not reliably confirm UTI by culture. Interpret symptoms, inflammation, collection method, organism and colony count together. Negative nitrites do not rule out infection. Growth of several organisms often indicates contamination rather than a reason to prescribe broader therapy.

Interpret provisional urinalysis differently from inflammation assessed with culture. The table uses WBC per high-power field in an **uncentrifuged specimen** and LE for leukocyte esterase. Check laboratory methods before substituting automated counts or other microscopy. An ill infant with equivocal UA still needs evaluation.

| Age within the AAP diagnostic scope | While culture is pending | Once culture is available, inflammatory support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Younger than two months | At least 5 WBC/hpf or trace LE | At least 5 WBC/hpf or trace LE |
| Two months to five years | At least 10 WBC/hpf or 2+ LE | At least 5 WBC/hpf or 1+ LE |

With compatible illness and inflammation, the companion uses at least **10,000 CFU/mL of a single pathogenic organism for catheter/suprapubic specimens** and at least **100,000 CFU/mL for clean catch**. Do not apply the same threshold to every collection method or label every positive culture symptomatic infection. Obtain a culture at a lower inflammatory threshold when indicated; the higher provisional-treatment threshold is not a reason to discard every sample below it. [AAP 2026 UTI diagnostic companion](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/4/e2026078566/209462/Urinary-Tract-Infection-Diagnosis-A-Companion-to)

### Match UTI treatment to age, site and risk

Choose antibiotics by local susceptibility, prior cultures, allergy and infection site; narrow or stop after culture review. Avoid bladder-only drugs for kidney infection. Oral treatment beyond 28 days is appropriate within AAP scope only if tolerated and hospitalization is otherwise unnecessary; sepsis, vomiting, dehydration or instability changes care. Infants 8-28 days start parenterally, transitioning individually after improvement.

For community-onset febrile UTI from two months, with susceptible local organisms and suitable renal function, UCSF uses oral **cephalexin 25 mg/kg per dose three times daily, maximum 500 mg per dose, for seven days**. This lies within AAP's 5-7-day low-risk range; shorter courses do not fit every infant. Narrow or stop after culture review. Use AAP 2026 diagnostic thresholds with this regimen, not UCSF's older colony-count criteria. Susceptibility to one cephalosporin does not establish susceptibility to all others. [UCSF antimicrobial selection and dosing](https://idmp.ucsf.edu/content/urinary-tract-infections-community-onset)

For low-risk children, suggested durations are 3-5 days for nonfebrile cystitis from age two, and 5-7 days for UTIs at 2-24 months or suspected pyelonephritis in older children within scope. The issuer's age ranges overlap at exactly 24 months: use localization, risk and an approved pediatric protocol, not age alone, to choose a course. Infants under two months, bacteremia, abnormal anatomy or poor response may need longer treatment. These conditional recommendations do not establish a universal three-day course. Reassess persistent fever or poor response for resistance, obstruction, abscess or another diagnosis. [AAP 2026 pediatric UTI guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/4/e2026078565/209460/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis-and)

### Use imaging to answer the right urinary question

Renal/bladder ultrasound assesses structure but does not exclude vesicoureteral reflux, the backward flow of urine toward the kidneys. VCUG evaluates reflux more directly. Select imaging for its question rather than ordering a standard package after every dysuria episode. [AAP 2026 ultrasound and reflux technical report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/4/e2026078567/209464/A-Systematic-Review-and-Meta-Analysis-of-the-Role)

AAP recommends renal/bladder ultrasound after first febrile UTI under five, not necessarily during an uncomplicated acute episode. Routine VCUG is discouraged with normal ultrasound; important abnormalities, recurrence or atypical/high-risk disease alters that decision. Seek specialty input for structural disease or a concerning course and review prior cultures, treatment and imaging at recurrence.

Address withholding, dysfunctional voiding and constipation. AAP generally advises against routine prophylaxis for grade I-II reflux without bowel/bladder dysfunction; grade III risk is uncertain. High-grade reflux, scarring and other high-risk findings need individualized specialty care. Isolated functional constipation does not automatically meet the high-risk BBD category. Prophylaxis decisions remain individualized where evidence and subspecialist recommendations differ. [AAP 2026 guideline and implementation resources](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/urinary-tract-infection-clinical-practice-guideline/)

**Clinical Case: collection-dependent thresholds.** A three-year-old with fever, dysuria and vomiting has 15 WBC/hpf and a single E coli strain at 20,000 CFU/mL from catheter urine. This supports UTI under AAP 2026 despite being below the older 50,000 threshold. Mixed growth from poor clean catch differs. Assess systemic illness/oral tolerance, treat and arrange indicated post-febrile imaging.

### Read the illness around a childhood rash

Assess rash morphology, distribution, blanching, mucosa, palms/soles, pain/itch and fever sequence, with medicines, vaccination, exposure, travel and immune history. Rapid progression, marked pain, detachment, severe mucosal disease, poor perfusion or nonblanching rash with systemic illness requires urgent care. Early blanching does not exclude meningococcal disease. [RCH meningococcal recognition guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Acute_meningococcal_disease/)

| Pattern | Useful clinical association | Important caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High fever settles, then a trunk-predominant rash appears | Roseola in a young child | Reassess if the child remains seriously ill rather than assuming every post-fever rash is benign |
| Red cheeks followed by a lacy eruption | Parvovirus B19 | Hemolytic disorders, immunocompromise and pregnancy exposure need additional advice |
| Painful oral lesions with hand/foot eruption | Hand-foot-and-mouth disease | Pain can prevent drinking even when skin disease is mild |
| Itchy crops with lesions in different stages | Varicella | Vaccinated cases may have fewer or less vesicular lesions |
| Fever, sore throat and a fine rough eruption | Scarlet fever is possible | Confirm compatible GAS infection; the rash alone is not an antibiotic indication |

Roseola is often recognized by defervescence before the eruption; treatment of uncomplicated illness is supportive. Parvovirus B19 can cause transient marrow suppression, with particularly important consequences in chronic hemolytic disease, and exposure may matter for a pregnant contact. Hand-foot-and-mouth disease commonly needs pain control and hydration support rather than antibiotics. Varicella may require testing in a modified or uncertain presentation, plus appropriate infection-control and high-risk contact management. Avoid aspirin for a routine childhood viral fever. [AAP roseola overview](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/skin/Pages/Roseola-Infantum.aspx), [CDC parvovirus B19](https://www.cdc.gov/parvovirus-b19/about/index.html), [CDC HFMD](https://www.cdc.gov/hand-foot-mouth/signs-symptoms/index.html), [CDC varicella features](https://www.cdc.gov/chickenpox/hcp/clinical-signs/index.html)

Fever with cough, coryza, conjunctivitis and spreading rash, especially after exposure or with incomplete immunity, requires measles isolation, public-health coordination and molecular/serologic testing. Use airborne precautions and keep a potentially infectious child out of a shared waiting room. [CDC measles clinical overview](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/hcp/clinical-overview/)

Persistent fever with conjunctival injection, oral changes, extremity changes, rash or cervical adenopathy raises concern for Kawasaki disease. Incomplete presentations occur, especially in infants; do not wait for every classic sign if the course is concerning. Arrange pediatric assessment and cardiac evaluation because coronary involvement can be clinically silent. Other inflammatory syndromes, including MIS-C after SARS-CoV-2, and invasive infection may overlap. Shock or organ dysfunction requires urgent care rather than an outpatient rash label. [AHA Kawasaki disease overview](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/kawasaki-disease), [CDC MIS-C clinical overview](https://www.cdc.gov/mis/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

### Treat skin infection and eczema without confusing them

Impetigo causes superficial crusted lesions, often around the face or extremities. Limited disease may use topical treatment; numerous lesions generally need an appropriate oral agent covering likely streptococci and staphylococci. Bullous disease, systemic illness or an unusual course warrants reassessment. Cellulitis is a deeper spreading inflammatory infection; a fluctuant collection suggests an abscess needing source-control assessment. Pain out of proportion, rapid progression or toxicity should prompt urgent evaluation for a deeper dangerous infection rather than repeated topical therapy. [CDC impetigo guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/impetigo.html)

Atopic dermatitis combines barrier dysfunction and inflammation, usually with itch, dry skin and a relapsing age-related distribution. Use regular bland emollients and gentle skin care. For an inflammatory flare, AAP describes topical corticosteroid treatment **twice daily until improvement, usually a few days to 2-3 weeks**. Infants or the face at any age generally use low-potency **hydrocortisone 1% or 2.5%**; trunk/limb flares in older children may need a stronger preparation chosen for severity. Thin flexural skin also requires caution. Specify sites and duration, stop daily steroid when the flare resolves, and reassess nonresponse rather than increasing potency everywhere. Moisturizer alone may not control inflammation. [AAP atopic-dermatitis treatment](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/atopic-dermatitis/treatment-of-atopic-dermatitis/)

Before escalating eczema treatment, review product, amount, technique, adherence, irritants and infection. Broad food elimination/testing can harm nutrition; investigate compatible immediate reactions or specific concerns selectively. Severe/recurrent disease may need proactive topical, steroid-sparing or specialist care with age/formulation checks. Fever with painful uniform vesicles or punched-out erosions suggests eczema herpeticum and needs prompt systemic antiviral assessment, especially near eyes. [AAP 2025 skin-directed management update](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-pdf/155/6/e2025071812/1813068/pediatrics.2025071812.pdf)

**Clinical Case: painful erosions in eczema.** A five-year-old with itchy flexural eczema develops fever and uniform painful facial erosions. Suspect eczema herpeticum and arrange urgent assessment rather than merely increasing steroid potency or adding topical antibiotics. Eye involvement increases urgency.

### Finish with a plan the family can carry out

Explain diagnosis/uncertainty, demonstrate medicines/devices and assign cultures, breathing reassessment, imaging or growth review. Provide accessible instructions and an urgent route back for worsening breathing, poor drinking, reduced urine, altered responsiveness, severe pain or unexpected rash. Maintain continuity and preventive/immunization care through 11A.
