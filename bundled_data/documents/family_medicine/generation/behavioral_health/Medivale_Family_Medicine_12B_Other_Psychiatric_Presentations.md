# Medivale Family Medicine

## 12B. Other Psychiatric Presentations

Psychiatric symptoms require medical assessment. Voices may reflect primary psychosis, intoxication, mood illness, delirium, or neurologic disease; normal BMI can coexist with dangerous malnutrition. Negative tests do not make suffering imaginary. Establish the syndrome, immediate safety, and continuing care.

Adult psychosis, adolescent restriction, perinatal illness, and pediatric ADHD require age-, indication-, setting-, and reproductive-specific care. See 12A for mood, suicide, bipolar, and OCD management; 03E for insomnia, apnea, and restless legs.

### Recognize psychosis and decide what cannot wait

Psychosis involves impaired reality testing with delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized thought/behavior. Assess a belief's conviction, evidence appraisal, cultural context, and consequences. Hallucinations occur without an external stimulus; ask modality, onset, frequency, distress, commands, insight, and resistance. Sleep-transition, grief-related, and culturally shared experiences require context. [NIMH psychosis framework](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/understanding-psychosis)

Begin with airway/breathing/circulation when indicated, vital signs, attention, orientation, glucose when relevant, and a focused medical and neurologic examination. An acute fluctuating disturbance of attention or awareness favors delirium and requires investigation of its cause. Fever, new seizures, severe headache, focal findings, autonomic instability, rapidly changing cognition, or a new atypical presentation warrants urgent medical evaluation. Encephalitis, toxic-metabolic disease, medication effects, and withdrawal must not be overlooked because the patient also expresses a bizarre belief.

Assess suicide/violence intent, commands, weapons/medicine access, recent behavior, intake, housing, and dependent care. For commands, establish perceived authority, resistance, intent, and past actions; content alone cannot predict behavior. Calmness or denied suicidality does not establish self-care capacity. Most people with psychosis are not violent.

Use quiet surroundings, one speaker, respectful distance, and simple explanations. Acknowledge distress without endorsing delusions or arguing; seek permission and appropriate trusted support. Substantial risk, failed basic self-care, severe agitation, catatonia, delirium, or instability needs supervised emergency handoff. Assess decision-specific capacity and lawful emergency procedures; diagnosis or disagreement alone does not remove capacity.

**Catatonia** is a psychomotor syndrome, not simply quietness or a synonym for schizophrenia. Suspect it with combinations of stupor, mutism, negativism, posturing, waxy flexibility, echolalia/echopraxia, or purposeless agitation. It can accompany mood illness or medical/neurologic disease. Arrange urgent medical/psychiatric assessment, hydration/nutrition and complication prevention; fever or autonomic instability raises malignant catatonia or neuroleptic malignant syndrome. First-line specialist treatment is benzodiazepines (usually lorazepam) and/or ECT. A monitored lorazepam challenge can support the diagnosis, but response is not perfectly specific; do not reflexively escalate a dopamine blocker. [BAP final catatonia consensus guideline](https://www.bap.org.uk/pdfs/BAP_Guidelines-Catatonia.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 26-year-old has weeks of withdrawal and missed work, believing food is poisoned. Now dehydrated and tachycardic, he identifies no safe food but denies wanting to die. Arrange supervised medical/psychiatric assessment while assessing substances, medicines, mood, and neurologic symptoms; safety cannot await a six-month diagnosis.

### Establish the cause before attaching a lifelong label

Establish timeline and collateral history for function, sleep, mood, trauma, development, and prior episodes. Review cannabis, stimulants, hallucinogens, alcohol/sedative withdrawal, corticosteroids, dopaminergic drugs, and other exposures. Toxicology neither proves causation nor excludes all drugs. Assess infection, endocrine disease, seizures, sensory/cognitive change; select laboratory, pregnancy, toxicology, imaging, or CSF studies for the presentation.

| Clinical pattern | Distinction to establish | Consequence for care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute inattention or fluctuating awareness | Delirium, toxicity, withdrawal, or another medical process | Urgent cause-directed medical assessment |
| Psychosis during a major mood episode | Mania or depression may organize the illness | Treat the mood syndrome and safety needs |
| Persistent nonaffective psychosis | Duration, function, and exclusions determine the diagnosis | Early specialty care; reassess over time |
| New late-life hallucinations | Drugs, sensory loss, delirium, or neurodegeneration may contribute | Broader medical/cognitive evaluation |

Schizophrenia requires more than the presence of voices. The diagnostic framework includes at least two characteristic symptoms during an active phase of about one month, with at least one being delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized speech; functional deterioration and continuous disturbance for at least six months are also required. Mood, substance, and medical explanations must be addressed. Negative symptoms include diminished motivation or expression; cognitive difficulties may impair planning and working memory. Depression, sedation, and social deprivation can resemble these features and deserve separate assessment. [SAMHSA diagnostic framework](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK571021/box/ch4.b31/?report=objectonly)

Brief psychotic disorder lasts **at least one day but less than one month**, with eventual full return to prior function; schizophreniform disorder lasts **at least one month but less than six months** and does not require the functional decline of schizophrenia. Neither duration label determines acute safety. Schizoaffective disorder requires a major mood episode overlapping the schizophrenia syndrome, at least two weeks of delusions/hallucinations without a major mood episode, and major mood episodes occupying most of the total active/residual illness. Psychosis confined to mood episodes instead favors a mood disorder with psychotic features. Establish this longitudinally, not from one interview. [APA psychotic-disorder distinctions](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/schizophrenia/what-is-schizophrenia)

Refer first sustained psychosis promptly to psychiatric/coordinated early-psychosis care. NICE recommends psychiatrist consultation before primary-care antipsychotic initiation. Routine structural imaging is unnecessary in otherwise typical presentations but indicated for suspected neurologic/structural causes. Combine medication, psychotherapy, family support, and work/school assistance. [NICE first-episode recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg178/chapter/Recommendations)

The NICE adult antipsychotic framework obtains baseline weight, waist circumference, pulse/BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, movement findings, and nutritional/activity assessment. ECG is indicated by the product, cardiovascular history/risk, or inpatient admission, not automatically for every outpatient. Check weight weekly for six weeks, then at 12 weeks, one year, and annually; pulse/BP, glucose/HbA1c, and lipids at 12 weeks, one year, and annually. Follow abnormalities sooner. Monitor akathisia, dystonia, sedation, orthostasis, sexual effects, and adherence throughout. Fever, rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability suggest neuroleptic malignant syndrome: arrange emergency evaluation. [NICE adult antipsychotic monitoring](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg178/chapter/Recommendations)

Clozapine is a specialist treatment for appropriate treatment-resistant schizophrenia and selected patients with persistent suicide risk. Severe neutropenia, myocarditis, seizures, and potentially serious gastrointestinal hypomotility require active safeguards. **Removal of the U.S. clozapine REMS does not remove the need for ANC monitoring according to prescribing information.** Reconcile smoking changes because smoke exposure, rather than nicotine alone, can alter concentrations of selected antipsychotics. Do not abruptly change a stable regimen without coordinating the responsible team. [FDA clozapine monitoring update](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-removes-risk-evaluation-and-mitigation-strategy-rems-program-antipsychotic-drug-clozapine)

With normal ANC, the current label monitors weekly for months 1-6, every two weeks for months 7-12, then monthly; neutropenia requires a different algorithm. A treatment interruption needs dose reassessment: after more than two missed days, the label restarts at 12.5 mg once or twice daily, not the old maintenance dose, because hypotension, bradycardia, or syncope can occur. Even shorter interruptions require a reduced restart dose. Contact the prescriber before resuming. Ask about bowel function; abdominal distension/pain, vomiting, or severe constipation warrants urgent assessment. [Clozapine monitoring and interruption instructions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=90876802-0e3a-44c9-9ff7-1754dfbe736a)

### Identify eating disorders across body sizes

Ask privately and neutrally about restriction, weight-gain fears, bingeing, vomiting, laxatives/diuretics, exercise, supplements, and weight-control medicines. Review weight/growth trajectory, endocrine changes, dizziness/syncope, constipation, dental symptoms, injury, and function. Food insecurity, GI disease, dysphagia, and diabetes may coexist. Normal weight, male sex, older age, or athletic appearance does not exclude serious illness. [NIMH eating-disorder overview](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/eating-disorders)

Anorexia nervosa involves restriction leading to significantly low weight for the person's context, fear of gaining weight or persistent behavior preventing gain, and disturbed weight/shape appraisal or recognition of illness. Amenorrhea is not required. Atypical anorexia has the other central features without currently low weight despite substantial weight loss. Bulimia involves recurrent binge eating and inappropriate compensation, with excessive influence of weight/shape on self-evaluation; the binge and compensatory behaviors occur on average at least weekly for three months and not exclusively during anorexia. Binge-eating disorder involves distressing recurrent binges without the regular compensatory pattern; frequency is at least weekly for three months, with associated behavioral features.

ARFID involves restrictive intake related to low interest, sensory aversion, or fear of consequences such as choking, rather than a weight/shape-driven aim. It can produce nutritional deficiency, impaired growth or weight loss, supplement dependence, or marked psychosocial impairment. Assess whether food availability, cultural practices, another disorder, or medical disease better explains the problem. Pica and rumination also merit recognition: investigate nonfood ingestion, toxicity/deficiency risk, or recurrent regurgitation rather than assuming all eating problems are attempts to become thinner. [APA eating-disorder distinctions](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/eating-disorders/what-are-eating-disorders)

### Determine medical stability before choosing the treatment setting

Assess temperature, pulse/BP, orthostasis, hydration, weight trajectory, malnutrition, and purging complications. Select CBC, electrolytes, renal/liver measures, magnesium, phosphorus, and glucose. Restriction, bradycardia, purging, electrolyte abnormalities, or relevant medicines may warrant ECG. Normal electrolytes do not exclude severe compromise. Explain necessary measurements and accommodate distress without abandoning safety.

| Finding or behavior | Why it matters | Appropriate response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Syncope, marked bradycardia, hypothermia, or hypotension | Reduced cardiovascular reserve from malnutrition | Urgent medical stability assessment |
| Purging with potassium loss or prolonged QT | Ventricular arrhythmia risk | Correct abnormalities in an appropriate monitored setting |
| Rapid weight loss at a normal or high BMI | The rate and extent of restriction may be dangerous | Do not defer care until an arbitrary low weight |
| Food refusal, uncontrolled purging, or unsafe mental state | Outpatient nutrition and safety may be unachievable | Escalate the level of multidisciplinary care |
| New weakness, edema, or arrhythmia during refeeding | Electrolyte/fluid shifts may be life-threatening | Urgent reassessment and protocol-based management |

Use age-appropriate stabilization criteria. Adolescent hospitalization factors include severe bradycardia, dehydration, electrolyte/ECG abnormalities, orthostasis, growth failure, food refusal, failed outpatient care, and psychiatric comorbidity. A daytime pulse below 50/min is one concerning finding, not a requirement that all criteria coexist. Adult thresholds differ. [SAHM restrictive-eating position paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864000/)

Starvation reduces energy availability and can suppress reproductive signaling, impair bone accrual, and affect cardiac and gastrointestinal function. Purging can cause electrolyte depletion, alkalosis with vomiting, dental erosion, and salivary swelling. Different purging methods do not produce identical acid-base patterns. During nutritional rehabilitation, insulin-driven intracellular shifts can lower phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium, with thiamine and fluid-balance concerns. Refeeding risk is not a reason to leave the patient malnourished; it is a reason for experienced monitoring and treatment. Avoid a universal outpatient calorie prescription for an unstable patient.

NICE rejects an absolute BMI/weight admission threshold: assess deterioration, monitoring needs, and achievable outpatient nutrition. Stabilization and weight restoration do not alone resolve the disorder. Oral contraceptives do not treat nutritional amenorrhea, and withdrawal bleeding does not establish restored energy balance. [NICE eating-disorder care setting](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng69/chapter/recommendations)

**Clinical Case.** A 16-year-old previously near the upper growth-chart range loses 14 kg over four months through rigid restriction and daily exercise. BMI remains normal, but pulse is 44/min with near-syncope and marked standing dizziness. Arrange urgent stabilization assessment, collateral history, and evaluation of restriction, purging, mood, and safety. Normal initial phosphorus does not eliminate refeeding risk.

### Restore nutrition and address the maintaining behaviors

Coordinate medical, nutritional, and psychological care for adequate intake, function, behavioral recovery, and relapse prevention. Family-based treatment is first-line for adolescent anorexia, with caregivers as resources, not causes. Adults need disorder-focused psychotherapy and individualized nutrition; CBT-based care is central for bulimia/BED. Refer early and match intensity to illness, support, and response.

Medication is not a substitute for nutritional rehabilitation or eating-disorder psychotherapy. Selected antidepressant treatment, particularly fluoxetine in adult bulimia, may complement therapy. Bupropion is contraindicated with current or prior anorexia or bulimia because of seizure risk. Lisdexamfetamine has a U.S. indication for moderate-to-severe binge-eating disorder in adults, **not weight loss**; it is not a general treatment for restrictive disorders or an automatic pediatric BED prescription. Review cardiovascular, psychiatric, misuse, appetite, and sleep risks before considering it. [Current lisdexamfetamine indication and warnings](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=704e4378-ca83-445c-8b45-3cfa51c1ecad)

The adult bulimia fluoxetine target is **60 mg orally each morning**, with titration over several days when appropriate; organ function and interactions can require adaptation. Do not assume the usual initial depression dose is an adequate bulimia trial or use medication instead of correcting purging-related instability. [Fluoxetine bulimia dosing](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=6fff3732-efc3-4cbd-a8fa-cde3181f03e6)

Review intake/compensation, vitals, weight/growth, indicated laboratories, exercise, mood/self-harm risk, and engagement. Address meal support, school/work, dental/bone health, and posthospital transition rather than focusing solely on weight. Escalate fainting, chest symptoms, dehydration, inability to eat, uncontrolled purging, or worsening safety.

### Treat somatic distress without dismissing symptoms

Somatic symptom disorder involves distressing physical symptoms together with disproportionate thoughts, anxiety, or time/energy devoted to them; the symptomatic state is persistent, typically more than six months. Symptoms need not be medically unexplained. A patient with inflammatory disease, cancer, or chronic pain can also have excessive illness-related preoccupation, but ordinary concern about a serious illness is not automatically a psychiatric disorder. Illness anxiety disorder centers on fear of serious disease when somatic symptoms are absent or mild. Neither diagnosis means that symptoms are deliberately fabricated. [APA somatic-symptom framework](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/somatic-symptom-disorder/what-is-somatic-symptom-disorder)

Explore feared illness, prior explanations, and responses to testing. Checking, avoidance, searches, and reassurance may briefly relieve anxiety while reinforcing vigilance. Sleep loss, stress physiology, attention, and activity avoidance can amplify symptoms; explain these interactions between bodily processes and interpretation without attributing everything to one stressor.

Review records and examine current symptoms. Investigate clinical probability and new findings, avoiding repeated low-yield reassurance testing with its incidental findings, complications, costs, and conflicting explanations. New weight loss, bleeding, focal deficits, fever, or other meaningful change warrants reassessment despite a somatic diagnosis.

Use one coordinating clinician, scheduled visits, a shared explanation, and functional goals. Validate symptoms, summarize exclusions and remaining uncertainty, and define reassessment triggers. CBT addresses checking, avoidance, and catastrophic interpretation; treat coexisting mood/anxiety illness. Psychotropics and activity plans require clinical indications and individual tolerability, not symbolic dismissal or universal-cure claims.

Functional neurologic disorder is a related but distinct diagnostic problem. It is supported by positive clinical findings showing a functional pattern, not just normal imaging or a trauma history. Symptoms are involuntary, and neurologic disease may coexist. Factitious disorder involves intentional falsification without an obvious external reward; malingering involves intentional production for an external incentive. Neither should be inferred from frustration, inconsistent recall, disability applications, or negative testing alone. Coordinate neurology and rehabilitation when indicated. [Johns Hopkins functional-neurologic clinical overview](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/expert-qa/functional-neurological-symptom-disorder)

**Clinical Case.** A 39-year-old with treated reflux, stable examination/weight, and appropriate prior testing repeatedly requests pancreatic-cancer scans. Hours of checking and missed work persist despite reassurance. Review overlooked risks/new findings, then schedule follow-up targeting checking and function. Later jaundice or persistent weight loss requires investigation.

### Separate insomnia from psychiatric and withdrawal emergencies

Chronic insomnia means difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep or early awakening despite adequate opportunity, with daytime impairment, generally at least three nights weekly for three months. Distinguish it from insufficient sleep opportunity and circadian misalignment. In **mania**, little sleep may accompany sustained energy, increased activity, pressured speech, or risky behavior rather than next-day exhaustion. New insomnia after reducing alcohol or a benzodiazepine, especially with tremor, autonomic symptoms, hallucinations, confusion, or seizures, requires withdrawal assessment, not an isolated hypnotic prescription. See 12A for bipolar illness and 12C for withdrawal/tapering.

Ask about apnea, restless legs, pain, medicines/substances, and dangerous daytime sleepiness. Do not treat sleep-driving risk or witnessed apnea with an automatic sedative refill. **CBT-I is the core treatment for chronic insomnia**, not sleep-hygiene advice alone; adapt sleep-window restriction when bipolar illness, uncontrolled seizures, excessive sleepiness, or acute instability makes sleep deprivation unsafe. Detailed assessment, behavioral protocols, medication selection, and sleep-disorder differentials belong in **03E, Sleep Disorders**. [VA/DoD insomnia framework](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Provider-Summary_final_20250422.pdf)

Psychiatric comorbidity does not make antipsychotics or benzodiazepines routine chronic-insomnia treatment. Review alcohol/opioid combinations, falls, cognition, respiratory disease, and reproductive circumstances before sedating medication. Conversely, do not abruptly stop a dependent benzodiazepine or remove a drug needed for another indication merely because insomnia is also present.

### Distinguish perinatal depression, intrusive thoughts, and psychosis

Assess persistent despair, impaired function, or frightening perinatal changes rather than assuming adjustment. Review depression/bipolar/psychosis history, prior postpartum illness, family history, support, sleep opportunity, substances, violence, and medical complications. EPDS/PHQ-9 screening requires diagnostic follow-up and treatment access. Assess bipolar illness before antidepressant pharmacotherapy. [ACOG perinatal screening framework](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/patient-screening)

| Presentation | Helpful distinction | Clinical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Postpartum blues | Mild, fluctuating symptoms during the first two weeks | Practical support and reassessment; escalation if severe |
| Perinatal depression | Sustained depressive syndrome with distress or impairment | Assess safety and offer active treatment |
| Intrusive harm obsessions | Unwanted, distressing thoughts with preserved insight | Assess actual risk and arrange OCD-informed care |
| Postpartum psychosis or mania | Delusions, disorganization, loss of insight, or markedly reduced sleep need | Psychiatric emergency and supervised assessment |

Blues should improve within about two weeks; the label must not explain away severe symptoms during that period. Depression can affect sleep even when another caregiver provides an opportunity to rest, and it may include guilt, anhedonia, hopelessness, or suicidal thinking rather than only exhaustion. Assess anemia, thyroid disease, medicines, and other medical contributors as indicated. The broader clinical perinatal period is not identical to every diagnostic specifier or research eligibility window. [NIMH perinatal depression](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/perinatal-depression)

Clarify harm thoughts without equating an unwanted, horrifying image with delusional conviction or intent. Assess insight, intent, behavior, rituals, hallucinations, mood elevation, and safe caregiving. Neither automatic reassurance nor automatic dangerousness labeling is appropriate; obtain urgent specialist help if diagnosis or safety is uncertain.

Postpartum psychosis commonly has abrupt onset with severe insomnia, mood change, confusion, delusions, or disorganization, often in a bipolar-spectrum context. It is an emergency even without a stated plan to cause harm. Arrange immediate psychiatric and medical evaluation, continuous appropriate supervision, and safe infant care; do not leave the affected parent as the sole caregiver while awaiting a routine visit. Fever, seizures, severe headache, hypertension, focal findings, or fluctuating consciousness also require evaluation for postpartum medical/neurologic emergencies. Treatment is specialist-led and may involve antipsychotic/mood-stabilizing treatment or ECT according to the clinical syndrome.

Psychotherapy is an initial option for nonpsychotic perinatal depression, especially mild/moderate illness. Severity, preference, prior response, and access guide medication. Balance untreated illness and fetal/infant exposure; do not abruptly stop effective treatment solely for pregnancy/lactation. ACOG favors SSRIs, with sertraline or escitalopram reasonable without a helpful medication history, individualized to the patient. [ACOG perinatal treatment guideline](https://pnqinma.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Treatment_and_Management_of_Mental_Health.36-OBG-Green-J-June-2023.pdf.pdf)

ACOG's focused update supports considering zuranolone within 12 months after delivery for severe depression beginning in the third trimester or within four weeks postpartum. This population differs from the broader FDA adult PPD indication. It does not treat postpartum psychosis or replace safety/continuing care. Brexanolone is no longer commercially available in the United States. [ACOG focused update, full document](https://pnqinma.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/zuranolone_and_brexanolone_for_the_treatment_of.1381.pdf)

Zuranolone is an oral neuroactive steroid acting through GABA-A receptor modulation, given as a 14-day course. Important safeguards include CNS depression, additive sedative effects, CYP3A interactions, organ-function adjustments, and **no driving or hazardous activity for at least 12 hours after each dose**. Plan another capable caregiver when impairment would affect infant care. Effective contraception is advised during treatment and for one week afterward. Low milk transfer does not establish infant safety: the current label reports no infant-outcome data, so breastfeeding decisions require individualized discussion. Review symptoms and safety during treatment and afterward; completion of a short course is not proof of durable recovery. See 14D for the dose/organ-adjustment example and postpartum feeding follow-up. [Current zuranolone label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=f18e53b0-d0bb-422d-8de7-ab64b7292b29)

**Clinical Case.** Nine days postpartum, a 31-year-old sleeps little despite relatives caring for the baby, believes television sends infant-related instructions, and packs to leave without a destination. Prior low-sleep/impulsive-spending episodes suggest bipolar illness. Arrange immediate supervised emergency assessment, medical evaluation, and safe infant care.

### Evaluate ADHD as a developmental and functional pattern

ADHD involves persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that is developmentally inappropriate and causes impairment. Symptoms last at least six months, several were present before age 12, and several occur in at least two settings. Children through age 16 require at least six symptoms in a relevant domain; age 17 and older requires at least five. Impairment and exclusion of better explanations remain essential. Distractibility during one stressful month or a positive questionnaire alone is insufficient. [CDC diagnostic criteria and assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/hcp/treatment-recommendations/)

Assess lost materials, unfinished multistep tasks, interruptions, impulsivity, and disorganization. Adult hyperactivity may appear as inner restlessness. Strong grades can mask impairment through effort/support, while poor grades alone do not establish ADHD. Explain attention/executive regulation without equating it to laziness, parenting, inability to concentrate on interests, or one unmeasured neurotransmitter.

Use parent/caregiver, teacher, school, and patient reports, developmentally appropriate rating scales, and collateral records. Adults may describe childhood difficulties retrospectively; school reports or family history can help establish the timeline. Assess sleep duration/apnea, hearing/vision concerns, learning/language disorders, anxiety, depression, trauma, autism, tics, substance use, and bipolar symptoms. Symptoms that occur only during mood episodes suggest a different formulation, although disorders can coexist. Neuropsychological tests, imaging, or a computerized attention test do not independently make the diagnosis. [NIMH adult ADHD assessment](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/adhd-what-you-need-to-know)

The AAP pediatric pathway begins with parent training in behavior management and/or behavioral classroom intervention for children aged four to five years. Methylphenidate may be considered when these interventions do not adequately improve persistent substantial impairment; weigh young-age adverse effects and access to behavioral care. For ages six to eleven, combine an appropriate FDA-approved medication with parent/classroom behavioral intervention and educational supports. Adolescents should participate in decisions, including assent for medication, with attention to diversion, driving, substance use, and transition to adult services. [AAP pediatric ADHD guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/144/4/e20192528/81572/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis)

Provide school/work accommodations, organization, routines, and behavioral goals. Adult care may combine medication and ADHD-focused skills. Reassess severe mood illness, substance problems, or uncertainty before routine stimulant initiation. Pregnancy requires individualized benefit-risk assessment, not automatic continuation or cessation.

### Choose ADHD treatment with monitoring built in

Before medication, assess personal/family cardiac history, exertional syncope/chest symptoms, examination, pulse/BP, weight, and pediatric growth; investigate concerning findings. NICE does not require routine ECG before stimulants, atomoxetine, or guanfacine without clinical/concurrent-drug cardiac risks. Check product-specific ages and formulations; short-/long-acting products are not casually interchangeable. [NICE medication assessment and monitoring](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)

| Treatment group | Practical role | Important monitoring or caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Methylphenidate or amphetamine products | Often effective for core ADHD symptoms | Appetite, sleep, growth, pulse/BP, mood, misuse/diversion |
| Atomoxetine | Nonstimulant option for selected patients | Youth suicidal ideation warning, BP/pulse, hepatic symptoms, interactions |
| Extended-release guanfacine or clonidine | Selected pediatric monotherapy or adjunct use | Sedation, hypotension/bradycardia; avoid abrupt withdrawal |
| Behavioral and environmental supports | Address daily function and treatment implementation | Define goals across home, school, or work |

Titrate according to meaningful improvement and tolerability, with planned contacts during changes. Review appetite, weight/growth, sleep, pulse/BP, mood, tics, and daily function rather than adjusting solely to a rating score. New psychosis, mania, significant cardiac symptoms, or severe adverse effects requires prompt reassessment. Atomoxetine has a boxed warning about suicidal ideation in children/adolescents; strong CYP2D6 inhibitors can affect exposure. Extended-release guanfacine can cause hypotension and sedation, and abrupt discontinuation can produce rebound hypertension, so use its product-specific taper. [Current atomoxetine safety scope](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=14f2804d-2914-4dd9-86c0-d084fcfd7e50), [guanfacine label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=1c492e87-3406-ddb8-aaf2-e609c2ded503)

In NICE's monitoring schedule, measure pediatric height every six months; weight every three months through age 10, and at three and six months after initiation then every six months in older children. Check adult weight every six months. Measure pulse/BP before and after each dose change and every six months. Investigate sustained abnormalities or concerning symptoms sooner; routine ECG or laboratory screening is unnecessary without a clinical indication. New tics require assessment of natural fluctuation, impairment, and medication timing, not an automatic assumption that every stimulant must be stopped. [NICE ADHD follow-up schedule](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)

Discuss secure storage, nonsharing, refills, and diversion without presuming addiction. FDA stimulant warnings cover misuse, addiction, and overdose, including nonprescribed routes/doses. Stimulants are not peer study aids or weight-control drugs. Reassess changing demands/supports and plan transition from pediatric care. [FDA prescription-stimulant safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-updating-warnings-improve-safe-use-prescription-stimulants-used-treat-adhd-and-other-conditions)

**Clinical Case.** A 10-year-old loses assignments and interrupts at school and home, with longstanding symptoms and disproportionate reading difficulty. Assess ADHD and a possible reading disorder. Coordinate school evaluation, classroom/family support, and medication discussion. Track function, relationships, growth, sleep, and adverse effects; attention treatment does not replace reading instruction.

### Maintain continuity across psychiatric and medical care

Document diagnosis/uncertainty, functional goals, treatment ownership, monitoring, and reassessment. Coordinate metabolic, nutritional, sleep, reproductive, and school care. Address access, privacy, burden, and treatment beliefs; use outreach and warm handoffs when symptoms impede attendance.

Give condition-specific escalation instructions for confusion/psychosis, unsafe self-care, severe restriction/fainting, dangerous sleepiness, or major medication reactions. Reassess new physical disease despite psychiatric diagnoses and preserve behavioral care alongside medical treatment.
