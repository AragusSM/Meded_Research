# Medivale Family Medicine

## 12C. Substance Use

Substance-related illness can present as disclosed use, abnormal screening, insomnia, falls, pain, anxiety, or emergency. Establish immediate safety and accessible care. Withdrawal, addiction treatment, overdose prevention, and comorbidity care are distinct tasks: withdrawal completion does not establish recovery, and continued use does not preclude treatment.

CDC cessation, ASAM withdrawal, and SAMHSA opioid-treatment pathways support adult care. Adolescents, pregnancy, severe intoxication/withdrawal, and complex organ disease require additional safeguards. See 12D for chronic pain and safeguarding.

### Ask about use, then establish the clinical problem

Ask neutrally about alcohol, tobacco/vaping, cannabis, nonprescribed pills, injected/inhaled drugs, and prescription misuse. Establish amount, frequency, route/source, last use, combinations, reasons, change attempts, and goals. Assess pain, sleep, trauma, withdrawal, pregnancy, driving, caregiving, housing/food, overdose, self-harm, and violence without presuming every concern applies.

Screening tools such as AUDIT-C or a validated alcohol question, and TAPS or ASSIST for other substances, identify the need for further assessment. The USPSTF adult drug-screening recommendation concerns **asking questions**, not routinely testing biological specimens, and assumes access to diagnostic and treatment services; it does not endorse punitive screening. Its adolescent insufficient-evidence statement does not mean that concerning symptoms or disclosed use should be ignored. Explain confidentiality and its limits before asking sensitive questions. [USPSTF drug-screening framework](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/drug-use-illicit-screening)

A substance use disorder is diagnosed from a problematic pattern with clinically significant impairment or distress, not from one positive urine test. Assess loss of control, unsuccessful efforts to reduce use, time spent obtaining/using/recovering, craving, role and relationship consequences, abandoned activities, hazardous use, continued use despite harm, tolerance, and withdrawal. For the usual DSM framework, at least two applicable criteria within a 12-month period support a diagnosis; two to three indicate mild, four to five moderate, and six or more severe illness. The pattern can be diagnosed before a full year of continued use has elapsed. With prescribed opioids taken solely under appropriate medical supervision, tolerance and withdrawal alone do not count as OUD criteria. [CDC OUD diagnostic criteria](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/hcp/clinical-care/opioid-use-disorder-diagnosis.html)

Distinguish appropriate-treatment dependence from impaired control/harmful use. Drug effects, withdrawal, medical illness, and independent psychiatric disease may coexist; establish their timeline. Order laboratories for clinical questions. Immunoassays may miss fentanyl/other agents or cross-react; confirm consequential unexpected results. Neither negative testing nor reported exposure excludes other causes of altered consciousness.

### Make tobacco treatment a practical plan

Assess tobacco/nicotine products, daily cigarettes, time to first cigarette, quit attempts, household exposure, and triggers. Nicotinic-receptor activation reinforces use; withdrawal causes craving, irritability, restlessness, poor concentration, sleep change, and appetite increase. Combustion adds cardiovascular, pulmonary, and cancer risks. Explain relevant cessation benefits without promising reversal of all injury.

For nonpregnant adults who smoke, combine counseling with suitable medication. Plan triggers, coping, support, access, technique, and follow-up around the quit attempt and setbacks. Brief care, quitlines, individual/group counseling, and digital support can complement each other; use a lapse to adjust treatment. [CDC clinical tobacco treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/hcp/patient-care-settings/clinical.html)

The **5 A's** organize a brief intervention: **Ask** about tobacco, **Advise** cessation clearly and personally, **Assess** willingness to try, **Assist** with treatment and barriers, and **Arrange** follow-up. Ambivalence calls for continued engagement, not discharge from care. [AHRQ tobacco-intervention framework](https://www.ahrq.gov/prevention/guidelines/tobacco/5steps.html)

| Option | How it helps | Important selection or counseling point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nicotine patch plus gum or lozenge | Baseline replacement with additional craving relief | Teach technique and adequate use; review total nicotine exposure |
| Varenicline | Partial nicotinic agonism reduces craving and cigarette reward | Gradual titration; renal function, nausea, sleep and behavioral effects |
| Bupropion SR | Nonnicotine medicine reduces withdrawal and craving | Seizure, eating-disorder, withdrawal and interaction safeguards |
| Counseling and follow-up | Builds coping and supports repeated attempts | Tailor support to the person's triggers, access and preferences |

Combination replacement usually pairs a daily patch with short-acting nicotine, not every possible drug combination. Review pregnancy, unstable cardiovascular illness, skin/oral problems, medicines, and instructions. Dispose of patches safely and protect children from nicotine. Reassess nausea, palpitations, or dizziness for nicotine exposure and other causes. [CDC combination therapy](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/campaign/tips/quit-smoking/quit-smoking-medications/how-to-use-quit-smoking-medicines/how-to-combine-medicines.html)

For an adult smoking more than 10 cigarettes daily, CDC suggests considering a **21-mg/24-hour patch** initially; lighter/nondaily use needs individualized dosing. Apply a fresh patch daily to a different clean, dry site and remove the old one. If vivid dreams disrupt sleep, remove it at bedtime and apply a new one the next morning. A smoking lapse is not by itself a reason to abandon replacement; reassess symptoms and resume the quit plan. [CDC patch instructions](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/campaign/tips/quit-smoking/quit-smoking-medications/how-to-use-quit-smoking-medicines/how-to-use-a-nicotine-patch.html)

Gum works through buccal absorption. Chew slowly until tingling, then park it against the cheek and repeat; continuous rapid chewing increases swallowed nicotine and gastrointestinal symptoms. For an adult daily cigarette smoker, the usual gum strength is 4 mg when the first cigarette is within 30 minutes of waking and 2 mg when it is later, with individualized advice for lighter or nondaily use. The usual gum maximum is 24 pieces daily; do not transfer that maximum to lozenges. Avoid eating or drinking for 15 minutes before and during gum use. Combined patch/gum frequency may differ from gum-only treatment. [CDC nicotine-gum instructions](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/campaign/tips/quit-smoking/quit-smoking-medications/how-to-use-quit-smoking-medicines/how-to-use-nicotine-gum.html)

For a typical adult without a need for adjustment, CDC describes varenicline titration as 0.5 mg daily on days 1-3, 0.5 mg twice daily on days 4-7, then 1 mg twice daily from day 8. Treatment commonly lasts 12 weeks and may be extended. Take with food and water to reduce nausea. Severe renal impairment requires a different regimen; tolerability can also require adjustment. Discuss vivid dreams, sleep impairment, driving precautions, and new behavioral or mood changes. A psychiatric history calls for assessment and monitoring, not an automatic assumption that the medicine is forbidden. [CDC varenicline instructions](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/campaign/tips/quit-smoking/quit-smoking-medications/how-to-use-quit-smoking-medicines/how-to-use-varenicline.html)

With estimated creatinine clearance **below 30 mL/min**, the varenicline label starts at 0.5 mg once daily and permits titration only to 0.5 mg twice daily. During hemodialysis, the maximum is 0.5 mg once daily if tolerated. These are not the usual 1-mg-twice-daily target. Stop and seek prompt assessment for a seizure, sleepwalking, serious skin reaction, or new severe behavioral symptoms. [Varenicline renal and safety instructions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=c325d582-8f43-40ea-b348-87837de97a07)

Bupropion SR differs from other formulations. Assess current/prior anorexia/bulimia, seizures, interacting medicines, abrupt alcohol/sedative withdrawal, BP, sleep, polarity, and duplicate products. Pregnancy starts with behavioral support and individualized medication discussion; youth need age-specific care. E-cigarettes are not FDA-approved cessation drugs. Complete combustible substitution differs from dual use, but vaping is not harmless or appropriate in pregnancy/youth. [CDC vaping and quitting](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/e-cigarettes/quitting.html)

For an appropriate adult without organ-function adjustment, the cessation SR label starts **150 mg orally daily for three days, then 150 mg twice daily**, at least eight hours apart, beginning one week before the quit date; do not exceed 300 mg/day. Swallow tablets whole. Renal or mild hepatic impairment may require reduced dose/frequency; with Child-Pugh 7-15, do not exceed 150 mg every other day. Do not borrow a depression XL schedule or add another bupropion product. [Bupropion SR cessation label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a591e33a-52f0-41c8-a00b-4a1afcc3dc4c)

**Clinical Case.** A 46-year-old resumes smoking after a week on a patch, removing it during cravings and rapidly chewing several gum pieces together. Correct replacement/gum technique, address coworker-break triggers, and arrange follow-up before labeling medication failure.

### Assess alcohol exposure without equating a drink count with a diagnosis

Clarify actual container size and alcohol concentration. One U.S. standard drink contains about 14 g of pure alcohol, approximately 12 fluid ounces of 5% beer, 5 ounces of 12% wine, or 1.5 ounces of 40% spirits. A large high-strength beer or mixed drink may contain several equivalents. Ask about heavy occasions, blackouts, morning drinking, injuries, unsuccessful attempts to reduce use, and symptoms when intake falls. Normal aminotransferases do not exclude AUD or alcohol-related harm. [NIAAA standard-drink reference](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/alcohols-effects-health/what-standard-drink)

AUD depends on symptoms/consequences, not cirrhosis, homelessness, or daily intoxication. Brief counseling can help unhealthy use; established AUD often needs ongoing behavioral/medication care. Discuss reduction versus abstinence and no-alcohol contexts, including pregnancy, driving, and interactions. Assess withdrawal risk before advising abrupt cessation. [NIAAA AUD framework](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/brochures-and-fact-sheets/understanding-alcohol-use-disorder)

Review medical effects such as hypertension, arrhythmia, pancreatitis, liver disease, neuropathy, malnutrition, sleep disruption, and cancer risk, together with mental health, injury, and family consequences. Select CBC, chemistry, liver measures, glucose, magnesium/phosphorus, coagulation, or other studies according to illness and treatment needs. Treat complications and nutrition alongside the use disorder. A high blood alcohol concentration does not exclude head injury, infection, hypoglycemia, or withdrawal beginning as the concentration falls.

### Recognize dangerous alcohol withdrawal and choose the setting

Chronic exposure alters inhibitory/excitatory signaling; reduced intake can cause tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, insomnia, tachycardia, hypertension, seizures, or delirium. Symptoms often start within 6-24 hours but vary with exposure/sedatives. Seizures may precede warning symptoms; delirium often emerges over subsequent days. Preserved attention during hallucinations differs from delirium, but both need assessment.

Ask about previous withdrawal seizures or delirium, repeated withdrawals, recent consumption, other sedative dependence, severe medical or psychiatric illness, pregnancy, and reliable support. Current confusion, seizure, unstable vital signs, significant dehydration, severe vomiting, or inability to take treatment requires a monitored acute-care setting. Withdrawal may occur despite a positive alcohol level. A symptom score helps track a suitable communicative patient but does not by itself establish the diagnosis, predict all future risk, or make an unsafe home situation safe. [ASAM alcohol-withdrawal framework](https://www.asam.org/quality-care/clinical-guidelines/alcohol-withdrawal-management-guideline)

| Assessment | Ambulatory care may be feasible | Escalation concern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current symptoms and prior course | Limited symptoms without major complicating risk | Seizure, delirium, severe symptoms or prior complicated withdrawal |
| Medical and psychiatric state | Stable comorbidity and ability to take treatment | Acute illness, unsafe mental state, pregnancy-related complexity |
| Home and follow-up | Reliable support, contact and rapid reassessment | Unstable setting, absent monitoring or inability to obtain care |
| Course after treatment starts | Symptoms improve without oversedation | Worsening symptoms, persistent vomiting or respiratory depression |

Benzodiazepines are first-line treatment for clinically significant alcohol withdrawal and prevention of its severe complications in appropriate patients. Choose the regimen and setting according to severity and monitoring; symptom-triggered treatment requires reliable assessment, and fixed regimens still need reassessment. Significant liver disease favors a medicine with less dependence on hepatic oxidative metabolism, such as lorazepam or oxazepam. Phenobarbital requires clinicians experienced with its use and an appropriate monitoring environment. Selected low-risk cases may use other agents, but these are not substitutes for monitored treatment of complicated withdrawal.

Give thiamine and address fluid, electrolyte, and nutritional deficits. Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy requires urgent parenteral treatment; the full confusion/ataxia/ocular triad need not be present. Do not delay treatment of hypoglycemia while waiting to give thiamine; ASAM allows glucose and thiamine in either order or concurrently. Clonidine or beta-blockers may reduce autonomic signs but do not prevent withdrawal seizures or delirium and must not be the sole treatment for those risks. CIWA-Ar is not appropriate for tracking delirious patients because several items require meaningful self-report. [ASAM selected withdrawal recommendations](https://downloads.asam.org/sitefinity-production-blobs/docs/default-source/quality-science/the_asam_clinical_practice_guideline_on_alcohol-1.pdf)

For selected ambulatory withdrawal, arrange qualified daily check-ins for up to five days after cessation/reduction and clear escalation access. Review vitals, hydration, orientation, alcohol/other use, symptoms, medicines, and sedation. Connect ongoing AUD care before withdrawal treatment ends; an unmonitored sedative supply is inadequate.

**Clinical Case.** A 58-year-old seeking cessation has mild tremor but prior withdrawal seizure, persistent vomiting, and no overnight monitoring because his partner works. Arrange supervised acute care despite a modest score, and begin planning ongoing AUD treatment.

### Offer ongoing AUD treatment matched to the patient

Offer motivational work, CBT-based coping, other structured treatments, and optional mutual support. Primary care can provide medication with assessment/follow-up without requiring prior failures. Define improvement through drinking, function, and medical risk; a return to use calls for reassessment, not discharge. [NIAAA treatment options](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/health-professionals-communities/core-resource-on-alcohol/recommend-evidence-based-treatment-know-options)

| Medicine | Useful role | Major selection issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Naltrexone | Reduces alcohol reward and heavy drinking; oral or long-acting injectable options | Current opioids/dependence, anticipated analgesia and hepatic assessment |
| Acamprosate | Supports maintenance of abstinence after stopping alcohol | Renal clearance, frequent dosing and current renal function |
| Disulfiram | Supports an informed abstinence plan through an alcohol-aversive reaction | Adherence, hidden alcohol, liver/cardiac and psychiatric risks |
| Selected off-label options | May fit particular comorbidity or treatment histories | Agent-specific evidence, contraindications and specialist support |

Naltrexone blocks opioid receptors. Review opioid exposure, including prescribed analgesics and medications for OUD, before either AUD formulation; an antagonist can precipitate severe withdrawal. Evaluate liver disease and signs of acute hepatic injury, and plan for possible future pain treatment. Stable liver disease does not automatically exclude treatment, but active injury and product warnings still require careful review. Oral and injectable initiation instructions are not identical: the injectable AUD label requires ability to abstain before initiation and no active drinking at the first administration. Neither formulation treats acute alcohol withdrawal. [Current extended-release naltrexone label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cd11c435-b0f0-4bb9-ae78-60f101f3703f)

Acamprosate is started for maintenance of abstinence and does not manage acute withdrawal. For adults, the usual labeled regimen is 666 mg orally three times daily; creatinine clearance above 30 through 50 mL/min requires 333 mg three times daily, while clearance at or below 30 mL/min is a contraindication. Assess adherence feasibility, diarrhea, mood/safety, and renal changes. Its renal elimination can make it useful when hepatic concerns affect other choices, but impaired kidneys may remove that advantage. [Acamprosate label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=91769a96-3182-4e38-90c3-b22c1beae398)

Disulfiram inhibits aldehyde metabolism, causing potentially dangerous alcohol reactions without treating withdrawal or reliably eliminating craving. Use informed consent, contraindication/liver review, and counseling about hidden alcohol; never covert or punitive administration. Selected topiramate/gabapentin AUD use is off-label and requires cognitive, renal, reproductive, sedation, and misuse review. Track drinking, function, safety, adherence, and goals, not liver enzymes alone.

**Clinical Case.** A 63-year-old seeks abstinence medication after supervised withdrawal but takes opioid analgesia pending cancer treatment and has creatinine clearance 42 mL/min. Naltrexone may block analgesia or precipitate withdrawal. Consider renal-adjusted acamprosate if assessment and dosing feasibility support it.

### Treat OUD with medication and continuing support

Assess opioid exposure, prior treatment/overdose, withdrawal, sedatives, psychiatric symptoms, pain, infection, and pregnancy. Offer indicated medication promptly. Buprenorphine/methadone reduce withdrawal/craving and are associated with lower treatment-period overdose mortality. Naltrexone requires a safe opioid-free transition. Withdrawal-only care lowers tolerance and can increase danger on resumed use. [SAMHSA OUD treatment resource](https://www.samhsa.gov/resource/ebp/tip-63-medications-opioid-use-disorder)

| Medication | Main pharmacology | Practical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buprenorphine | High-affinity partial opioid agonist | Office-based access; initiation must address precipitated withdrawal risk |
| Methadone | Full opioid agonist with long, variable elimination | OUD maintenance generally through an OTP; accumulation and interactions matter |
| Extended-release naltrexone | Opioid antagonist | Requires opioid-free transition; blocks opioid analgesia and has post-treatment overdose risks |

OUD medication may continue for years without a universal stopping deadline. Discuss requested taper risks, recurrence, and overdose with continuing follow-up. Alpha-2 agonist withdrawal care requires BP monitoring and does not replace ongoing OUD treatment. Return to use warrants reviewing dose, access, exposure, and support.

Conventional initiation requires objective withdrawal after full agonists, not a clock alone: high-affinity buprenorphine can displace opioids and precipitate withdrawal. Fentanyl makes timing less predictable. Interpret COWS with history/examination. Expert low-dose initiation with opioid continuation and other selected strategies require distinct protocols. ASAM's high-potency-opioid clinical considerations are consensus guidance, not a complete evidence-graded guideline. [ASAM individualized buprenorphine initiation](https://downloads.asam.org/sitefinity-production-blobs/docs/default-source/advocacy/letters-and-comments/asam_clinical_considerations__buprenorphine.212-%281%29.pdf?sfvrsn=1d12567a_1)

As a **product-specific conventional example for short-acting opioid dependence with objective moderate withdrawal**, the SUBOXONE film label begins 2 mg/0.5 mg or 4 mg/1 mg sublingually, reassessing under supervision and titrating in 2- or 4-mg buprenorphine increments about two hours apart, up to 8 mg/2 mg on day 1 and 16 mg/4 mg on day 2. These paired numbers are buprenorphine/naloxone, not interchangeable totals. This is not a fentanyl, methadone-transition, low-dose, or injectable-product protocol; complex exposure needs an individualized plan. [SUBOXONE film induction instructions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8a5edcf9-828c-4f97-b671-268ab13a8ecd)

Buprenorphine can still cause respiratory depression, especially with other depressants; a ceiling effect does not make overdose impossible. Naloxone in combination products helps deter some parenteral misuse but does not make the product an overdose rescue medicine. Sublingual/buccal and long-acting injectable products have different instructions and cannot be assumed equivalent milligram for milligram. Review oral health for transmucosal products and drug interactions, hepatic status, safe storage, and the patient's ability to obtain medication. [SAMHSA buprenorphine overview](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment/options/buprenorphine) [Current buprenorphine/naloxone label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8a5edcf9-828c-4f97-b671-268ab13a8ecd)

Methadone can be particularly useful according to prior response, tolerance, preference, and treatment access. Its long, variable half-life means respiratory depression can accumulate during initiation or after changes; sedation, QT risk, and interactions need review. OUD maintenance generally occurs through a federally certified opioid treatment program, with separate hospital/emergency exceptions. Current federal rules allow individualized take-home decisions; daily attendance is not an immutable requirement for every patient. Pain prescribing rules are not a route around OUD regulations. Confirm doses and missed doses with the program before continuing treatment in another setting. [SAMHSA current OTP rule implementation](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment/opioid-treatment-program/42-cfr-part-8/faqs)

Naltrexone initiation requires an adequate opioid-free interval and clinical assessment. The extended-release label recommends at least 7-10 days after short-acting opioids; vulnerability to precipitated withdrawal can last up to two weeks after buprenorphine or methadone. A negative routine urine test does not guarantee a safe transition. Discuss loss of tolerance, overdose risk near the end of a dosing interval, after missed/discontinued treatment, or from attempts to overcome blockade. An antagonist is not automatically preferable because it is not a controlled substance. Provide access to overdose reversal and a plan for acute pain.

Buprenorphine OUD prescribing no longer requires the federal X-waiver or its patient limits, but DEA registration, scope, state rules, and applicable training remain necessary. Counseling access should not block medication. Temporary federal telemedicine flexibilities extend through December 31, 2026; confirm applicable federal/state requirements when providing remote care. [SAMHSA waiver elimination](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment/resources/mat-act), [current federal telemedicine rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-31/html/2025-24123.htm)

Concurrent benzodiazepine or alcohol use increases overdose risk but should not automatically exclude buprenorphine or methadone treatment. Assess current intoxication/sedation, coordinate prescribers, reduce avoidable depressants safely, and choose a suitable level of monitoring. Pregnancy generally favors coordinated treatment with methadone or buprenorphine rather than withdrawal-only care; neonatal withdrawal risk does not make untreated OUD safer. Product choice, including combination versus single-ingredient buprenorphine, should follow current perinatal expertise, not a blanket assumption that every combination is prohibited. Continue pain, psychiatric, infectious, and reproductive care alongside OUD treatment. [SAMHSA perinatal whole-person advisory](https://library.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/pep23-02-01-002.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 34-year-old with repeat counterfeit-oxycodone overdose, likely fentanyl exposure, and prior precipitated withdrawal requests care. She is alert with little objective withdrawal. Avoid a fixed eight-hour rule; provide overdose prevention and arrange expert individualized initiation. Therapy waiting lists should not block medication.

### Respond to overdose and reduce the next risk

Suspect overdose with unresponsiveness, slow/absent breathing, or abnormal snoring/gurgling; pinpoint pupils are not required. Activate emergency services, give naloxone, and support breathing/resuscitation per training and dispatch instructions, including CPR/AED when indicated. Do not wait for medication when breathing is abnormal; continue assessment because naloxone does not reverse every cause.

For a common 4-mg naloxone nasal-spray product, deliver one full device into one nostril. Do not prime or test it. If breathing does not improve adequately, or respiratory depression returns, give another dose with a new device every 2-3 minutes while awaiting emergency help, alternating nostrils according to instructions. Device strengths and routes differ, so follow the actual product. Emergency care is still necessary after apparent recovery because naloxone can wear off before the opioid. Withdrawal after reversal may be distressing, but inadequate breathing is the immediate priority. [Naloxone nasal-spray instructions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a0ebc83d-2892-40de-bc0a-775ce90b30db)

Other sedatives, including xylazine in some illicit supplies, may cause persistent impairment after the opioid effect is reversed. Give naloxone when opioid exposure is possible, but continue airway and breathing support and obtain emergency care; naloxone does not reverse the nonopioid sedative itself. Do not assume lack of full awakening proves that no opioid was involved. Mixed exposures also make home observation after a brief response unsafe. [CDC xylazine clinical guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/media/pdfs/2024/07/Xylazine-Clinical-Management-and-Harm-Reduction.pdf)

Provide naloxone and training to at-risk patients and potential witnesses. Discuss lost tolerance after abstinence, hospital/incarceration, or withdrawal-only care; counterfeit/fentanyl supplies; and sedative combinations. Avoid solitary use and arrange a responder. Fentanyl strips cannot establish safety, detect every analog, or quantify the whole supply. Harm reduction and treatment are compatible. [CDC fentanyl information](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/fentanyl.html)

Assess HIV, hepatitis B/C, sexually transmitted infections, vaccination needs, wounds, and access to sterile equipment according to exposures. Syringe services can connect people with testing, vaccination, wound care, naloxone, and treatment. Avoid sharing preparation equipment as well as syringes. Fever, spreading soft-tissue infection, severe pain, new back pain, or embolic findings requires assessment for serious infection rather than attribution to uncomplicated withdrawal. Coordinate indicated HIV prevention and hepatitis treatment without requiring complete abstinence first. [CDC infection-prevention care](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcp/populations-settings/pwid.html)

**Clinical Case.** A 29-year-old survives roommate-administered naloxone and plans his prehospital opioid amount. Explain reduced tolerance and uncertain contents. Provide multiple rescue doses, train the roommate, assess sedatives/infection, and connect OUD treatment; prior survival does not predict safe re-exposure.

### Address stimulants and other substances without losing medical urgency

Stimulant intoxication may produce agitation, mydriasis, sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, chest pain, seizures, or psychosis. Evaluate for ischemia, arrhythmia, stroke, rhabdomyolysis, dehydration, and mixed exposure. Severe symptoms belong in emergency care. Young age and a positive stimulant test do not exclude acute coronary syndrome. Treat hyperadrenergic toxicity in an appropriate monitored setting; benzodiazepines are a common initial option when not contraindicated. Cooling, fluids, cardiac evaluation, and other treatment depend on the complication. Treat oxygenation, ischemia and blood pressure according to the actual findings; stimulant exposure does not by itself require oxygen or justify one drug rule for every cardiovascular presentation. [ASAM/AAAP stimulant framework](https://downloads.asam.org/sitefinity-production-blobs/docs/default-source/quality-science/stud_guideline_document_final.pdf)

Stimulant withdrawal often brings fatigue, sleep changes, increased appetite, dysphoria, and craving. A crash can include substantial suicide risk even without the autonomic pattern of alcohol withdrawal. Assess psychosis, mood, safety, nutrition, and other substances; persistence beyond expected intoxication requires reassessment. For ongoing stimulant use disorder, contingency management is a central evidence-based treatment, often paired with CBT or community reinforcement. Selected off-label pharmacotherapy may be considered by appropriately experienced clinicians, including naltrexone/bupropion in some amphetamine-type stimulant cases. This is not a general prescription for every stimulant exposure, and current opioid use/dependence, seizure risk, and eating-disorder history can make that combination inappropriate.

Cannabis can impair attention, memory, coordination, and driving, and can provoke anxiety or psychotic symptoms in susceptible individuals. Ask about potency, frequency, concentrates, edibles, and synthetic products. Delayed edible effects can lead to repeated ingestion before the first dose is felt. Recurrent vomiting in a frequent user may represent cannabinoid hyperemesis, but assess dehydration and alternative causes; a hot-shower history is not a mandatory diagnostic test. Sustained cessation is important to prevent recurrence. Withdrawal can include irritability, sleep difficulty, anxiety, low appetite, and craving. Behavioral approaches are the main CUD treatment; cannabis-based products approved for other indications are not thereby approved to treat CUD. [NIDA cannabis resource](https://nida.nih.gov/research-topics/cannabis)

Assess suspected hallucinogen, dissociative, inhalant, performance-enhancing, or supplement exposure with safety, vitals, indicated glucose, and evaluation for trauma, temperature abnormalities, seizures, arrhythmia, or respiratory compromise. Supportive surroundings do not replace toxicity assessment. Product names/screens may miss contents; obtain toxicology expertise for uncertain exposures.

### Distinguish sedative dependence from a use disorder and taper safely

Benzodiazepines and other sedatives can cause sedation, falls, impaired cognition, and respiratory depression, particularly with opioids or alcohol. Regular prescribed use may produce physical dependence without the behavioral features of a use disorder. Abrupt discontinuation or an excessively rapid reduction can cause severe anxiety, insomnia, autonomic symptoms, seizures, or delirium. Do not abruptly stop a dependent patient's supply as a response to an unexpected test, a practice change, or concern about long-term risks. Assess immediate danger and arrange continuity or a higher level of care when needed. [FDA benzodiazepine safety](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-requiring-boxed-warning-updated-improve-safe-use-benzodiazepine-drug-class)

The 2025 joint benzodiazepine-taper guideline generally starts with individualized reductions of about 5%-10% every 2-4 weeks, often slower with long exposure or significant symptoms. It generally advises not exceeding 25% every two weeks. These are planning ranges, not a compulsory schedule or a dose-conversion formula. Reassess after each change and slow or pause when indicated. Consider formulation, half-life, liver disease, age, interactions, and prior withdrawal; switching to a longer-acting agent is not always suitable. Treat the original anxiety/insomnia problem and offer behavioral support. Severe withdrawal, seizure risk, or unstable illness may require inpatient management. [Joint tapering guideline](https://downloads.asam.org/sitefinity-production-blobs/docs/default-source/guidelines/benzodiazepine-tapering-2025/bzd-tapering-document---final-approved-version-for-distribution-02-28-25.pdf)

Flumazenil is not routine empiric treatment for an undifferentiated sedative overdose in a dependent patient or mixed ingestion because it can precipitate seizures. Support ventilation and obtain emergency/toxicology guidance. As with alcohol, withdrawal prevention and ongoing treatment are separate tasks. Coordinate a sedative plan with OUD treatment rather than insisting that one disorder disappear before the other receives care. [Flumazenil safety label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=53d9144a-b7ee-b52a-e063-6294a90a09c5)

**Clinical Case.** A 70-year-old on nightly benzodiazepines for years wants immediate cessation after two falls and now also takes fracture-related opioids. Assess sedation/breathing and each indication; plan individualized gradual taper and alternative insomnia care. Falls strengthen reassessment, not abrupt discontinuation.

### Protect confidentiality and make referral a completed connection

Explain who can see the record, how billing or portals may expose information, and the limits of confidentiality before testing or involving others. Private time is especially important for adolescents, alongside developmentally appropriate family support and jurisdiction-specific consent rules. Do not promise absolute secrecy or automatically disclose an adult's results to a relative who requested testing. Assess immediate danger, capacity, and applicable reporting duties separately from whether the patient uses a substance.

Federal **42 CFR Part 2** adds protections for qualifying SUD program records; it does not automatically apply to every mention of alcohol or drug use in every primary-care note. HIPAA, state laws, and other rules may also apply. Under the current final rule, compliance was required by February 16, 2026. A single consent may authorize future treatment, payment, and health-care-operations uses/disclosures, while separately maintained SUD counseling notes require specific consent and legal-proceeding disclosures have additional safeguards. Do not infer that a general consent authorizes unrestricted law-enforcement access. Consult the organization's privacy expertise for the actual record, recipient, purpose, and jurisdiction. [HHS current Part 2 explanation](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/part-2/index.html), [HHS final-rule fact sheet](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/regulatory-initiatives/fact-sheet-42-cfr-part-2-final-rule/index.html)

Specify referral destination/urgency, appropriate medication bridge, transport, cost, attendance confirmation, and missed-visit follow-up. Coordinate with consent. Peer, social, integrated behavioral, and medication support can proceed together despite unresolved social problems; medication alone cannot resolve housing or violence.

**Clinical Case.** A 22-year-old seeks stimulant-use treatment but fears parental insurance access will expose it. Clarify confidentiality, billing/portal risks, safety, and treatment access before disclosure. Arrange direct care and private follow-up under the applicable service/record rules; parental concern alone does not authorize disclosure.

Review exposure, overdose/withdrawal risk, treatment response/adverse effects, mood/safety, complications, function, and goals. Escalate respiratory impairment, severe withdrawal, confusion, hyperthermia, chest pain, seizures, or unsafe behavior. Continue care after setbacks, not only after abstinence.
