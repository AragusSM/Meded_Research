# Medivale Family Medicine

## 12D. Chronic Pain and Safeguarding

Assess pain mechanism, persistence, and functional loss alongside possible barriers to safe care. Pain does not establish abuse, and trauma history does not diminish physical symptoms. Give a credible explanation, practical treatment, and individualized safety assessment.

CDC 2022 guides US adult outpatient opioid prescribing within defined exclusions. USPSTF routine screening differs from suspected-abuse evaluation; reporting duties vary by jurisdiction. See 07A-07D for musculoskeletal disease, 12A-12B for psychiatric care, and 12C for addiction treatment.

### Establish the pain mechanism and its consequences

Pain continuing beyond about three months is generally chronic, but duration alone does not identify its cause. Ask about onset, location, radiation, character, variability, triggers, associated symptoms, prior investigations, and response to each treatment. Distinguish an unchanged longstanding syndrome from a new superimposed illness. A person with chronic back pain can still develop a fracture, infection, cancer, or spinal cord compression. New progressive weakness, sphincter dysfunction, fever with concerning examination, substantial trauma, or a suspected vascular emergency changes the urgency of evaluation.

Pain involves sensory processing, emotion, attention, sleep, and experience; it does not directly measure tissue damage. Unrevealing imaging does not establish malingering. Mechanisms overlap, burning alone does not prove neuropathy, and allodynia (pain from normally nonpainful stimuli) is a finding, not an etiologic diagnosis. [IASP pain terminology](https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/terminology/)

| Predominant mechanism | Clinical reasoning | Treatment implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nociceptive | Nociceptor activation from actual or threatened nonneural tissue injury, such as joint inflammation | Address the tissue disorder, loading, rehabilitation, and appropriate analgesia |
| Neuropathic | A somatosensory lesion or disease explains a plausible distribution, such as postherpetic neuralgia | Investigate the cause and consider a suitable neuropathic-pain treatment |
| Nociplastic | Altered pain processing without a clear tissue injury or somatosensory lesion sufficient to explain the pain | Explain the mechanism and combine function-focused, sleep, behavioral, and selected drug strategies |
| Mixed | More than one mechanism contributes | Treat identifiable components without assuming one medicine addresses everything |

Record effects on walking, dressing, caregiving, concentration, sleep, intimacy, and work. Agree on specific goals such as meal preparation or a modified shift. Track pain and function together; reduced pain with dangerous sedation is not success. A functional scale cannot independently establish safety, truthfulness, or treatment eligibility.

Examine relevant joints/spine, strength, reflexes, sensation, gait, perfusion, skin, and systemic findings. Assess mood, trauma, substances, adverse effects, and sleep without dismissing disease. Target tests to clinical questions: unchanged symptoms seldom benefit from repeated imaging, but new deficits require reassessment despite prior reassuring scans. Negative panels are not prerequisites for credible symptoms.

Use interpreters, accessible responses, observed behavior, and collateral history for communication/cognitive impairment. Caregiver accounts supplement the patient's report; visible distress is not a calibrated pain measure. Ask about medicine control, withholding/theft, and blocked appointments as concrete safety concerns.

**Clinical Case.** A 44-year-old has nine months of widespread aching, poor sleep, fatigue, and concentration difficulty, with diffuse tenderness but no synovitis, focal weakness, or sensory level. After targeted alternative assessment, explain possible nociplastic pain. Focus on reliable work attendance and walking with her sister, while reassessing new findings rather than repeating imaging or escalating opioids.

### Build a multimodal plan the patient can carry out

Explain the diagnosis, uncertainty, and reassessment triggers. Treat identifiable disease and combine complementary treatments rather than accumulating marginally helpful medicines. Agree on benefit/adverse-effect measures, review timing, and withdrawal of ineffective therapies.

Use physical/occupational therapy for strength, mobility, gait, ergonomics, pacing, and adaptations. Match activity to disease, cardiovascular status, ability, and priorities, with tolerable starts and review. Avoid fixed progression despite fatigue or postexertional worsening; distinguish soreness, flare, and new injury. [CDC nonopioid clinical options](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/hcp/clinical-care/nonopioid-therapies-for-pain-management.html)

Behavioral care addresses pain responses, fear, distress, and disrupted activity without implying fabrication. CBT may target avoidance, goals, relaxation, and flare plans. Treat insomnia/depression and assess apnea with snoring, witnessed pauses, obesity, somnolence, or respiratory depressants. Selected passive relief should not displace rehabilitation or disease treatment.

Fibromyalgia combines widespread pain with symptoms such as fatigue, impaired sleep, and cognitive difficulty. There is no single confirmatory imaging or laboratory test. Assess relevant alternatives and coexisting disorders rather than declaring that every future symptom belongs to fibromyalgia. Education, individually paced activity, sleep care, behavioral approaches, and selected medications form a combined plan. Anti-inflammatory drugs do not specifically correct the altered pain processing, although a coexisting arthritic condition may independently justify one. [NIAMS fibromyalgia assessment and care](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/fibromyalgia/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

### Choose nonopioid medicines by indication and risk

Reconcile prescriptions, OTC cold/sleep/combination products, supplements, alcohol, and cannabis. Assess organ function, pregnancy, bleeding/cardiovascular risk, falls, and sedatives. Make attributable changes, monitor benefit/toxicity, and discontinue ineffective medicines with appropriate tapering; nonopioid treatment still carries risk.

| Option | Selected clinical role | Important safeguards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topical NSAID | Localized superficial-joint osteoarthritis or selected peripheral musculoskeletal pain | Lower systemic exposure is not zero exposure; review allergy, skin, pregnancy, and concurrent NSAIDs |
| Oral NSAID | Selected inflammatory or musculoskeletal pain | Gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney injury, fluid retention, blood pressure, cardiovascular and interaction risks |
| Acetaminophen | Selected nociceptive pain when appropriate; benefit varies by condition | Count all combination products; liver disease, alcohol exposure, dose and duration matter |
| SNRI, such as duloxetine | Selected neuropathic or chronic musculoskeletal pain; may also address depression | Review renal/hepatic suitability, blood pressure, serotonergic interactions, mood polarity, and discontinuation effects |
| Selected tricyclic antidepressant | Some neuropathic-pain syndromes | Anticholinergic effects, orthostasis, arrhythmia, overdose toxicity, and falls can limit use |
| Gabapentin or pregabalin | Selected neuropathic indications, not a default for every back-pain syndrome | Adjust for renal function; dizziness, edema, sedation, misuse and respiratory-depressant combinations |
| Local lidocaine or capsaicin preparation | Selected localized neuropathic pain | Indication, intact skin, concentration, application instructions and tolerability differ by product |

Use an NSAID at the lowest effective dose for the needed duration, not as an automatic permanent prescription. Avoid stacking different oral NSAIDs. A gastroprotective strategy does not remove renal or cardiovascular toxicity. Gabapentinoids can contribute to serious respiratory depression, especially with opioids or other depressants, underlying respiratory impairment, or older age. Titration and renal adjustment do not eliminate this interaction. [FDA gabapentinoid warning](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/neurontin-gralise-horizant-gabapentin-and-lyrica-lyrica-cr-pregabalin-drug-safety-communication)

For appropriate adult chronic musculoskeletal pain or fibromyalgia, duloxetine delayed-release starts **30 mg orally once daily for one week, then 60 mg once daily**. Higher doses lack established additional pain benefit and increase adverse effects. Avoid GFR below 30 mL/min or chronic liver disease/cirrhosis. Review alcohol, BP, polarity, serotonergic interactions, and withdrawal. This is not a universal back-pain or pediatric musculoskeletal regimen. [Duloxetine pain-specific label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=7da3d2c4-8793-4da3-b54a-ae20717cf066)

Pregnancy requires drug-specific assessment, not the inference that an OTC medicine is safe. FDA advises avoiding NSAIDs from about 20 weeks unless specifically necessary under clinician supervision because fetal renal dysfunction can reduce amniotic fluid. Between 20 and 30 weeks, necessary treatment should be limited; at 30 weeks and later there is also a ductal-closure concern. This warning does not replace the separate use of prescribed low-dose aspirin for an obstetric indication. Do not abruptly stop a dependent patient's opioid regimen on discovering pregnancy; coordinate obstetric and pain/addiction expertise. [FDA NSAID pregnancy communication](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic)

**Clinical Case.** A 73-year-old with knee osteoarthritis, stage 3b CKD, ulcer bleeding history, and anticoagulation requests ibuprofen. Review diagnosis, walking aids, therapy, and a suitable topical option with residual-risk counseling. Oral NSAIDs compound existing hazards; follow walking function and adverse effects.

### Decide whether an opioid trial has a justified role

The CDC framework concerns outpatients aged 18 years and older with acute, subacute, or chronic pain. It **does not govern sickle-cell-related pain, cancer-related pain, palliative care, or end-of-life care**, and is not a pediatric prescribing guideline. These exclusions mean use the appropriate condition-specific pathway, not withhold analgesia. The framework is also not a rigid dosing law. Applicable prescribing rules and individual product restrictions must be checked separately. [CDC 2022 scope and recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/71/rr/rr7103a1.htm)

Prefer nonopioid/nonpharmacologic care for chronic noncancer pain. Initiate opioids only when expected pain/function benefits outweigh individual risks, with monitoring and an exit plan. Routine initiation poorly suits fibromyalgia or chronic headache. Reassessing established therapy still requires evaluation and continuity.

When starting an opioid, use an appropriate immediate-release preparation at the lowest effective dose; extended-release products and fentanyl patches are not a shortcut for an opioid-naive patient. For acute pain, prescribe only the quantity needed for the expected period of pain severe enough to require an opioid and reevaluate unexpectedly persistent symptoms. There is no single CDC three-, five-, or seven-day limit applicable to every diagnosis. Product labels, local law, and the actual recovery course still matter. [CDC opioid initiation guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/hcp/clinical-care/initiating-opioid-therapy.html)

Discuss constipation, nausea, driving impairment, sedation, respiratory depression, dependence/OUD, overdose, and storage. Assess apnea, organ disease, prior overdose, psychiatric illness, and substances; risk scores cannot guarantee safety. Tramadol adds seizure/serotonergic risks; methadone accumulation/QT and fentanyl-patch exposure require expertise. Brands or abuse-deterrent formulations do not eliminate overdose.

Build a bowel plan when opioids will be taken regularly: hydration and activity as feasible, with a prophylactic stimulant laxative such as senna when appropriate. A stool softener alone is not adequate opioid-constipation prophylaxis. Ask about stool frequency, straining, and abdominal symptoms rather than waiting for a complaint; severe pain, distension, vomiting, or obstipation needs assessment for impaction/obstruction. See 15D for laxative escalation and refractory opioid-induced constipation. [CDC bowel-regimen guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/71/rr/rr7103a1.htm)

Morphine milligram equivalents help estimate exposure across certain opioid analgesics; they do not define an equivalent safe dose for every person. Before an increase to **50 MME/day or more**, pause and reassess benefit, risk, and additional precautions. This is a cautionary guidepost, not a universal ceiling, a declaration that lower doses are safe, or an instruction to force an existing patient below it. Conversion factors are not direct opioid-rotation instructions; incomplete cross-tolerance and agent-specific pharmacology matter. Do not apply analgesic MME thresholds to set OUD-treatment doses. [CDC PDMP and MME interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/hcp/clinical-guidance/prescription-drug-monitoring-programs.html)

FDA's July 2025 labeling action emphasizes persistent long-term opioid risks that generally increase with exposure, without a risk-free duration. Worsening pain warrants reassessment for new disease, tolerance, interdose withdrawal, or hyperalgesia before automatic escalation. [FDA long-term opioid labeling update](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-requiring-opioid-pain-medicine-manufacturers-update-prescribing-information-regarding-long-term)

### Monitor treatment without turning surveillance into punishment

Reassess within **1-4 weeks** of subacute/chronic opioid initiation or dose increase, sooner with higher risk. Review long-term therapy at least every three months, more often as needed, for function, pain, sedation, constipation, breathing, actual use, new medicines, and preferences. Continue only through active benefit-risk reassessment. [CDC continuing-therapy and follow-up guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/hcp/clinical-care/continuing-opioid-therapy.html)

Check PDMP at initiation and at least every three months in long-term therapy, ideally before each prescription when accessible. Clarify unexpected entries, duplicate names, procedures, and legitimate overlapping prescriptions with patient/prescribers. Alerts do not diagnose OUD or justify abandonment; apply consistent, nondiscriminatory safety policies.

Consider the benefits and risks of toxicology testing before initiation and periodically, at least annually, during opioid treatment. Explain the purpose and how unexpected findings will be handled. An opiate immunoassay may miss fentanyl or a prescribed synthetic agent; detection also depends on the specimen, timing, assay, and other factors. Seek confirmatory testing when an unexplained result will drive a consequential decision. A positive result does not quantify impairment, and a negative result does not prove diversion. Use results to improve care rather than automatically dismissing the patient.

Agree on goals, refills, pharmacy coordination, and safe use; signatures cannot replace informed consent. Offer naloxone/household training, particularly with prior overdose, higher doses, apnea, lost tolerance, or depressants. Naloxone does not make hazardous combinations safe. Unresponsiveness or slowed breathing requires emergency action.

### Continue, reduce, or transition treatment deliberately

Continue beneficial treatment while improving the broader plan. If risks predominate, discuss reduction, discontinuation, or alternatives. Except for immediate life-threatening danger such as impending overdose, avoid abrupt cessation or forced rapid taper. Address disagreement with explanation, balanced assessment, and continuity; tapering does not invariably improve pain or suit every long-term patient.

After a year or longer of treatment, reductions around **10% per month or slower** may be better tolerated, over months to years. Individualize rather than mechanically reducing the original dose; withdrawal, distress, function, and goals may require slowing/pausing. Follow at least monthly, more often when needed, and reassess new pain. [HHS patient-centered tapering principles](https://www.hhs.gov/system/files/Dosage_Reduction_Discontinuation.pdf)

Explain that tolerance falls after reduction or interruption, so returning to a former dose can cause overdose. Assess depression, suicidality, nonprescribed use, and access to treatment during transitions. Tolerance and withdrawal during appropriate supervised prescribing do not alone establish OUD. If impaired control or harmful use suggests OUD, assess the criteria and offer evidence-based medication treatment while continuing pain care. Detoxification alone is not equivalent to ongoing OUD treatment. Buprenorphine transitions require an appropriate protocol; an indiscriminate switch can precipitate withdrawal. The medication and access pathways are developed in volume 10F. [CDC OUD diagnostic distinction](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/hcp/clinical-care/opioid-use-disorder-diagnosis.html)

**Clinical Case.** A 58-year-old transferring after four years on stable opioids reports fewer painful awakenings but new falls after a sleep drug and witnessed apneas. He is alert without imminent overdose. Reconcile therapy, assess apnea, offer naloxone, and coordinate a safer plan, potentially gradual reduction rather than abrupt withholding for exceeding a clinic target.

### Recognize intimate partner violence and respond privately

Abuse can include physical injury, sexual coercion, threats, stalking, humiliation, reproductive coercion, and control of finances, medicines, transport, or communication. There may be no visible injury. Ask about specific experiences in private using neutral, nonjudgmental language. Arrange a qualified interpreter rather than relying on a partner or child. Explain confidentiality and reporting limits before a detailed disclosure; do not promise secrecy that the law cannot support.

USPSTF's **June 2025 final recommendation** supports screening women of reproductive age, including pregnant and postpartum women, and providing or referring those with positive findings to appropriate multicomponent interventions with ongoing support. A brief questionnaire is a doorway to assessment, not the intervention itself. Evidence gaps in other populations do not justify ignoring concerning symptoms, fear, or a disclosure by a man, an older woman, or a gender-diverse patient. The recommendation does not establish a single evidence-based screening interval. [USPSTF current IPV screening recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/intimate-partner-violence-and-abuse-of-elderly-and-vulnerable-adults-screening)

Validate safety needs, assess immediate danger/medical care, and ask what help is wanted. Do not demand separation; leaving can increase danger. Connect advocacy, housing/legal, mental health, and continuing medical support. Avoid default couples counseling or joint interviews for coercive control because disclosure can provoke retaliation. [WHO first-line support principles](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK174251/)

| Finding or disclosure | Immediate clinical question | Appropriate next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Threats, weapons, escalating violence, stalking, or fear of returning home | Is there imminent danger to this person or dependents? | Urgent safety assessment with trained advocacy and emergency services when indicated |
| Neck compression, voice change, painful swallowing, breathing symptoms, loss of consciousness, or neurologic symptoms | Could there be occult airway, vascular, or brain injury? | Urgent emergency evaluation; an unmarked neck is not reassuring |
| Recent sexual assault | What injuries, pregnancy risk, infections, and time-sensitive prevention need care? | Consent-based medical and specialized forensic assessment; do not delay urgent treatment |
| Control of medicines, money, contact, or transport | Can the patient obtain care or communicate safely? | A private, individualized access and safety plan with ongoing support |

Strangulation may cause serious injury despite minimal external findings, and some complications appear later. Stabilization takes priority; imaging and observation depend on the emergency assessment, not a primary-care rule that every patient needs the same test. Document the reported mechanism and symptoms rather than substituting a bruise count for an airway or neurologic evaluation. [ACEP clinical discussion of strangulation](https://www.acepnow.com/article/how-to-evaluate-strangulation/)

After sexual assault, obtain consent for each part of the examination and involve trained services. Address injuries, emergency contraception when applicable, STI evaluation/prevention, vaccines, and mental health needs. HIV nPEP is time-sensitive: when indicated, start as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours after exposure under the current CDC pathway. Do not wait for an assailant's test or a police report to provide urgent medical care. A forensic evidence window is not a reason to refuse medical care after that window. [CDC 2025 nPEP guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/rr/rr7401a1.htm)

**Clinical Case.** A 32-year-old with headaches privately reports partner neck compression last evening. New hoarseness and painful swallowing require urgent internal-injury and safety assessment despite no bruise. Emergency care and support do not depend on leaving the relationship.

### Assess possible child maltreatment in developmental context

Assess physical, sexual, and emotional abuse and neglect through mechanism, witnesses, timing, development, and prior injuries/illness. Record each account and discrepancies neutrally. Developmentally incompatible injury warrants assessment; income, demeanor, or familiarity cannot establish or exclude abuse.

Bruising in a nonmobile infant, patterned injury, concerning torso/ear/neck injury, or unexplained oral injury can signal occult harm. Assess the whole child, not only the presenting mark. **Bruise color cannot reliably date an injury**; document color and morphology without assigning a precise age from appearance. Consider coagulopathy, dermatologic mimics, medication exposures, and accidental injury, while recognizing that a medical condition and inflicted injury can coexist. [AAP physical-abuse clinical report, reaffirmed 2026](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-lookup/doi/10.1542/peds.2015-0356)

Fracture interpretation depends on age, mobility, location, mechanism, associated findings, and bone health. A spiral configuration alone is not a diagnosis of abuse. AAP's 2025 guidance recommends a properly performed skeletal survey for children younger than 24 months with fracture and increased concern for abuse; selected children aged 24-60 months may also need one. This is not one whole-body radiograph. Repeat the survey about **two weeks** later for positive/equivocal findings or persistent strong suspicion despite a negative study. Evaluate occult head injury urgently: head imaging is indicated in infants younger than six months with a fracture suspicious for abuse and may be considered through the first year. Do not wait for neurologic symptoms or the follow-up survey before obtaining indicated acute imaging. [AAP 2025 fracture evaluation](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-lookup/doi/10.1542/peds.2024-070074)

Investigate relevant bleeding or bone disorders without allowing testing to delay protection from immediate danger. History alone cannot exclude every bleeding disorder, and normal initial screening studies do not exclude every hemostatic diagnosis. The required tests depend on the presentation and specialist advice. Severe head injury, altered consciousness, seizures, vomiting with other concerning findings, or abdominal signs require urgent evaluation for occult injury; do not infer that a normal skin examination excludes serious trauma. [AAP bleeding-disorder assessment](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-abstract/150/4/e2022059276/189510)

**Clinical Case.** A 10-week-old who cannot roll has an unexplained ear bruise but normal feeding/alertness. Arrange prompt child-protection-informed assessment for occult injury, consider bleeding causes, and meet reporting duties. Do not await another injury or date the bruise as two days old by color.

When sexual abuse is disclosed or suspected, ask only the limited open-ended questions needed for medical care and safety, record the child's words, and obtain specialized assessment. Avoid repeated leading interviews or attempts to reproduce an alleged event. A normal genital examination does not exclude abuse. STI findings require age-, organism-, transmission-, and testing-specific interpretation; they are not all automatically proof of the same mechanism. Use an expert pediatric pathway rather than applying the adult empiric-treatment protocol to every child. [CDC child-specific STI evaluation](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/sexual-assault-children.htm)

Assess unmet needs, harm/risk, caregiver capacity, and resources. Missed visits may reflect transport or cost barriers; offer practical assistance while protecting safety. Poverty/disability do not establish neglect, and stress does not excuse danger. Document, treat, and report at the applicable threshold rather than deciding criminal guilt.

### Recognize elder abuse, exploitation, and unsafe caregiving

Older and vulnerable adults may experience physical or sexual abuse, psychological intimidation, neglect, abandonment, or financial exploitation. Ask privately about food, hygiene, medicines, mobility assistance, finances, and who controls access to people or services. Unpaid bills despite adequate resources, unexplained account changes, fear of a caregiver, withheld medication, or injuries inconsistent with the account warrant assessment. Frail skin, anticoagulation, falls, delirium, and disease progression can mimic or complicate findings, but do not automatically exclude mistreatment. [NIA signs of elder abuse](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/elder-abuse/spotting-signs-elder-abuse)

The USPSTF insufficient-evidence statement concerns routine screening for caregiver abuse in older or vulnerable adults. It does **not** recommend ignoring weight loss, pressure injury, financial coercion, or a disclosure. Assess nutrition, hydration, wounds, medication access, delirium, function, and the support needed at home. Caregiver exhaustion may call for respite, home services, training, and treatment; it does not excuse intentional harm. Self-neglect may also require protective support, but should not be conflated with abuse by another person.

Assess capacity for the particular decision: can the person understand the information, appreciate the consequences, reason about the options, and communicate a choice with appropriate support? Dementia, an unusual preference, or refusal of a suggested placement does not alone establish incapacity. Correct reversible contributors such as delirium and hearing barriers. If an alleged abuser is also the usual surrogate, involve safeguarding, ethics, and legal resources to address the conflict rather than simply accepting that person's authority. Distinguish bedside clinical capacity from a court's legal determination of competence.

| Situation | What to establish | Useful response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possible financial exploitation | The patient's account, understanding, access to funds, and unexplained changes | Document concerns and connect to protective/legal resources without assuming all family assistance is abuse |
| Inadequate personal care | Immediate medical needs, actual available assistance, and barriers | Treat complications, arrange support, and assess reporting/protective needs |
| Caregiver controls all communication | Whether private contact is possible and the patient feels safe | Create a safe interview and communication plan; do not rely only on the caregiver |
| Unsafe discharge or contested surrogate | Capacity, imminent danger, alternatives, and conflicts of interest | Multidisciplinary protection and lawful decision-making; no automatic forced placement |

Adult Protective Services is a principal public route for concerns about elder or vulnerable-adult abuse, neglect, or exploitation. Exact eligibility, duties, and reporting channels vary. Facility concerns may also involve the long-term-care ombudsman, licensing authorities, or law enforcement as required; notifying a facility manager alone may not meet a reporting obligation. An emergency requires immediate medical and protective action. A capable adult's choices remain important even when clinicians disagree with them. [ACL protective-service pathways](https://acl.gov/programs/protection-and-advocacy-systems/what-if-i-suspect-abuse-neglect-or-exploitation)

**Clinical Case.** An 81-year-old with mild dementia misses meals and refills. His niece cites inadequate pension, but he privately reports pressure to sign transfers he did not understand. Assess nutrition, medication harm, decision-specific capacity, appropriate collateral consent, and protective support. Dementia does not remove autonomy or resolve the niece's potential conflict.

### Make safety planning, documentation, and follow-up usable

Individualize safety plans around feasible contacts, dependents, medicines, documents, transport, and shelter. Arrange help without alerting communication monitors. Printed materials, portal entries, voicemail, or visit summaries can expose disclosures; agree on safe contact times/routes and institutional privacy procedures without promising hidden records.

Separate observations from reports: record relevant patient words, collateral sources, injury location/size, examination, tests, consultations, safety, reporting, actions, and follow-up ownership. Follow consent, photography, body-map, storage, and forensic-custody policies. Avoid unsupported labels, speculative injury dates, or claims that normal examination disproves disclosure.

Child-abuse reporting generally rests on suspicion or reasonable concern under the applicable statute, not proof after a clinician completes an investigation. Check the jurisdiction's threshold, designated receiving agency, urgency, written follow-up, and individual reporter duties. A supervisor's notification does not necessarily discharge a clinician's own obligation. Do not discharge a child to an unsafe setting while awaiting an outpatient investigation. Adult IPV, vulnerable-adult abuse, weapon injuries, and trafficking have different reporting rules; there is no blanket national rule to report every competent adult's IPV disclosure to police. Explain required disclosures when doing so does not increase danger; avoid confronting the alleged perpetrator or promising an absolute legal outcome. [AAP fracture-report reporting principle](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-lookup/doi/10.1542/peds.2024-070074)

**Clinical Case.** A patient reports coercive control, shared-portal monitoring, and partner access to pharmacy texts. They need medicines and a safe future call, not a take-home brochure. With advocacy, agree on safe contact, assess danger/dependents, and explain reporting limits. Disclosure-revealing automated messages could undermine safety.

Suspected trafficking calls for the same private, trauma-informed attention to safety and choice, with specialized services and locally required protection/reporting. Do not infer trafficking from a tattoo, nationality, occupation, or a person being reluctant to speak. Treat the immediate medical problem and avoid demanding a full account or participation in a prosecution as the price of care. Detailed trafficking protocols, minor-specific rules, and jurisdictional reporting procedures require dedicated local resources rather than a universal bedside shortcut. [HHS survivor-centered care principles](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/private/pdf/262596/hhs-2018-symposium-report-final.pdf)

Address cost, accessibility, language, rural distance, leave, housing, discrimination, and unsafe dependence. Coordinate primary care, pharmacy, rehabilitation, behavioral/pain/addiction care, social work, and advocacy as needed. Confirm referrals and functional/safety benefit. Missed visits may warrant safe outreach rather than assumptions of nonadherence.
