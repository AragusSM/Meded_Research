# Medivale Family Medicine

## 11A. Headache

Assess headache for dangerous secondary causes while treating an established primary disorder. Routine scanning is unnecessary for a familiar primary pattern with a reassuring assessment. Migraine is disabling neurologic disease, but does not protect against hemorrhage, infection, vascular disease, or pregnancy complications.

ICHD-3 defines headache syndromes; AAN/AHS guides adult prevention and ACP acute treatment. Children, pregnancy/lactation, and emergency rescue require population-specific eligibility and dosing.

### Reconstruct the headache, not just its location

Establish when headaches began, the speed from onset to peak pain, duration, frequency, and evolution. Ask the patient to distinguish an individual attack from the background headache between attacks. Record headache days and migraine-feature days per month, functional impairment, associated nausea, sensory sensitivity, visual or other neurologic symptoms, autonomic symptoms, and recovery. Ask about sleep, meals, caffeine, exertion, position, menstruation, trauma, infection, pregnancy/postpartum status, vascular risks, cancer, immune suppression, and family history. Review every acute medicine by **days used**, including combination products and medicines taken for other pain.

Examine vital signs, general appearance, mental status, cranial nerves, visual function, strength, sensation, coordination, gait, and the fundi when possible. Look for meningismus, relevant head/neck findings, and a painful red eye. In an older adult, ask specifically about jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, constitutional symptoms, and shoulder/hip stiffness. A limited or uncertain funduscopic examination is not proof that papilledema is absent. Document what was assessed and what requires another examiner. [VA/DoD assessment framework](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/pain/headache/VA-DOD-CPG-Headache-Provider-Summary.pdf)

Use a diary to track symptoms, treatment timing, response, and disability, including ten days of lesser pain the patient may overlook. Assess recovery of work, sleep, and caregiving, not pain scores alone. Before labeling treatment failure, check drug choice, timing, vomiting, affordability, and trial duration.

### Identify a primary pattern without turning clues into requirements

Migraine involves altered sensory processing and trigeminovascular pain pathways, including CGRP signaling; describing it simply as dilating blood vessels is inadequate. In adults, migraine without aura ordinarily involves attacks lasting **4-72 hours untreated or unsuccessfully treated**, with at least two of unilateral pain, pulsation, moderate/severe intensity, or aggravation by routine activity. There must also be nausea/vomiting or both photophobia and phonophobia, with no better explanation. Definite classification requires at least five attacks; fewer otherwise compatible attacks may be classified as probable migraine. Children can have shorter and more often bilateral attacks. [ICHD-3 migraine without aura](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-1-migraine-without-aura/)

| Pattern | Typical clinical behavior | Distinction worth asking about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Migraine | Activity worsens a disabling attack; nausea or light/sound sensitivity is common | Bilateral or nonpulsating pain does not by itself exclude migraine |
| Episodic tension-type headache | Pressing/tightening, mild/moderate pain without worsening from routine activity | Vomiting or combined photophobia and phonophobia argues against the usual episodic pattern |
| Cluster headache | Excruciating one-sided orbital/temporal pain with autonomic findings or restlessness | The patient often paces rather than seeking stillness in a dark room |

Tension-type headache is not synonymous with stress. Definite episodic disease requires at least ten episodes, lasting 30 minutes to seven days, with at least two of bilateral location, pressure/tightness, mild/moderate intensity, and no worsening with routine activity. There is no nausea/vomiting and no more than one of light or sound sensitivity. Frequent episodic disease occupies 1-14 days/month for more than three months; chronic disease occupies at least 15 days/month for more than three months and can be unremitting. Chronic tension-type headache permits no more than one of photophobia, phonophobia, or mild nausea, but not moderate/severe nausea or vomiting. Migraine and episodic tension-type attacks can coexist; if chronic migraine criteria are met, do not also label the remaining pressure-like days chronic tension-type headache. [ICHD-3 episodic criteria](https://ichd-3.org/2-tension-type-headache/2-2-frequent-episodic-tension-type-headache/), [chronic criteria](https://ichd-3.org/2-tension-type-headache/2-3-chronic-tension-type-headache/)

**Clinical Case.** A 41-year-old has six monthly headache days. Disabling episodes worsen with stairs and include light/sound intolerance; milder days feel like bilateral pressure. Recognize the migraine component, document each pattern, assess secondary features, and provide effective acute treatment.

### Aura is a specific neurologic sequence

Aura consists of fully reversible neurologic symptoms, commonly visual, sensory, or speech/language phenomena. ICHD-3 requires at least two attacks and at least three of six features: spread over at least five minutes, successive symptoms, each symptom lasting 5-60 minutes, unilateral symptoms, positive symptoms, and headache accompanying or following within 60 minutes. Not every feature is obligatory; aura without headache is possible. Sequential symptoms can make the whole aura longer than an hour, and motor symptoms can last up to 72 hours. A spreading scintillating area followed by migrating tingling differs from simultaneous abrupt loss of function, but this distinction is not an emergency rule-out test. [ICHD-3 migraine with aura](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-2-migraine-with-aura/)

First weakness, new monocular visual loss, persistent deficits, or unusual late-life symptoms require assessment even when headache follows. Sudden deficits need stroke/TIA evaluation; changed stereotyped aura also warrants reassessment. Clarify whether vision changes affect one eye or the same field with either eye open. Migraine with aura makes combined hormonal contraception US MEC category 4 (unacceptable health risk); discuss alternatives and smoking. [CDC contraceptive eligibility](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html)

Chronic migraine requires headache on **at least 15 days per month for more than three months**, with migraine features on at least eight days per month in a patient with the appropriate migraine history and no better explanation. Counting attacks is not the same as counting days: one prolonged attack can occupy several days. Medication-overuse headache can be diagnosed alongside chronic migraine. A precisely remembered onset of continuous daily pain from the first day suggests a different history that needs evaluation rather than automatically representing gradual migraine chronification. [ICHD-3 chronic migraine](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-3-chronic-migraine/)

### Red flags change the disposition

Assess thunderclap onset, systemic illness, new neurologic findings, onset after age 50, cancer/immune impairment, pregnancy/puerperium, progression, positional or exertional/Valsalva symptoms, trauma, and papilledema. Urgency follows the suspected cause, not pain intensity; emergencies require emergency-capable assessment. [ACR headache imaging scenarios](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69482/Narrative/)

| Finding or context | Important concern | Immediate clinical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abrupt maximal or rapidly peaking severe pain | Hemorrhage, RCVS, dissection, other vascular emergencies | Emergency evaluation even with a normal initial neurologic examination |
| Fever, meningismus, altered awareness, or immune impairment | Infection or an intracranial complication | Urgent testing and treatment; do not wait for every classic feature |
| Papilledema, progressive visual symptoms, or new focal deficit | Raised pressure, mass, venous thrombosis, stroke | Urgent neurologic/ophthalmic assessment with targeted imaging |
| New headache after age 50 with jaw or visual symptoms | Giant cell arteritis | Same-day treatment assessment; visual ischemia is an emergency |
| Pregnancy/postpartum with hypertension or neurologic symptoms | Preeclampsia/PRES, CVT, hemorrhage | Urgent obstetric and neurologic evaluation |

Thunderclap reaches peak intensity within a minute. Exclude subarachnoid hemorrhage and other vascular causes before diagnosing primary thunderclap headache. Recurrent episodes suggest possible reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome; early unrevealing imaging does not settle persistent or changing symptoms. Ask about exertion, sexual activity, neck injury, and vasoactive exposures. [ICHD-3 thunderclap differential](https://ichd-3.org/other-primary-headache-disorders/4-4-primary-thunderclap-headache/)

Suspected nontraumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage generally requires emergency noncontrast head CT. ACEP permits selected neurologically intact adults to be ruled out with a normal high-quality CT obtained **within six hours**, under specified scanner and interpretation conditions. Not every normal CT excludes hemorrhage. If risk persists, additional evaluation may involve lumbar puncture or CTA, selected through the emergency pathway with discussion of limitations. Delayed imaging, uncertain timing, or another suspected diagnosis changes the decision. [ACEP acute headache clinical policy](https://www.acep.org/siteassets/sites/acep/media/clinical-policies/cp-headache.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 58-year-old develops maximal headache while lifting a planter; CT nine hours later shows no hemorrhage. The six-hour selected-patient rule does not apply at nine hours. Persistent concern requires further emergency evaluation, even if an antiemetic relieves pain.

### Choose testing for the suspected mechanism

A stable migraine or tension-type pattern with a normal examination and no concerning features generally does not require routine neuroimaging. Imaging for reassurance alone can reveal incidental findings without improving care. When investigation is indicated, select the modality and timing for the question: acute bleeding, a mass/posterior fossa process, arterial disease, and venous thrombosis do not all use the same study. Routine EEG or broad laboratory panels do not confirm an ordinary migraine diagnosis. [AHS neuroimaging guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31891197/)

Cerebral venous thrombosis can cause progressive headache, visual symptoms, seizures, or focal deficits, particularly with pregnancy/puerperium, estrogen exposure, malignancy, infection, or other prothrombotic states. Arrange urgent assessment with **CT venography or MR venography**; routine noncontrast CT cannot equivalently exclude it. [AHA cerebral venous thrombosis statement summary](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/diagnosis-and-management-of-cerebral-venous-thrombosis/top-things-to-know)

Papilledema with headache requires assessment for a mass, venous thrombosis, and other causes of raised intracranial pressure. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension is a diagnosis made after appropriate exclusion, not simply headache in a patient with obesity. Visual fields and optic-nerve assessment matter because vision can be threatened even when pain is manageable. Imaging, including venous assessment, precedes appropriately selected lumbar puncture for pressure and CSF analysis. Disease-modifying weight management, selected pressure-lowering treatment, and urgent visual-protection procedures belong in coordinated specialist care. [IIH consensus investigation framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6166610/)

Headache, dizziness, or nausea affecting several people in an enclosed space suggests carbon monoxide. Ask about heaters, generators, fires, and improvement away from exposure. Move to safety and arrange emergency assessment and appropriate oxygen treatment. Ordinary pulse oximetry inadequately distinguishes carboxyhemoglobin and can mislead. [CDC carbon monoxide guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

### Build an acute migraine plan before the next attack

Match acute treatment to disability, prior response, contraindications, vomiting, attack speed, and access. Treat early in the headache phase with a usable formulation. Specify initial treatment, steps for inadequate/recurrent relief, dose ceilings, and emergency precautions for unfamiliar symptoms.

For nonpregnant adults with acute episodic migraine inadequately relieved by an NSAID, the **2025 ACP pathway recommends adding a triptan to the NSAID**; it also suggests adding a triptan when acetaminophen alone is inadequate. Consider combination treatment early in an appropriate patient rather than repeatedly waiting for a familiar inadequate response. Assess kidney disease, GI bleeding, anticoagulants, cardiovascular disease, and cumulative acetaminophen exposure before selecting the nonspecific analgesic component. [ACP acute-treatment recommendations](https://www.acponline.org/acp-newsroom/new-acp-guideline-recommends-combination-therapy-for-acute-episodic-migraines-in-outpatient)

| Acute-treatment situation | Reasonable treatment direction | Safety boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appropriate nonpregnant adult with inadequate NSAID/acetaminophen response | Add a suitable triptan; match route to the attack | Check vascular contraindications and recent triptan/ergot use |
| Vomiting or rapid escalation | Consider a suitable nasal/injectable option and an antiemetic | A dissolving oral tablet does not necessarily bypass GI absorption |
| Triptan contraindication or inadequate tolerability | Consider a migraine-specific nonvasoconstrictive option such as a gepant | Check the individual drug's interactions, organ limits, and vascular warnings |
| Prolonged severe symptoms or inability to hydrate | Reassess diagnosis and arrange supervised rescue when needed | Do not replace evaluation with routine opioid or barbiturate escalation |

Oral sumatriptan single doses are **25, 50, or 100 mg**. Consider repeat dosing after some initial response, at least two hours apart, to a maximum of **200 mg in 24 hours**. Mild/moderate hepatic impairment limits a single dose to 50 mg; severe impairment is a contraindication. Intranasal and injectable products require their own dosing limits. [Sumatriptan tablet label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=2efd4269-5657-9669-e063-6394a90a583a)

Triptans are unsuitable with coronary ischemia/vasospasm, prior stroke/TIA, uncontrolled hypertension, and certain other vascular or conduction conditions. The US sumatriptan label also contraindicates hemiplegic or basilar migraine, ergot/another triptan use within 24 hours, and MAO-A inhibitor use currently or within the preceding two weeks. Unusual aura subtypes need specialist planning. Review serotonergic combinations; chest symptoms after a dose require risk assessment, not automatic reassurance.

Rimegepant has separate acute and preventive uses: **75 mg orally as needed, no more than 75 mg in 24 hours**, versus 75 mg every other day for episodic prevention. Safety beyond 18 doses in 30 days is unestablished. Avoid strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and strong/moderate CYP3A inducers; with a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor or potent P-glycoprotein inhibitor, do not repeat within 48 hours. Avoid severe hepatic impairment or end-stage renal disease. Hypersensitivity, hypertension, and Raynaud warnings still apply: nonvasoconstrictive does not mean free of monitoring or interactions. [Current rimegepant label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=9ef08e09-1098-35cc-e053-2a95a90a3e1d)

**Clinical Case.** A 63-year-old with prior myocardial infarction requests a previously effective triptan. The contraindication overrides prior benefit. Discuss a nonvasoconstrictive alternative, interactions, blood-pressure effects, affordability, prevention, and emergency assessment of unfamiliar symptoms.

An antiemetic can improve nausea and make the overall rescue strategy usable; dopamine antagonists also have evidence in supervised acute migraine care. Persistent severe attacks, dehydration, or a changed pattern need reassessment before assigning treatment resistance. In the **2025 AHS emergency update**, intravenous prochlorperazine and greater occipital nerve block have strong recommendations for eligible patients requiring parenteral treatment, whereas intravenous hydromorphone must not be offered for migraine. These are trained, monitored emergency-care options, not office instructions for an undifferentiated headache. [Current AHS emergency evidence assessment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41321235/)

Status migrainosus is a debilitating attack lasting more than 72 hours in a patient with established migraine, otherwise resembling prior attacks; brief remissions up to 12 hours from sleep or treatment are allowed. Reassess secondary causes and overuse before applying the label. When full medication-overuse headache criteria explain the course, classify that disorder and the underlying migraine rather than adding status migrainosus. Inability to hydrate or maintain function warrants supervised rescue, not waiting at home for the duration threshold. [ICHD-3 status migrainosus](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-4-complications-of-migraine/1-4-1-status-migrainosus/)

### Offer prevention for disability, not only very frequent attacks

The **2026 AAN/AHS framework** recommends offering preventive treatment with four or more migraine days per month, four or more moderate/severe headache days per month, or meaningful interference with work and daily tasks. Frequency alone should not determine access: fewer prolonged disabling attacks may still justify prevention. Review reproductive plans, comorbidities, route preference, cost, and adverse effects. Explain that prevention reduces burden and improves function; it is not necessarily a promise of zero attacks. [AAN current guideline overview](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/5361)

AHS supports **CGRP-targeting therapies as first-line options without mandatory prior failure of older classes**. The 2026 AAN/AHS guideline individualizes older and newer options to patient priorities. Insurance step-therapy requirements govern access, not clinical appropriateness. [AHS current prevention update](https://americanheadachesociety.org/news/updated-guideline-on-migraine-prevention-medications-for-adults), [AHS CGRP position statement](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.14692)

ACP's **2025 nonpregnant episodic-migraine** pathway differs: selected older oral monotherapy first, CGRP agents after inadequate response/intolerance, then topiramate, with cost and preferences informing priorities. This sequence is not universal adult or chronic-migraine guidance. Both ACP and AAN/AHS require patient-specific safety assessment; sequence never overrides contraindications. [ACP prevention framework](https://www.acponline.org/acp-newsroom/new-acp-guideline-on-migraine-prevention-shows-no-clinically-important-advantages-for-newer)

| Preventive option | Clinical fit to discuss | Reason to choose cautiously or avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propranolol or metoprolol | A useful overlapping cardiovascular indication | Bradycardia, hypotension; nonselective beta blockade is problematic in asthma |
| Topiramate | May fit some patients concerned about weight | Cognitive effects, stones/acidosis, ocular symptoms, reproductive risk |
| Amitriptyline or selected other antidepressant | Some overlapping sleep, pain, or mood problems | Sedation, anticholinergic burden, orthostasis; monitor drug-specific risks |
| CGRP monoclonal antibody or preventive gepant | Migraine-specific treatment and route/frequency preference | Individual labeling, constipation/vascular warnings, interactions, pregnancy uncertainty, access |
| OnabotulinumtoxinA | Selected chronic migraine | Requires appropriate chronic-migraine eligibility and a trained injection protocol |

Preventive options differ in evidence, indications, and off-label use. Topiramate can cause cognitive slowing, bicarbonate loss, stones, and acute ocular complications. Assess cognition, stone history, bicarbonate, kidney function, pregnancy potential, and new symptoms; titrate individually. Do not abruptly stop antiseizure medicines without a plan. [Topiramate label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=2f5b1b0b-fd8a-4c4e-a85a-519ca841a0db)

For an eligible adult choosing immediate-release topiramate, the labeled migraine schedule starts at 25 mg nightly, then weekly advances to 25 mg twice daily, 25 mg morning/50 mg evening, and 50 mg twice daily. Slower titration may be needed. This is a 100-mg/day migraine target, not an epilepsy regimen. With creatinine clearance below 70 mL/min/1.73 m2, the label recommends half the usual adult dose; dialysis needs a separate plan. Discuss effective contraception and fetal risk before treatment, not after a positive pregnancy test. [Topiramate dosing and renal sections](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=2f5b1b0b-fd8a-4c4e-a85a-519ca841a0db)

Valproate migraine prevention is **contraindicated in pregnancy and in patients of childbearing potential not using effective contraception** under the US label. Assess liver disease, mitochondrial/urea-cycle disorders, pancreatitis, thrombocytopenia, and interactions, with ongoing reproductive counseling. [Current divalproex label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=08a65cf4-7749-4ceb-6895-8f4805e2b01f)

Set an assessment date: AAN/AHS recommends **8-12 weeks at a recommended tolerated dose for most preventives**, and **24 weeks for onabotulinumtoxinA**, before judging efficacy. Review adverse effects sooner; an adequate trial does not require enduring unsafe treatment. Compare migraine/headache days, rescue use, disability, and the patient's goals. Optimize a suboptimal dose when appropriate, switch ineffective treatment deliberately, and maintain an acute plan while prevention takes effect. After six months of effective prevention, discuss individualized benefits/risks of tapering rather than assuming lifelong use or automatic discontinuation. [2026 AAN/AHS prevention recommendations](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214881)

### Medication overuse is a treatment problem, not a character judgment

Medication-overuse headache requires headache on **15 or more days per month**, a pre-existing headache disorder, and regular overuse for **more than three months**, with no better explanation. The exposure threshold depends on the medicine. Taking an analgesic often is not by itself enough to diagnose the full syndrome, and improvement after withdrawal is not required before recognizing it. Document both the underlying headache disorder and overuse when criteria are met. [ICHD-3 medication-overuse headache](https://ichd-3.org/8-headache-attributed-to-a-substance-or-its-withdrawal/8-2-medication-overuse-headache-moh/)

| Acute medication pattern | Monthly exposure associated with the ICHD-3 subtype | Counting point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Triptans, ergots, opioids, or combination analgesics | At least 10 days for more than three months | Count use-days, not just the number of tablets |
| Nonopioid analgesics such as acetaminophen or NSAIDs | At least 15 days for more than three months | Different nonopioid analgesics contribute to the same class assessment |
| Multiple relevant classes, none individually overused | Combined use on at least 10 days for more than three months | Count distinct treatment days and review overlap |

**Clinical Case.** A patient has 20 headache days monthly for four months, using sumatriptan on six days and an NSAID on seven, with one overlapping day: **6 + 7 - 1 = 12 distinct acute-treatment days**. Neither class individually reaches its threshold, but this pattern supports evaluation for multiple-class overuse. [ICHD-3 multiple-class subtype](https://ichd-3.org/8-headache-attributed-to-a-substance-or-its-withdrawal/8-2-medication-overuse-headache-moh/8-2-6-medication-overuse-headache-attributed-to-multiple-drug-classes-not-individually-overused/)

Explain the symptom-rescue cycle and agree on withdrawal/reduction, prevention, and close follow-up, anticipating temporary worsening. Opioid or barbiturate dependence may require supervised tapering and higher-level care rather than simple analgesic/triptan reduction. Assess withdrawal risk before stopping a barbiturate-containing product and provide alternative pain treatment. [Medication-overuse management guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9422154/)

### Treat tension-type and cluster headache as different disorders

For an uncomplicated episodic tension-type headache, a suitable simple analgesic and attention to contributing sleep, posture, workload, or musculoskeletal issues may suffice. Frequent or chronic symptoms require reassessment for migraine, overuse, and secondary causes. Amitriptyline is an option for chronic tension-type prevention, with attention to sedation, orthostasis, anticholinergic effects, and overdose risk. Do not transfer a chronic-migraine botulinum-toxin indication to chronic tension-type headache. Physical activity and appropriately selected rehabilitation can support function rather than implying pain is imaginary.

Cluster headache consists of severe unilateral orbital, supraorbital, or temporal attacks lasting **15-180 minutes untreated**, with ipsilateral autonomic findings such as tearing, conjunctival injection, rhinorrhea, ptosis/miosis, or marked restlessness. Attacks may recur from once every other day to eight times daily during an active period. Definite classification requires at least five attacks and exclusion of a better explanation. Periodicity and pacing help distinguish the syndrome, but first presentations or atypical findings require evaluation for secondary mimics. [ICHD-3 cluster headache](https://ichd-3.org/3-trigeminal-autonomic-cephalalgias/3-1-cluster-headache/)

Because attacks peak quickly, ordinary oral analgesics are usually poorly matched to the problem. The EAN pathway recommends **100% oxygen at a flow of at least 12 L/min through a nonrebreather mask**, or an appropriate rapid triptan such as **sumatriptan 6 mg subcutaneously**, in an eligible adult. Oxygen equipment and fire safety matter; a low-flow nasal cannula is not an equivalent treatment. Verapamil prevention requires baseline and follow-up ECG assessment for conduction effects, including during titration. Transitional corticosteroid or nerve-block treatment and alternative preventives belong in specialist planning rather than prolonged empiric steroid courses. [EAN cluster treatment guideline](https://www.uems-neuroboard.org/web/images/docs/exam/2024/papers/06-EAN-guideline-cluster-headache2023.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 36-year-old has 45-minute right orbital attacks with tearing and pacing, twice nightly for several weeks. After assessment, use an oxygen-based acute plan and specialist prevention. Verapamil requires ECG monitoring.

### Recognize trigeminal neuralgia rather than labeling every facial pain a headache

Brief, recurrent unilateral electric-shock facial pain triggered by light touch, chewing, speaking, or tooth brushing suggests **trigeminal neuralgia**, usually in the maxillary/mandibular distribution. Attacks typically last a fraction of a second to two minutes; a pain-free interval does not invalidate the history. Examine cranial nerves and the mouth, and distinguish dental disease, zoster, and temporomandibular pain. Sensory/motor loss, bilateral symptoms, young onset, systemic illness, or hearing/visual findings raises concern for a secondary cause, including MS or a mass. Avoid repeated irreversible dental treatment without a dental diagnosis.

Arrange specialist assessment and MRI for the underlying cause; RCS guidance supports starting treatment while referring when the syndrome is convincing. Carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine is usual first-line drug treatment, not routine opioids. Selection requires interaction, sodium, blood-count/liver, reproductive, and serious-rash risk assessment, including ancestry-dependent pharmacogenetic precautions where indicated. Refractory pain or intolerance warrants specialist procedural discussion rather than indefinite analgesic escalation. [RCS final trigeminal-neuralgia guideline](https://www.rcseng.ac.uk/-/media/files/rcs/fds/guidelines/trigemina-neuralgia-guidelines_2021_v4.pdf), [Carbamazepine safety label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=f3465f71-8931-43b6-baea-eac7046a4400)

### Pregnancy, postpartum headache, and older age

A new, severe, changed, or persistent headache in pregnancy or after delivery needs a lower threshold for secondary evaluation. Check blood pressure and consider preeclampsia, hemorrhage, CVT, PRES, infection, and pituitary disease according to the presentation. Preeclampsia can first appear postpartum, including after a normotensive pregnancy. A neuraxial procedure may suggest a postdural-puncture headache, particularly with a positional pattern, but does not exclude another simultaneous disorder. [ACOG pregnancy/postpartum headache scope](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2022/05/headaches-in-pregnancy-and-postpartum)

**Clinical Case.** Nine days postpartum, a patient with migraine develops persistent unfamiliar headache, blurred vision, and marked hypertension. Arrange urgent obstetric/emergency assessment despite an uncomplicated delivery.

For confirmed primary headache in pregnancy, begin nonpharmacologic care and obstetric medication review. ACOG supports judicious acetaminophen at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary duration; unproven neurodevelopmental causation does not justify abandoning it. Metoclopramide, cautiously individualized sumatriptan, and selected other treatments may be considered; safety cannot be generalized across triptans. [ACOG acetaminophen advisory](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-advisory/articles/2025/09/acetaminophen-use-in-pregnancy-and-neurodevelopmental-outcomes), [ACOG patient medication guidance](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/headaches-and-pregnancy)

Avoid unsupervised NSAIDs from **20 weeks onward** because of fetal renal/low-amniotic-fluid risk. If necessary between 20 and 30 weeks, clinician-directed treatment uses the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration, with additional monitoring considerations if prolonged; avoid at 30 weeks and later because of ductal risk as well. This does not mean stopping prescribed low-dose aspirin used for an obstetric indication. Preventive drugs need separate preconception review, especially valproate, topiramate, renin-angiotensin drugs, and newer agents with limited pregnancy data. Lactation decisions depend on the specific drug and infant, not a blanket rule for an entire class. [FDA pregnancy NSAID warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic)

New headache after age 50 warrants attention to vascular, inflammatory, neoplastic, and medication causes. **Giant cell arteritis** can threaten vision: a new headache with jaw claudication, visual symptoms, scalp tenderness, constitutional illness, or polymyalgia features needs urgent assessment. Obtain appropriate inflammatory/hematologic tests and arrange confirmatory vascular/biopsy evaluation, but do not delay indicated glucocorticoid treatment for a scheduled biopsy. Threatened visual loss may require intravenous pulse treatment through emergency specialist care. A normal-looking eye or an incomplete set of systemic symptoms does not make ischemia safe to observe. [ACR/VF giant cell arteritis guidance](https://www.vasculitisfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2021-ACR-VF-Guideline-for-Management-of-Giant-Cell-Arteritis-and-Takayasu-Artheritis.pdf)

### Follow the patient, not only the prescription

Agree on manageable sleep, meal, hydration, caffeine, and activity goals; address mood, sleep disorders, and other pain. In children, assess school/family function and use pediatric guidance, not informally scaled adult doses. In older adults, review cognitive/anticholinergic burden, falls, kidney function, anticoagulation, and vascular contraindications.

Review the diary, access, adverse effects, rescue days, and regained activities. Specify changes and reassessment responsibility. Urgent precautions include abrupt onset, new deficits, fever, altered awareness, persistent visual change, or substantially different headache.
