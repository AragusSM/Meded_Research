# Medivale Family Medicine

## 11C. Movement and Cognitive Complaints

Assess movement and cognition through daily tasks: pouring, buttoning, rising, navigating, and managing medicines. Establish pattern, time course, and functional impact, first identifying abrupt change, medical instability, medication effects, or progressive disability.

The AAN early Parkinson framework, MDS essential-tremor evidence review, and Alzheimer's Association DETeCD-ADRD evaluation pathway guide the main decisions. Symptomatic medicines, advanced procedures, and disease-targeting Alzheimer treatments have different purposes and eligibility requirements.

### Describe the movement before diagnosing it

Tremor is rhythmic oscillation. Observe rest, outstretched posture, writing/spiral drawing, and reaching for a cup. Ask onset, progression, symmetry, distribution, triggers, substances, family history, and medicine changes. Examine tone, repetitive movements, coordination, eye movements, speech, gait, arm swing, and balance. Task recordings can supplement the examination.

Distinguish tremor from dystonia (patterned twisting/posturing), chorea (irregular flowing movement), myoclonus (brief shock-like jerks), tics (recurrent movements/sounds, often with urge and temporary suppressibility), and akathisia (distressing need to move). These patterns guide medication review and differential diagnosis; worsening under observation does not establish anxiety.

The MDS syndrome definition of essential tremor includes **bilateral upper-limb action tremor for at least three years** without another defining neurologic disorder. A shorter-duration compatible tremor still warrants evaluation and symptom care; the duration criterion is not a reason to ignore impairment. Family history and improvement with alcohol can support the history but are not required or diagnostic, and alcohol is not a recommended treatment. Reassess the label if additional neurologic signs emerge. [MDS tremor definitions and clinical overview](https://www.movementdisorders.org/MDS/About/Movement-Disorder-Overviews/Tremor--Essential-Tremor.htm)

Testing follows the history rather than a universal tremor panel. Consider glucose, thyroid function, relevant chemistry, and medication levels when clinically indicated. A younger patient with atypical tremor, dystonia, liver disease, or psychiatric/neurologic findings needs assessment for disorders such as Wilson disease. A single ceruloplasmin result is not a complete Wilson evaluation. Acute onset, focal findings, major gait change, toxic exposure, or rapidly progressive illness calls for expedited investigation rather than routine essential-tremor treatment.

| Pattern | Findings that help distinguish it | Important alternative or caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Essential-tremor pattern | Predominantly bilateral upper-limb action tremor, sometimes with head/voice involvement | Isolated head tremor, sudden onset, dystonia, or ataxia requires another explanation |
| Parkinsonian pattern | Slowness with decrement on repetitive tasks, rigidity, often asymmetric rest tremor | Tremor may be absent; action tremor does not exclude Parkinson disease |
| Enhanced physiologic/drug-related tremor | Fine action tremor associated with stimulants, adrenergic drugs, metabolic change, or withdrawal | Correct the provoking condition rather than assigning a lifelong tremor diagnosis |
| Cerebellar pattern | Worsening near a target with dysmetria, gait/limb ataxia, or ocular findings | An acute presentation can indicate stroke or toxicity |
| Dystonic or functional tremor | Abnormal posturing, or positive variability/distractibility/entrainment findings respectively | Functional diagnosis requires positive examination features, not psychiatric history alone |

### Treat essential tremor according to disability and tolerability

Base essential-tremor care on function: utensils, writing, skilled work, or social participation. Review avoidable triggers and provoking medicines without indiscriminate withdrawal. Adapt grips, utensils, and tasks. Visible tremor need not require medication, while modest tremor can disable precision work.

Propranolol and primidone are commonly used, but the **2026 MDS review found insufficient certainty for confident efficacy conclusions about the studied medical interventions**, including these drugs. Evidence limitations do not establish absence of individual benefit. Discuss uncertainty and compare a meaningful task before and after a monitored trial. [MDS 2026 medical treatment review](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.70184)

Propranolol is a nonselective beta blocker; bronchial asthma, significant bradycardia/conduction disease, and relevant cardiovascular circumstances can make it unsuitable. Review other rate-slowing drugs and hypoglycemia risk. Primidone can cause prominent early dizziness, ataxia, nausea, or sedation; cautious initiation and titration matter, particularly with falls or other sedatives. Its use for tremor differs from its labeled seizure regimens. Topiramate is another selected option but can worsen cognition and has reproductive and other safety concerns discussed in the headache chapter. [Propranolol label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=62ef3d7d-c87f-4d2f-af12-47d50e8b5488), [primidone label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=1c56d09c-4224-4875-bb22-e8bfd595d378)

**Clinical Case.** A 59-year-old stained-glass artist has seven years of bilateral action tremor without bradykinesia, rigidity, or ataxia. Asthma and unsteadiness after a sleep aid complicate propranolol/primidone selection. Review sedation, task adaptations, and any medication trial's functional goal and adverse effects.

Refer persistent disabling tremor despite appropriate trials. Selected botulinum toxin, deep brain stimulation, and focused ultrasound differ in targets, reversibility, weakness, speech, and balance risks. Eligibility depends on distribution, comorbidity, cognition, expectations, and expertise. The 2026 medical-treatment review does not assess all procedures; tremor suppression need not restore full function.

### Establish parkinsonism and look for clues to another syndrome

Parkinsonism requires **bradykinesia plus rigidity or rest tremor**, not tremor alone. Bradykinesia includes slowing and progressive reduction in amplitude or speed during repeated movements. Typical Parkinson disease often begins asymmetrically with reduced arm swing, smaller handwriting, stiffness, a soft voice, or a rest tremor. Nigrostriatal dopamine loss alters basal-ganglia motor signaling; wider neurodegeneration explains why sleep, autonomic, mood, and cognitive symptoms cannot be reduced to a dopamine deficiency alone.

Ask about constipation, reduced smell, dream enactment, mood, orthostasis, sleepiness, and falls; none independently confirms Parkinson disease. Early recurrent falls, vertical gaze impairment, prominent autonomic failure, cerebellar/pyramidal signs, rapid progression, or unusual treatment response suggests alternatives such as progressive supranuclear palsy, multiple system atrophy, vascular parkinsonism, or drug effects.

Arrange specialist clinical assessment when Parkinson disease is suspected, preferably before starting treatment when feasible. MRI is used to investigate selected alternatives, not to confirm ordinary Parkinson disease. Dopamine-transporter imaging may help selected uncertain tremor/parkinsonism presentations, but does not by itself distinguish Parkinson disease from every atypical degenerative parkinsonian disorder. Document the baseline examination so later response and progression can be interpreted. [NICE Parkinson disease assessment framework](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng71/chapter/Recommendations)

### Match Parkinson treatment to the symptom and the person

Treat motor symptoms when they impair function or quality of life rather than withholding useful treatment solely to postpone levodopa exposure. The AAN early-disease framework preferentially recommends levodopa for patients seeking motor-symptom treatment, usually beginning with an immediate-release preparation at the lowest effective dose. Dopamine agonists are selective alternatives rather than a default strategy for an older patient; cognitive impairment, hallucinations, sleepiness, impulse-control problems, and age-related vulnerability are important cautions. Mild symptoms may be managed with a selected MAO-B inhibitor. These treatments are symptomatic; they are not established cures or proven methods of stopping neurodegeneration. [AAN early Parkinson treatment summary](https://www.aan.com/Guidelines/home/GetGuidelineContent/1049)

Levodopa converts to brain dopamine; carbidopa reduces peripheral conversion and adverse effects. Monitor nausea, orthostasis, hallucinations, and mobility. Food/protein can alter response, especially later, but avoid nutrition-compromising protein restriction. Coordinate timing with specialists/dietitians. Formulation changes require a conversion plan, not milligram-for-milligram substitution.

As an immediate-release prescribing example, Sinemet 25/100 mg means 25 mg carbidopa plus 100 mg levodopa per tablet; its labeled usual start is one tablet orally three times daily, individualized to response and tolerability. The current label also calls for vitamin B6 assessment before treatment, periodically and when clinically indicated, with replacement if deficient. New neuropathy, confusion, anemia or seizures deserves assessment, not automatic attribution to Parkinson progression. Do not confuse this preparation with extended-release or infusion dosing. [Current Sinemet label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=9b17b028-964a-473c-823d-81423535bd66)

| Treatment group | Main clinical role | Safety or monitoring priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levodopa/carbidopa | Strong motor-symptom benefit | Nausea, orthostasis, hallucinations, wearing-off, dyskinesia; preserve exact administration times |
| Dopamine agonists | Selected early or adjunctive treatment | Sleep attacks, compulsive behaviors, hallucinations, edema, orthostasis; supervised taper if stopping |
| MAO-B or COMT inhibitors | Selected symptom treatment or prolonging levodopa response | Product-specific interactions and organ restrictions; additional dopaminergic adverse effects |
| Amantadine | Selected management, including troublesome dyskinesia | Renal clearance, confusion/hallucinations, edema and livedo; formulation-specific use |
| Anticholinergic agents | Limited specialist-selected use, not routine older-adult therapy | Cognitive impairment, urinary retention, constipation, visual and heat-related adverse effects |

Ask patients and, with permission, care partners about gambling, shopping, hypersexuality, binge eating, and repetitive behaviors during dopamine-agonist treatment. Sudden sleep episodes endanger driving and work. Disabling adverse effects require specialist-guided adjustment, often gradual reduction; abrupt withdrawal can cause a withdrawal syndrome. [AAN dopamine-agonist counseling](https://www.aan.com/Guidelines/home/GetGuidelineContent/1049)

**Clinical Case.** A 73-year-old develops compulsive online purchases and falls asleep in conversation after a dopamine-agonist increase. Stop driving pending assessment, review cognition and contributors, and contact the specialist for supervised adjustment. Adding sedation without reviewing the agonist can worsen harm.

### Distinguish wearing-off, dyskinesia, and progression

Wearing-off is return of symptoms as a dose effect wanes; dyskinesia is excessive involuntary movement associated with treatment and disease-related changes in response. Ask about exact dose times, meals, constipation, delayed gastric emptying, missed doses, and which activities fail during an off period. A movement diary can distinguish freezing or rigidity before a dose from writhing movements during a good-mobility interval. More medication may improve one problem while worsening another.

Specialists may adjust levodopa timing/formulation, add adjuncts, or select infusion/device treatment. The 2025 MDS review addresses motor fluctuations in patients already taking levodopa, not universal advanced treatment. Deep brain stimulation benefits selected medication-responsive problems or refractory tremor, but does not cure disease or reliably reverse dementia, speech, or all gait problems. Assess cognition and psychiatric status first. [MDS 2025 motor-fluctuation review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40055961/)

Preserve antiparkinsonian dose times across hospital, surgery, rehabilitation, and residential care. Abrupt cessation, missed doses, or impaired absorption can cause severe deterioration, including rigidity/fever syndrome. Inability to swallow requires an urgent alternative administration plan. [NICE medication continuity recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng71/chapter/Recommendations)

Exercise, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech/swallow support are central care, not substitutes offered only after drugs fail. Review orthostatic blood pressure, nutrition, constipation, continence, sleep, depression, aspiration symptoms, falls, and caregiver workload. New hallucinations require assessment for infection, metabolic illness, visual impairment, delirium, and medication effects. If treatment is needed, use a Parkinson-compatible specialist plan; routine dopamine-blocking antipsychotics or metoclopramide can worsen motor function. Do not abruptly remove all dopaminergic therapy to suppress hallucinations.

Pimavanserin is a U.S.-labeled option specifically for hallucinations/delusions associated with Parkinson disease psychosis, not every dementia-related psychosis; its QT, interaction, and elderly mortality warnings still apply. NICE considers low-dose quetiapine in selected patients without cognitive impairment and clozapine when standard treatment is ineffective, with its required safeguards. These options have different evidence and monitoring; haloperidol is not a routine Parkinson hallucination treatment. [Current pimavanserin indication and warnings](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=1e6bea44-57d6-4bac-9328-46e1ee59f83b), [NICE Parkinson psychosis pathway](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng71/chapter/Recommendations)

### Medication-induced movements are different adverse effects

Obtain a complete timeline of prescriptions, nonprescription products, injections, dose increases, and discontinuations. Antipsychotics and dopamine-blocking antiemetics are important exposures; lithium, valproate, antidepressants, stimulants, bronchodilators, and other drugs can cause different movement patterns. A drug effect can also unmask a previously subtle neurodegenerative disorder. Symmetry and timing help but do not conclusively distinguish drug-induced parkinsonism from Parkinson disease. [MDS medication-induced movement overview](https://www.movementdisorders.org/MDS/Resources/Patient-Education/Medication-Induced-Movement-Disorders-Essential-Facts.htm)

| Syndrome | Typical clinical problem | Management distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute dystonia | Painful patterned spasm, torticollis, jaw/eye deviation soon after exposure | Stop/reassess the culprit and give appropriate acute treatment; airway involvement is an emergency |
| Akathisia | Inner restlessness with pacing or inability to remain still | Do not simply increase an antipsychotic for presumed agitation; assess distress and medication timing |
| Drug-induced parkinsonism | New slowness/rigidity, often after dopamine blockade | Coordinate reduction/substitution when possible; monitor for persistent underlying disease |
| Tardive dyskinesia | Persistent orobuccolingual or other involuntary movements after sustained exposure | Not treated like acute dystonia; review specialist VMAT2-inhibitor options and psychiatric stability |

Anticholinergic treatment can relieve selected acute dystonic reactions, but benztropine does not treat tardive dyskinesia and may worsen it. Its cognitive, urinary, bowel, visual, and heat-related effects also matter. Fever, severe rigidity, altered mental state, and autonomic instability after a dopamine-blocking exposure suggest a medical emergency such as neuroleptic malignant syndrome, not an ordinary extrapyramidal effect to manage at the next appointment. [Benztropine safety labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=b03bd28e-3b44-41e4-9a7e-28b9d7975245)

**Clinical Case.** A 66-year-old becomes slow and stiff over several weeks on a dopamine-blocking antiemetic. Review exposure, indication, alternatives, and neurologic findings before adding an anticholinergic that may worsen constipation/cognition. Fever, confusion, and marked rigidity require emergency assessment.

Assess movements during antipsychotic care, using AIMS when indicated. APA recommends reversible VMAT2 inhibition for moderate/severe or disabling tardive dyskinesia, with selected use in consequential milder disease. Review product-specific labeling/interactions and coordinate psychiatric changes; abrupt cessation can worsen psychiatric illness or expose/worsen tardive movements. [APA tardive assessment](https://eguideline.guidelinecentral.com/i/1313488-tardive-dyskinesia/2), [APA treatment recommendation](https://eguideline.guidelinecentral.com/i/1313488-tardive-dyskinesia/3)

### Recognize other movement presentations across ages

Tourette syndrome involves multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic at some point, symptoms persisting for at least a year, onset before age 18, and no better drug/medical explanation. Motor and vocal tics need not occur simultaneously. Coprolalia is not required. Ask about ADHD, OCD, anxiety, pain, learning, bullying, and functional impact. A child should not be punished for an involuntary symptom. [CDC current tic-disorder criteria](https://www.cdc.gov/tourette-syndrome/diagnosis/index.html)

Nonimpairing tics may need education/support alone. Comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics is evidence-based; medication depends on impairment, comorbidities, and adverse effects. Abrupt atypical tic-like symptoms need informed assessment rather than automatic Tourette labeling; functional symptoms are involuntary. [CDC 2026 tic treatment overview](https://www.cdc.gov/tourette-syndrome/treatment/index.html)

Progressive chorea with behavioral or cognitive change and a suggestive family history raises concern for Huntington disease, but chorea also has acquired causes. Huntington disease is related to an autosomal-dominant HTT repeat expansion; juvenile presentations may be more rigid/bradykinetic rather than obviously choreic. Diagnostic assessment and predictive testing are different encounters. Arrange specialist genetic counseling rather than casually testing an unaffected relative; discuss psychological, family, consent, and insurance implications. Symptom-directed care includes mood/safety assessment, nutrition, mobility, and swallowing support. [NINDS Huntington disease resource](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2025-05/huntingtons-disease-hope-through-research.pdf), [HDSA juvenile presentation](https://hdsa.org/what-is-hd/history-and-genetics-of-huntingtons-disease/juvenile-onset-hd/)

### Start cognitive assessment with change from baseline

Investigate reported changes in memory, language, judgment, behavior, navigation, or tasks. Establish onset and steady, stepwise, fluctuating, or abrupt course with collateral history that respects privacy and participation. Social fluency can persist despite serious financial or medication-management impairment.

Distinguish subjective concern, objective cognitive impairment, and interference with independence. Mild cognitive impairment generally preserves independence, although tasks may require more effort or compensatory strategies. Dementia/major neurocognitive disorder interferes with independent daily function because of cognitive decline; it is not defined by one test score or by dependence caused solely by arthritis or blindness. Assess instrumental activities such as finances, medicines, cooking, transportation, and appointments as well as basic self-care. [NIA dementia definitions and assessment overview](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-and-dementia/what-dementia-symptoms-types-and-diagnosis)

Symptomatic evaluation is not the same as universal screening of people without concerns. The USPSTF final statement finds insufficient evidence to determine the balance of routine screening benefits and harms in asymptomatic community-dwelling adults aged 65 or older. That statement is not a reason to dismiss a reported decline or ignore cognitive assessment required in an appropriate clinical encounter. [USPSTF cognitive screening population and scope](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/cognitive-impairment-in-older-adults-screening)

Use a validated assessment suited to language, education, sensory function, and setting; interpret the pattern with the history rather than treating a cutoff as the diagnosis. Hearing/vision impairment, low literacy, language mismatch, fatigue, anxiety, and motor limitations can distort performance. Correct remediable testing barriers. Persisting concern despite a reassuring brief score can justify neuropsychological or specialist assessment, especially with high prior function or a focal language/executive presentation. [DETeCD-ADRD primary-care recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12173843/)

**Clinical Case.** A conversationally fluent 76-year-old pays bills repeatedly and duplicates evening medicines. His daughter suspects poor hearing. Provide hearing-supported testing, but assess cognition, collateral history, mood, medicines, medical contributors, and safety: sensory impairment does not explain away lost independence.

### Investigate contributors and recognize a cognitive syndrome

Review depression, sleep apnea, substances, anticholinergic/sedative burden, pain, nutrition, thyroid/B12 disorders, and systemic illness. Examine neurologic function, gait, senses, vitals, and indicated orthostasis. Depression may coexist with neurodegeneration; treat contributors without promising complete reversibility or dismissing persistent decline after mood improves.

The DETeCD-ADRD framework recommends tiered testing. Its routine cognitive laboratory panel includes CBC, metabolic testing, TSH, B12, homocysteine, ESR, and CRP; clarify what the local chemistry panel includes, rather than assuming magnesium is included. Structural imaging is ordinarily obtained, with MRI preferred and CT when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated. Higher-tier testing is selected for the phenotype and risks, not ordered wholesale. Rapid progression, young onset, seizures, focal signs, unusual behavior/language change, or diagnostic uncertainty accelerates referral. [DETeCD-ADRD tiered testing framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11772716/)

| Syndrome to consider | Common early clues | Interpretation caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alzheimer-type presentation | Gradually progressive new-learning/episodic-memory difficulty | Other presentations occur; a memory complaint does not establish amyloid pathology |
| Vascular cognitive impairment | Executive slowing, vascular injury, gait/focal findings, sometimes stepwise change | Course may also be gradual; mixed pathology is common |
| Lewy body presentation | Fluctuation, recurrent formed visual hallucinations, REM-sleep behavior, spontaneous parkinsonism | Marked antipsychotic sensitivity can create harm |
| Frontotemporal presentation | Early behavior/personality or language change | Memory can be relatively less affected initially; not simply late-life misbehavior |
| Normal-pressure hydrocephalus or another structural process | Gait disorder with cognitive/urinary symptoms and compatible imaging | The symptom triad alone neither confirms the cause nor predicts shunt benefit |

Syndromes suggest, but do not establish, pathology. Imaging may show mass, subdural collection, hydrocephalus, vascular injury, or atrophy; normal imaging does not exclude early neurodegeneration. Refer gait-predominant syndromes before profound dementia, and reassess rapid decline rather than attributing it to established Alzheimer's disease. [NINDS dementia differential](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/dementia)

Dementia with Lewy bodies often combines fluctuations, visual hallucinations, sleep behavior changes, and parkinsonism. The clinical distinction from Parkinson disease dementia depends partly on the timing of cognitive symptoms relative to established motor disease; the one-year convention helps classify the syndrome rather than representing a biological dividing line. Review autonomic symptoms, falls, and medication sensitivity. Do not reflexively use a dopamine-blocking antipsychotic for visual hallucinations without assessing distress, danger, delirium, and safer options. [DLB Consortium diagnostic framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5496518/)

### Explain what biomarkers and treatments can and cannot do

Blood biomarkers, CSF studies, and PET can answer selected etiologic questions when the result changes management. They do not replace history, objective assessment, functional staging, or consideration of another cause. The 2025 Alzheimer's Association blood-biomarker guideline concerns **objectively impaired patients in specialized care**, using tests that meet specified performance standards; it is not permission to diagnose Alzheimer's from any commercial blood result or screen all asymptomatic adults. A positive disease biomarker and the degree of clinical disability are different pieces of information. [Alzheimer's Association 2025 biomarker guidance](https://aaic.alz.org/releases-2025/clinical-practice-guideline-blood-based-biomarkers.asp)

Cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine provide symptomatic treatment for selected dementias; memantine is used in appropriate moderate/severe Alzheimer disease pathways. Selection depends on the diagnosis and stage, not the presence of any memory complaint. They do not restore normal cognition or replace care planning. Assess benefit and tolerability over time rather than continuing indefinitely without a goal. Donepezil can cause gastrointestinal symptoms, anorexia/weight loss, bradycardia or heart block, and syncope; check whether a new fall or low pulse followed initiation or titration. [NICE dementia treatment framework](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations), [donepezil label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8138f616-83f3-4c47-84ef-1bce2db339f7)

For Lewy body dementia, NICE offers donepezil or rivastigmine in mild/moderate disease and considers them in severe disease. This diagnosis-specific pathway includes off-label use where applicable; U.S. indications differ among dementia medicines. [NICE Lewy body treatment recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations/)

Anti-amyloid antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab are distinct from symptomatic drugs. Treatment is initiated in selected patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer disease and confirmed amyloid pathology. They can slow decline in appropriate patients, not reverse established losses or treat every dementia. Counseling includes treatment burden, expected benefit, baseline and serial MRI, genetic risk discussion, and serious edema/hemorrhage risks termed **amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA)**. Current product/formulation-specific protocols govern monitoring; older infusion-number instructions should not be assumed to cover every current regimen. [Current lecanemab label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=9d1ff786-e577-410a-a273-c4d7d0e4e975), [donanemab label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=190352d4-ef62-4679-b4fa-e846e2766afa)

For example, the July 2026 lecanemab label calls for baseline MRI and repeat MRI after **1, 2, 3, and 6 months** of treatment. Donanemab instead specifies baseline MRI and MRI before infusions **2, 3, 4, and 7**. New symptoms may require additional imaging; neither schedule replaces symptom assessment or the product's ARIA interruption rules.

ARIA can cause headache, confusion, visual symptoms, seizures, or focal deficits, although many radiographic events are asymptomatic. A patient receiving anti-amyloid therapy with acute neurologic symptoms needs emergency assessment and explicit communication of that treatment, especially before decisions involving thrombolysis or anticoagulation. APOE epsilon4 homozygosity increases ARIA risk; explain testing implications without implying that genotype alone establishes the clinical diagnosis. Access to reliable MRI and follow-up is part of safe eligibility, not an optional administrative detail.

### Delirium is an acute medical syndrome, including when the patient is quiet

Delirium is acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention/awareness with cognitive change over hours/days, including on preexisting dementia. Hypoactive disease causes withdrawal, slowing, poor intake, or drowsiness. Obtain collateral baseline; quietness or a transiently better interval does not exclude it.

| Feature | Delirium | Established dementia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time course | Acute or subacute change, often fluctuating | Usually chronic progressive decline, with subtype variation |
| Attention/arousal | Commonly impaired or altered | May be relatively preserved early; can be affected later |
| Clinical priority | Find and treat acute cause(s), protect safety | Establish syndrome/etiology, support function, plan longitudinal care |

In the NICE hospital/long-term-care framework, use the **4AT** when delirium indicators are present; critical-care/recovery settings use an appropriate tool such as CAM-ICU or ICDSC. A positive assessment requires clinical interpretation and evaluation of causes. If dementia and delirium cannot readily be separated, address delirium first. These tools aid recognition; they do not identify which precipitant is responsible. [NICE delirium assessment recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg103/chapter/Recommendations)

Evaluate airway/oxygenation, glucose, vital signs, pain, hydration, medication changes/withdrawal, urinary retention, constipation, metabolic disturbance, infection, and neurologic disease as indicated. A new focal deficit, head injury, seizure, meningismus, or rapidly worsening consciousness changes the testing and disposition. Several contributors may coexist. An outpatient who cannot be safely monitored or has a serious possible cause needs acute assessment rather than a prescription for sleep.

Do not label delirium as a urinary infection solely because bacteriuria is found. In an older cognitively impaired person without local urinary symptoms or systemic infection signs, IDSA recommends looking for other causes and observation rather than treating asymptomatic bacteriuria. Hemodynamic instability or systemic concern still requires appropriate investigation and treatment; the rule is not a reason to withhold care for sepsis. [IDSA bacteriuria and delirium guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/asymptomatic-bacteriuria/)

**Clinical Case.** An 82-year-old with mild dementia becomes quiet and inattentive over two days after a sedating medicine. Poor intake, painful urinary retention, and bacteriuria are present. Assess delirium and treat identified contributors rather than assuming dementia progression or urinary infection. Reassess because recovery may lag.

Use reassurance, orientation, familiar support, hearing/vision aids, adequate pain control, hydration/nutrition, mobility, and sleep-preserving care. Minimize unnecessary catheters, restraints, and disruptive transfers. Medication is not the routine treatment for delirium itself. If severe distress or immediate danger persists despite appropriate de-escalation, a short carefully selected regimen may be considered with cardiac, neurologic, and interaction risks assessed. Haloperidol is contraindicated in Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia; benzodiazepines can worsen ordinary delirium and are reserved for a specific indication such as appropriate withdrawal treatment. [MHRA delirium medication safeguards](https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/haloperidol-haldol-reminder-of-risks-when-used-in-elderly-patients-for-the-acute-treatment-of-delirium)

### Preserve mobility, safety, and participation

Assess falls/near-falls, fear, transfers, nighttime toileting, freezing, footwear, vision, home hazards, and medicines. Check indicated orthostasis, gait/balance, and device use. Sedation, hypotension, arrhythmia, syncope, neuropathy, and environmental hazards may be treatable despite Parkinson disease or dementia. [CDC STEADI outpatient assessment resources](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/hcp/clinical-resources/outpatient-care.html)

Exercise interventions are recommended for community-dwelling older adults at increased fall risk; multifactorial intervention is selected according to individual circumstances. Pair strength/balance work and relevant physiotherapy with a medication and home-safety plan. Review fracture prevention in the bone chapter. Avoid prolonged immobility as the default response to fear of falling, since deconditioning can deepen the problem. [USPSTF falls prevention](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/falls-prevention-community-dwelling-older-adults-interventions)

Driving requires attention, judgment, vision, motor control, and reliable wakefulness. Ask about getting lost, unexplained dents, near-misses, traffic citations, pedal errors, and caregiver concern. Neither age nor a single cognitive score establishes fitness. Current dangerous driving or sudden sleep episodes warrants stopping driving while an appropriate assessment is arranged. Use driving-rehabilitation or on-road assessment when indicated, and follow current jurisdiction-specific reporting/licensing requirements. Discuss transportation alternatives early rather than waiting until a crisis leaves the patient isolated. [NHTSA older-driver counseling](https://www.nhtsa.gov/older-drivers/how-understand-and-influence-older-drivers)

**Clinical Case.** A 69-year-old with cognitive decline cites a familiar grocery route as proof of safe driving. His spouse reports two episodes of getting lost and a near collision. Stop driving pending assessment, document concerns and local requirements, and arrange transportation.

Support medicine administration, nutrition, finances, cooking, wandering prevention, and caregiver respite as needs evolve. Decision-making capacity is **decision-specific**, not automatically lost with a dementia label or preserved by a polite conversation. Assess understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and communication of a choice for the decision at hand, with appropriate support. Involve a legally authorized surrogate when required while retaining the patient's voice. Advance planning is most useful before a crisis, but loss of independence does not remove the person's preferences or dignity.

### Follow the course and reopen a diagnosis that stops fitting

Track meaningful outcomes, including meals, off periods, transfers, medicine use, distress, and participation. Assign treatment adjustments, result review, interim help, and follow-up. Urgent precautions include new focal deficits, fever/rigidity with confusion, severe swallowing/breathing difficulty, sudden cognitive change, or unsafe sedation.

Reopen the diagnosis when timing, function, or treatment response changes: essential tremor can coexist with parkinsonism, Parkinson disease with medication toxicity, and dementia with delirium.
