# Medivale Family Medicine

## 11B. Focal and Episodic Symptoms

Assess neurologic symptoms by location, time course, and functional impact. Abrupt language, vision, strength, or coordination loss differs from progressive distal numbness. Transient or apparently small deficits can still be dangerous and disabling. Decide on emergency transfer before refining localization.

Acute stroke care follows AHA/ASA 2026; MS diagnosis uses the 2024 McDonald revisions published in 2025. Reperfusion and disease-modifying treatment require specialist selection; recognizing an emergency must not wait for diagnostic certainty.

### Recognize a vascular event before trying to name the artery

Suspect stroke with abrupt unilateral weakness/numbness, facial asymmetry, language/speech disturbance, visual loss, or severe imbalance. Posterior ischemia may cause diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, or inability to stand despite a reassuring face-arm-speech screen. Dizziness with these findings needs urgent assessment; isolated dizziness requires careful evaluation for vascular and nonvascular causes. [American Stroke Association recognition guidance](https://www.stroke.org/en/about-stroke/stroke-symptoms)

Establish the **last time known neurologically well**, not merely when the deficit was discovered. Record the witness's contact details, symptom progression, baseline function, recent trauma or surgery, bleeding history, anticoagulant use and last dose, and relevant glucose information. Activate emergency transport and the receiving stroke pathway. Check airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, and bedside glucose without delaying transfer. Hypoglycemia can mimic a focal event, but an abnormal glucose does not prove that every accompanying deficit is metabolic. Persistent findings after correction still need urgent evaluation.

Withhold food, drink, and oral medicines until swallowing safety is assessed. Use emergency transport, not patient driving, routine outpatient imaging, or observation after sleep. Seizure, pregnancy/postpartum status, childhood, and absence of conventional risks do not exclude stroke. The emergency team determines hemorrhage versus ischemia, imaging, and treatment eligibility. [AHA/ASA 2026 acute stroke overview](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-for-the-early-management-of-patients-with-acute-ischemic-stroke/top-things-to-know)

| Clinical pattern | Likely localization | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Face/arm-predominant weakness with aphasia or neglect | Cerebral hemisphere, often middle cerebral territory | Aphasia is a language disorder, not simply slurred speech |
| Leg-predominant weakness with frontal behavioral or initiation changes | Medial frontal region, often anterior cerebral territory | Distribution helps localization but does not establish vessel patency |
| Homonymous field loss or visual processing difficulty | Retrogeniculate visual pathways, often posterior cerebral territory | Ask about a field in both eyes, not just the patient's label of one-eye blur |
| Diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, crossed findings, or impaired consciousness | Brainstem/cerebellum and posterior circulation | An apparently modest limb examination can coexist with disabling gait or swallowing impairment |
| Pure motor or sensory deficit without cortical findings | A subcortical pathway is possible | A lacunar-looking syndrome does not replace imaging or etiologic evaluation |

Localization cannot exclude hemorrhage or determine thrombolysis eligibility. Syndromes may evolve; prior stroke, migraine, seizure, or functional symptoms do not exclude a new lesion. Describe disability directly, including inability to name, read, walk independently, or use the dominant hand, even with a low aggregate score.

### Understand what current acute treatment can offer

Noncontrast CT is useful for identifying acute hemorrhage, but an early unrevealing CT does not exclude ischemia. Additional vascular and tissue imaging addresses different questions. Under the 2026 AHA/ASA framework, eligible adults may receive alteplase or tenecteplase within the standard 4.5-hour window. Disabling deficits can justify treatment despite a low NIH Stroke Scale score. Selected unknown-onset or later-presenting patients may qualify through advanced imaging, and thrombectomy eligibility now extends to additional selected large-core infarcts and basilar occlusions. **Wake-up stroke is not synonymous with no treatment opportunity.** [AHA/ASA 2026 acute treatment changes](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-for-the-early-management-of-patients-with-acute-ischemic-stroke/top-things-to-know)

Hospital teams select reperfusion treatment; primary care should expedite transport even outside a familiar window. Avoid improvised aggressive BP reduction or antithrombotics before hemorrhage assessment. Distinguish nondisabling minor deficits from disabling aphasia or gait loss. The acute pathway also manages oxygenation, temperature, glucose, swallowing, and postreperfusion antithrombotic timing.

**Clinical Case.** A 54-year-old interpreter speaks normally at 10:30 PM but cannot form meaningful sentences at 6:15 AM, despite preserved arm strength. Arrange emergency transport with last-known-well and witness information. Disabling wake-up symptoms may still qualify for imaging-selected treatment.

### Resolved deficits still require urgent evaluation

TIA is transient focal ischemic dysfunction without acute infarction; symptom resolution alone cannot establish absence of infarction. Resolved abrupt monocular visual loss, unilateral weakness, or language disturbance warrants urgent assessment because early recurrence risk remains. A normal clinic examination does not establish safety.

Emergency evaluation combines the clinical history with brain imaging, vessel assessment, and cardiac investigation. Diffusion-weighted MRI is more sensitive for acute tissue injury when available; CT with appropriate vascular imaging can rapidly address hemorrhage and important stenosis. ECG, rhythm assessment, and selected extended monitoring seek atrial fibrillation or another embolic source. A low clinical score alone cannot safely exclude dangerous carotid disease or intermittent atrial fibrillation. Disposition must account for findings, specialist input, access to rapid investigations, and the reliability of follow-up, rather than simply counting risk factors. [AHA 2023 TIA scientific statement overview](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/diagnosis-workup-risk-reduction-of-transient-ischemic-attack-in-the-emergency-department-setting/top-things-to-know)

Aura commonly spreads over minutes; ischemic loss often starts abruptly. Seizure may cause positive movements followed by transient weakness. These distinctions are not absolute. Ask witnesses about lost function, simultaneous versus sequential onset, and component duration. First unusual events, persistent deficits, or uncertainty require acute assessment despite later normalization.

### Secondary prevention follows the cause, not the diagnostic label alone

Review discharge diagnosis, vascular/rhythm studies, medication changes, and pending investigations after stroke/TIA. Prevention depends on mechanism. Recurrence on aspirin requires checking adherence, affordability, adverse effects, swallowing, and competing causes, not automatically adding indefinite antithrombotics.

| Mechanism or finding | Prevention direction | Avoid the shortcut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noncardioembolic ischemic event | Antiplatelet-based prevention; selected early minor stroke/high-risk TIA may receive a short dual course | Long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel is not routine for every stroke |
| Atrial fibrillation with ischemic stroke/TIA | Anticoagulation when appropriate, with timing individualized to the acute event | Aspirin is not an equivalent replacement; adding aspirin also increases bleeding |
| Symptomatic extracranial carotid stenosis | Prompt specialist assessment for selected intervention plus medical prevention | An incidental bruit alone does not establish an operative indication |
| Severe symptomatic intracranial atherosclerosis | Intensive medical management and a defined specialist antithrombotic plan | Stenting is not the routine first preventive step |
| Cryptogenic event or embolic stroke of undetermined source | Complete mechanism evaluation, including selected rhythm monitoring | Do not prescribe empiric anticoagulation solely because an embolic source is suspected |

An important short-course group is noncardioembolic minor stroke with NIHSS at most 3 or high-risk TIA with ABCD2 at least 4, without IV thrombolysis: the acute pathway starts aspirin-clopidogrel early, usually within 24 hours, for **21 days**, then uses one antiplatelet. These thresholds select treatment populations, not patients safe for discharge. Selected atherosclerotic or intracranial disease follows different timing/protocols. Record the stop date. Anticoagulant-plus-antiplatelet therapy is not the default without another indication; PFO closure likewise requires a careful etiologic assessment, not merely finding a shunt. [AHA/ASA secondary prevention scope](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2021-guideline-for-the-prevention-of-stroke-in-patients-with-stroke-and-transient-ischemic-attack/top-things-to-know), [2026 acute stroke guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/STR.0000000000000513)

For a recent TIA or nondisabling stroke with **ipsilateral extracranial carotid stenosis of 70%-99%**, AHA/ASA recommends carotid endarterectomy when estimated perioperative morbidity/mortality is below 6%; with 50%-69%, benefit depends on age, sex, comorbidities, and procedural risk. Revascularization is not recommended for stenosis below 50%. When indicated, intervention is generally considered within two weeks, with timing selected by the stroke/vascular team rather than automatic emergency surgery. Continue intensive medical prevention regardless of intervention. These symptomatic criteria are not an asymptomatic-bruit screening rule. [AHA/ASA carotid recommendations](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/STR.0000000000000375)

Address BP, lipids, diabetes, tobacco, activity, diet, and indicated sleep-disordered-breathing care. For most neurologically stable patients, target BP below 130/80 mm Hg as tolerated; this is not an acute reperfusion protocol. Follow the cardiovascular chapter's risk-tiered lipid pathway and individualize glycemic goals to function, comorbidity, and hypoglycemia risk. Support home monitoring and medication organization. [AHA 2025 blood-pressure framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know)

**Clinical Case.** A 71-year-old taking aspirin has eight minutes of monocular curtain-like visual loss, now resolved, without weakness. Arrange emergency assessment for carotid or another embolic disease before choosing prevention; current normal vision does not justify routine follow-up or empiric antiplatelet addition.

Review swallowing/nutrition, mobility/falls, communication, mood/cognition, driving/work, and caregiver strain. Reconcile home medicines and assign pending rhythm results and rehabilitation referrals. Dysphagia, aspiration, deterioration, or recurrent deficits require reassessment. Adapt administration and refill support to the patient's ability.

### A first seizure needs an event history and a search for cause

Describe what happened before, during, and after the event. Obtain a witness account or safely obtained video when available. Ask about warning symptoms, position, exertion, palpitations, color change, eye/head deviation, the sequence of motor activity, responsiveness, duration, injury, and recovery. Ask about fever, sleep loss, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, recreational substances, new medicines, missed antiseizure doses, pregnancy, and previous subtle episodes. An unexplained nocturnal injury or recurrent brief impaired-awareness spells may mean the reported event was not truly the first.

Acute symptomatic seizures occur in relation to an acute insult such as a major metabolic disturbance, intoxication/withdrawal, infection, or acute brain injury. They are not automatically equivalent to an enduring epileptic disorder. Conversely, epilepsy need not require two witnessed convulsions: the ILAE definition includes two unprovoked/reflex seizures more than 24 hours apart, one unprovoked seizure with at least a 60% recurrence probability over the next ten years, or an established epilepsy syndrome. Diagnosis and the decision to start medication are related but distinct decisions. [ILAE practical definition](https://www.ilae.org/guidelines/definition-and-classification/the-2014-definition-of-epilepsy-a-perspective-for-patients-and-caregivers)

The 2025 ILAE classification separates focal, generalized, unknown whether focal or generalized, and unclassified seizures. Focal events may preserve or impair consciousness and progress to bilateral tonic-clonic activity. Establish the earliest symptom: bilateral shaking alone cannot prove generalized onset. Prefer precise current terminology to "grand mal" or "complex partial" alone. [ILAE 2025 seizure classification](https://www.ilae.org/files/dmfile/updated-classification-of-epileptic-seizures-2025.pdf)

| Event possibility | History that helps | Why one feature is insufficient |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epileptic seizure | Stereotyped onset, focal features, prolonged confusion, or a characteristic sequence | No single injury, movement, or episode of incontinence proves epilepsy |
| Syncope, including cardiac causes | Trigger/prodrome, posture, palpitations, brief loss of consciousness, recovery pattern | Brief jerking can accompany reduced cerebral perfusion |
| Functional seizure | Positive clinical assessment, ideally recording typical events with appropriate expertise | Stress, psychiatric history, or a normal interictal EEG does not establish the diagnosis |

Check glucose and perform a **12-lead ECG** after a first suspected seizure, alongside targeted metabolic, toxicologic, infectious, and pregnancy assessment when relevant. A cardiac arrhythmia can present as collapse with convulsive movements. Neuroimaging urgency depends on trauma, persistent altered state, focal findings, immune impairment, cancer, infection concern, and other features; subsequent epilepsy-protocol MRI can identify structural pathology missed by routine imaging. EEG supports classification and recurrence assessment, but **a normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy**. NICE recommends rapid specialist assessment after a first suspected seizure and, when requested, early EEG rather than an indefinite routine queue. [NICE NG217 diagnosis and assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng217/chapter/1-Diagnosis-and-assessment-of-epilepsy)

In that NICE pathway, arrange first-suspected-seizure specialist assessment within two weeks; if EEG is requested, obtain it as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours. These are service targets for a stable patient, not permission to delay emergency imaging or admission for persistent altered awareness, focal deficits, injury, or infection concern. [NICE first-seizure timing](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng217/chapter/diagnosis-and-assessment-of-epilepsy)

**Clinical Case.** A 26-year-old collapses while standing in a hot room, with pallor, brief irregular jerks, and prompt coherent speech when supine. Syncope is likely, but assess cardiac causes with ECG and history. Exertional collapse, palpitations, sudden-death family history, or ECG abnormalities increase urgency.

### Stop ongoing convulsions and protect the person

Time the event, protect the head, clear hazards, and position the person safely on the side when feasible. Do not restrain the limbs, place objects in the mouth, or give oral fluid or medicine before adequate alertness returns. Seek emergency help for a first seizure, breathing difficulty, significant injury, a seizure in water, pregnancy, or repeated events. Ongoing convulsive activity reaching **five minutes**, or repeated seizures without recovery, requires the emergency status epilepticus pathway; waiting for a much longer historical definition is unsafe. [CDC seizure first aid](https://www.cdc.gov/epilepsy/first-aid-for-seizures/index.html)

Emergency treatment includes airway/ventilatory support, glucose assessment, prompt appropriate benzodiazepine, and protocol-directed escalation if seizures continue. Individual rescue plans specify drug, route, dose, repeats, and emergency triggers. Persistent unexplained confusion may reflect nonconvulsive seizures or another acute illness and require urgent EEG, rather than being assumed postictal. [AES prolonged convulsive seizure treatment framework](https://cms.aesnet.org/clinical-care/clinical-guidance/guideline-prolonged-seizures/guideline-for-treatment-of-prolonged-seizures-in-children-and-adults)

For trained emergency teams, AES initial options include IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg, maximum 4 mg per dose, which may be repeated once, or IM midazolam 10 mg in a patient above 40 kg when IV access is absent. Monitor ventilation and count treatment already given before arrival. Persistent convulsions require prompt second-line antiseizure treatment and escalation; do not keep repeating small benzodiazepine doses indefinitely. These emergency doses are not instructions for unsupervised home use or a substitute for the patient's prescribed rescue product. [AES convulsive-status algorithm](https://cms.aesnet.org/docs/default-source/press-releases-assets/cse-treatment-chart-final_rerelease.pdf)

### Decide about recurrence prevention through shared assessment

After a first adult unprovoked seizure, prior brain injury, epileptiform EEG, significant imaging abnormalities, and nocturnal onset increase recurrence concern. Immediate antiseizure therapy reduces short-term recurrence without established improvement in everyone's long-term sustained remission. Discuss recurrence consequences, adverse effects, occupation, preferences, and test findings; this evidence framework does not settle every modern drug choice. [AAN/AES first unprovoked seizure clinician summary](https://www.aan.com/Guidelines/Home/GetGuidelineContent/688)

Provide written precautions: no driving until jurisdiction-specific legal and clinical requirements are met; avoid unsupervised swimming/baths and hazards involving heights, heat, machinery, or flames. Teach first aid and review adherence, sleep, substances, interactions, contraception, and pregnancy intentions. Do not abruptly stop effective therapy in pregnancy. The 2024 AAN/AES/SMFM framework balances convulsive-seizure prevention with lower fetal-risk options through neurology/obstetric care. [Current epilepsy and pregnancy guideline announcement](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/5170)

Functional seizures are real, involuntary events, not proof of deception. Appropriate diagnostic expertise, often video-EEG of typical episodes, helps avoid both unnecessary antiseizure treatment and missed coexisting epilepsy. Explain the diagnosis respectfully and maintain care while arranging indicated psychological/rehabilitative treatment. Antiseizure medicines and benzodiazepines are not treatments for functional seizures alone, although a separate indication may exist. Withdrawal of unnecessary therapy should be supervised. [AAN 2025 functional seizure clinician guideline](https://aanfiles.blob.core.windows.net/guidelines/2cceae40-edcf-484d-9054-bbdf76206bca/25_FunctionalSeizure-ClinGL_v03b.pdf)

### Localize weakness and sensory symptoms before ordering a panel

Clarify whether "weakness" means loss of force, pain-limited movement, fatigue, breathlessness, or imbalance. Compare proximal and distal strength, both sides, tone, reflexes, plantar responses, sensation, coordination, and gait. Ask about sphincter changes, saddle symptoms, a sensory level, ptosis/diplopia, swallowing, respiratory symptoms, and speed of progression. Sudden focal weakness follows the vascular emergency pathway. Rapid symmetric progression, bulbar symptoms, respiratory impairment, or signs of spinal cord/cauda equina compromise require urgent escalation rather than a routine neuropathy workup. [NICE NG127 adult neurologic referral recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng127/chapter/Recommendations-for-adults-aged-over-16)

| Examination pattern | Localization to consider | Useful next distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weakness with aphasia, neglect, or a visual field deficit | Brain | Abrupt onset is a vascular warning |
| Brisk reflexes below a level, gait change, sensory level, bladder symptoms | Spinal cord | Look for compression or inflammatory myelopathy; urgency follows progression and deficits |
| Dermatomal pain/sensory change with myotomal weakness and reflex change | Nerve root | Distinguish a single root from cord disease or several peripheral nerves |
| Distal nerve-territory loss or symmetric stocking sensory loss with reduced ankle reflexes | Peripheral nerve/polyneuropathy | Asymmetry, rapid change, or prominent motor loss is atypical for ordinary distal diabetic neuropathy |
| Fatigable ocular/bulbar weakness with preserved sensation, or fixed proximal weakness without sensory loss | Neuromuscular junction or muscle | Fatigability suggests junctional disease; proximal weakness also requires muscle/endocrine/drug assessment |

Functional motor symptoms require a **positive clinical diagnosis**, such as a characteristic Hoover sign or tremor entrainment assessed by an experienced clinician, not simply normal imaging or a history of stress. Symptoms are involuntary and can coexist with neurologic disease. Explain the demonstrated findings, arrange symptom-directed rehabilitation and appropriate psychological care, and reassess new objective changes. A functional formulation is not permission to bypass the emergency pathway for a new sudden deficit. [NHS functional-neurologic assessment pathway](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/neurology-pathways-including-headache/functional-neurological-disorder-fnd/)

### Distal polyneuropathy is a pattern, not an explanation

Length-dependent polyneuropathy often begins in the toes and progresses proximally before substantially involving the hands. Ask about burning pain, numbness, impaired position sense, falls, autonomic symptoms, alcohol, nutrition, chemotherapy or other neurotoxic exposure, renal disease, family history, and systemic illness. Examine skin, pulses, footwear, deformity, ulcers, and protective sensation. Pain may be absent despite substantial injury risk.

Higher-yield tests for unexplained distal symmetric polyneuropathy include glucose, B12 with metabolites such as methylmalonic acid when needed, and serum protein immunofixation. Select CBC, renal/hepatic chemistry, thyroid tests, and additional studies from the phenotype and risks rather than ordering indiscriminate toxin, infection, or antibody panels. [AAN/AANEM laboratory evidence review](https://www.aanem.org/docs/default-source/documents/dsp_lab_testing.pdf?sfvrsn=7ca979db_1)

Diabetes does not exclude another neuropathy. Chronic metformin exposure, nutritional deficiency, alcohol, renal disease, paraproteinemia, thyroid disease, and inflammatory neuropathies may change management. The ADA 2026 framework specifically reserves neurologic/electrophysiologic evaluation for unclear or atypical presentations, including acute/subacute onset, asymmetry, non-length-dependent findings, or prominent motor involvement. EMG/nerve-conduction studies can help localize and characterize large-fiber disease; a normal study does not exclude a small-fiber process. Routine spine MRI is not a substitute for a localization-based examination. [ADA 2026 neuropathy and foot care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690177/), [NINDS peripheral neuropathy overview](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/peripheral-neuropathy)

**Clinical Case.** A 63-year-old taking metformin has foot numbness, falls, reduced vibration, brisk knees, impaired tandem gait, and hand clumsiness. Assess possible cord disease and nutritional causes, including B12, rather than assuming uncomplicated diabetic neuropathy or merely escalating pain treatment.

Treat the cause and distinguish pain relief from nerve recovery. Choose neuropathic-pain treatment with renal function, sedation, falls, edema, mood, interactions, and goals in mind. New weakness requires reassessment. Preserve daily foot inspection, footwear, ulcer prevention, and balance care despite pain relief; assess infection or a hot swollen foot promptly. [NIDDK peripheral neuropathy care](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/preventing-problems/nerve-damage-diabetic-neuropathies/peripheral-neuropathy)

### Recognize rapidly progressive and bulbar weakness

Guillain-Barre syndrome often follows infection and can cause rapidly progressive bilateral weakness, reduced reflexes, autonomic instability, and respiratory failure. Sensory symptoms or pain may precede obvious weakness. Early CSF protein can be normal; do not delay admission until a classic laboratory pattern appears. Hospital assessment tracks respiratory mechanics and autonomic status, not oxygen saturation alone. Appropriate patients receive IV immunoglobulin or plasma exchange; corticosteroids are not routine disease-modifying treatment. [EAN/PNS 2023 Guillain-Barre guideline](https://www.uems-neuroboard.org/web/images/docs/exam/2026/4-EAN--Guideline-GuillainBarre-syndrome2023.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A 38-year-old develops progressive weakness ten days after diarrhea: difficulty rising, then inability to climb stairs the next morning, with reduced reflexes. Normal speech and oxygen saturation do not exclude neuromuscular respiratory failure. Arrange emergency hospital assessment.

Myasthenia gravis can produce fluctuating ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, chewing fatigue, or dysphagia with preserved sensation. Fatigability matters more than a complaint of generalized tiredness. New respiratory or bulbar compromise is an emergency, and infection or medication changes may precipitate deterioration. Medication reconciliation and specialist planning are essential; do not assume every breathless patient with normal lung auscultation has anxiety. [MedlinePlus myasthenia gravis](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000712.htm)

For a stable suspected myasthenic syndrome, specialist evaluation uses AChR antibodies, selected MuSK/other antibody testing, and neuromuscular electrophysiology; negative initial serology does not end the assessment. Chest imaging evaluates thymoma after the clinical diagnosis is pursued. Do not defer respiratory assessment until antibody results return. [MGFA diagnostic assessment](https://myasthenia.org/understanding-mg/diagnosing-mg/)

Progressive weakness with muscle wasting, fasciculations, and upper motor neuron findings raises concern for motor neuron disease, although several mimics exist. Progressive bulbar symptoms or respiratory decline should accelerate referral and supportive assessment. Sensory sparing can be a clue, not an absolute diagnostic test. Ask about swallowing, weight loss, communication, nocturnal breathing symptoms, and caregiver needs before severe visible disability develops. [NICE motor neuron disease assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG42/chapter/Recommendations)

### Facial weakness requires a cause and an eye-protection plan

Bell palsy is an acute peripheral facial neuropathy diagnosed after assessing alternative causes. Weakness generally involves the upper and lower face on one side, including impaired eye closure. Forehead sparing can suggest a supranuclear lesion, but complete ipsilateral facial weakness does **not** exclude a brainstem stroke when other findings are present. Diplopia, limb deficits, ataxia, sensory abnormalities, or impaired consciousness require an appropriate central assessment. Examine the ears, oral cavity, skin, other cranial nerves, and limbs.

For typical new Bell palsy in patients aged at least 16, the AAO-HNSF guideline recommends oral corticosteroids within **72 hours** when appropriate. Antiviral monotherapy is not recommended; adding an antiviral to steroids is an option rather than an obligatory treatment for every case. Protect an eye that does not close fully with lubrication and an appropriate closure/protection plan. Eye pain, redness, or visual change warrants urgent assessment. Routine laboratory testing or imaging is not needed for a straightforward presentation, but atypical progression or recurrence changes that judgment. [AAO-HNSF Bell palsy guideline](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1177/0194599813505967)

One trial-supported adult regimen is prednisolone 25 mg orally twice daily for ten days, started within that early window; assess glucose, infection and other steroid risks. Use lubricating drops while awake and ointment plus a properly demonstrated eyelid-closure strategy overnight when closure is incomplete. A patch that leaves the cornea exposed is not adequate protection. New ocular symptoms need urgent eye assessment even while facial strength is improving. [AAN steroid evidence](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/wnl.0b013e318275978c), [AAO-HNSF eye-protection recommendation](https://www.entnet.org/quality-practice/quality-products/clinical-practice-guidelines/bells-palsy/)

| Finding | Alternative concern | Practical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Severe ear pain, vesicles, hearing or vestibular symptoms | Varicella-zoster facial neuropathy/Ramsay Hunt syndrome | Reassess diagnosis and arrange directed treatment |
| Bilateral facial weakness, exposure history, rash, meningitic or radicular symptoms | Lyme neuroborreliosis or another systemic process | Perform exposure-informed evaluation rather than assuming idiopathic palsy |
| Gradual progression, recurrent same-side weakness, mass, or additional neurologic findings | Structural or other secondary cause | Targeted imaging/referral rather than repeated empiric courses |

In suspected Lyme-associated facial palsy, evaluate and treat the infection through the appropriate pathway. The IDSA/AAN/ACR guideline makes **no recommendation** for or against adjunctive steroids in established Lyme facial palsy because evidence is insufficient. That knowledge gap is not a universal prohibition against timely Bell palsy steroids in an adult without other objective Lyme evidence while the assessment proceeds. [IDSA/AAN/ACR Lyme guideline](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/lyme-disease/)

**Clinical Case.** A 47-year-old has 30 hours of isolated unilateral facial weakness with incomplete eye closure and no ear vesicles. Assess early steroid eligibility and demonstrate overnight eye protection. Return urgently for ocular or new neurologic symptoms; reassess/refer incomplete recovery by three months. Individualize treatment in pregnancy, diabetes, and younger children. [AAO-HNSF follow-up framework](https://www.entnet.org/quality-practice/quality-products/clinical-practice-guidelines/bells-palsy/)

### Multiple sclerosis: recognize a central syndrome without overcalling MRI findings

MS is an immune-mediated central nervous system disorder with inflammatory demyelination and neuroaxonal injury. Compatible presentations include optic neuritis, partial myelitis, and brainstem syndromes such as internuclear ophthalmoplegia. Painful monocular visual loss with impaired color perception, a sensory level, brisk reflexes, or a persistent central ocular-motor abnormality is more informative than nonspecific fatigue alone. New severe vision loss, cord dysfunction, or rapidly worsening deficits needs urgent assessment. Vascular disease, compression, B12 deficiency, infection, NMOSD, MOG-associated disease, and other mimics require consideration.

The **2024 McDonald revisions, published in 2025**, add the optic nerve as a fifth diagnostic location and incorporate additional selected imaging and CSF evidence. Dissemination in space involves characteristic lesions in at least two of five locations. Demonstration of dissemination in time remains useful but is not mandatory in every permitted diagnostic pathway. Oligoclonal bands or CSF kappa free-light chains and selected imaging biomarkers can support specified pathways. A specialist applies the complete criteria with **no better explanation**; a few nonspecific white-matter spots do not automatically establish MS. Some selected radiologically isolated presentations may meet current criteria, so requiring two clinical attacks in every patient is also inaccurate. [Primary 2024 criteria manuscript](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10211287/1/Thompson_FINAL_Pre_Edits_07162025.pdf), [ECTRIMS current criteria overview](https://ectrims.eu/mcdonald-diagnostic-criteria)

**Clinical Case.** A 32-year-old develops ascending numbness, urinary urgency, leg stiffness, brisk reflexes, and a sensory level over several days. Urgently assess the cord, excluding compression. If demyelination is found, specialist evaluation distinguishes MS from other inflammatory disorders before long-term treatment.

### Separate relapse treatment from long-term disease modification

In established MS, a relapse involves new or worsening neurologic symptoms lasting more than 24 hours, after at least one month of stability and without infection or another explanation. Heat, fever, or infection can temporarily worsen prior deficits without a new inflammatory lesion. Review urinary and other infection symptoms before attributing deterioration to relapse or giving steroids. Do not force a potentially new stroke, cord compression, or other emergency into the patient's existing MS diagnosis.

Relapses affecting function may warrant specialist-directed high-dose corticosteroids; not every fluctuation requires treatment. Steroids can speed recovery from an inflammatory episode but are not the long-term disease-modifying strategy. Disease-modifying therapies aim to reduce future disease activity and disability risk and have agent-specific infection, vaccination, monitoring, reproductive, and access considerations. Coordinate these plans rather than treating a drug name on the problem list as evidence that monitoring is complete. [NICE NG220 management and relapse recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng220/chapter/Recommendations)

NICE's specialist-discussed relapse regimen is oral methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for five days; IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for three to five days is an alternative for severe admitted relapse or failed/intolerable oral treatment. These are high-dose relapse courses, not a low-dose dose-pack or a standing supply for future self-treatment. Review infection, glucose and psychiatric risks, and notify the MS team because a relapse can change the longer-term plan. [NICE relapse treatment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng220/chapter/Recommendations)

Continue prevention, mood/sleep assessment, medication reconciliation, and care for pain, bowel/bladder symptoms, mobility, sexual health, fatigue contributors, and caregiver needs. Rehabilitation and accommodations support persistent deficits. Assign relapse contacts, emergency precautions, and laboratory monitoring; avoid standing self-directed steroid supplies.

### Close the encounter with an actionable handoff

Emergency handoffs need the deficit, onset/last-known-well, progression, medicines, and escalation reason. Outpatient plans need localization, alternatives, tests, result ownership, and follow-up. Urgent precautions include new weakness or speech/vision loss, inability to walk, swallowing/breathing difficulty, recurrent prolonged convulsions, or neurologic bladder dysfunction.

Reassess when the course changes: new asymmetric motor loss, eye pain during facial palsy, or recurrent vascular symptoms require renewed evaluation rather than continuation of the previous plan.
