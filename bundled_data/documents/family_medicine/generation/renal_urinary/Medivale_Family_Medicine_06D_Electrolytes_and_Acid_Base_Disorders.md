# Medivale Family Medicine

## 06D. Electrolytes and Acid-Base Disorders

Electrolytes reflect changing physiology. Sodium tracks water relative to exchangeable solute; potassium governs membrane excitability; calcium and magnesium affect muscle, nerve and cardiac function. Acid-base results expose disturbances of ventilation, perfusion, metabolism or renal handling. Recognize danger first, then identify the mechanism and avoid treatment-induced harm.

The adult pathways below use the European diagnostic framework for hypotonic hyponatremia, Society for Endocrinology acute sodium guidance, and UK Kidney Association acute hyperkalemia guidance. Local emergency protocols govern actual preparation, administration, monitoring, and escalation. Children, pregnancy, dialysis, and unstable patients need population-specific adaptations. Endocrine causes are developed in 04E, nutrition and refeeding in 04D, and kidney disease in 06C. [ESE hyponatremia guideline and correction](https://www.ese-hormones.org/publications/directory/ese-clinical-guideline-for-the-management-of-hyponatraemia/), [UKKA final adult hyperkalemia guideline](https://www.ukkidney.org/sites/default/files/FINAL%20VERSION%20-%20UKKA%20CLINICAL%20PRACTICE%20GUIDELINE%20-%20MANAGEMENT%20OF%20HYPERKALAEMIA%20IN%20ADULTS%20-%20191223.pdf)

### Begin with the patient, trajectory, and specimen

Seizures, impaired consciousness, threatening arrhythmia, severe weakness, shock, respiratory failure or rapidly changing results warrant emergency assessment. Obtain vital signs, repeat blood tests and an ECG for possible potassium or symptomatic calcium disturbance without delaying stabilization. Comfortable appearance does not exclude dangerous hyperkalemia; check possible specimen artifact while assessing risk.

Compare dated prior values. Ask about intake, thirst, vomiting, diarrhea, urine, weight change, sweating, drugs, supplements, laxatives and IV fluids. Assess perfusion, pressure, orthostasis when safe, lungs, JVP, edema and neurologic status. Heart failure and cirrhosis can cause excess interstitial fluid despite reduced effective arterial filling and ongoing renal retention. [AGA volume and sodium assessment in cirrhosis](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/management-of-ascites-volume-overload-and-hyponatremia-in-cirrhosis/)

Confirm units and collection conditions. Sodium and potassium in mmol/L are numerically equivalent to mEq/L because these ions are monovalent; this shortcut does not apply to every electrolyte. A chemistry panel's total carbon dioxide usually approximates bicarbonate, whereas a blood-gas bicarbonate is generally calculated. Unexpected disagreement calls for attention to timing, specimen handling, and the patient's condition. Record treatments already given before comparing blood and urine values.

### Low sodium: establish tonicity before assigning a cause

Hyponatremia generally means serum sodium below 135 mmol/L, but its urgency depends on symptoms, depth, speed, and cause. Water moves into cells during true hypotonic hyponatremia; rapid brain swelling can be life-threatening. With prolonged disease the brain adapts, making an excessive treatment-related rise hazardous. When duration is unknown, do not assume the patient has had only an acute fall because symptoms appeared today.

Measure serum osmolality and glucose. **Hyperglycemia causes hypertonic, translocational hyponatremia**: extracellular glucose draws water out of cells and lowers measured sodium. Mannitol can act similarly. This is different from **pseudohyponatremia**, an assay artifact associated with markedly increased lipid or protein when an indirect ion-selective method is used. A direct method can clarify that artifact. Neither situation should be treated as ordinary hypotonic hyponatremia merely because the sodium is low. Urea contributes to measured osmolality but is not an equivalent sustained driver of transcellular tonicity. [European guideline: nonhypotonic sodium and diagnostic pathway](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/170/3/G1/6668028)

In confirmed hypotonic disease, obtain paired blood and spot urine samples when feasible before treatment. The European framework starts with urine osmolality: **100 mOsm/kg or less** suggests appropriately dilute urine and water intake exceeding the capacity allowed by solute intake. Above 100, interpret urine sodium; **30 mmol/L or less** supports reduced effective arterial volume. Higher urine sodium requires assessment of diuretics, adrenal disease, kidney function, and other causes. These are working branches, not infallible labels. CKD and recent diuretics can make the usual urine relationships unreliable.

| Clinical situation | Mechanism to consider | Decision that follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gastrointestinal losses, poor perfusion, orthostatic symptoms | Sodium and water loss with water-retaining signals | Evaluate circulation and replace the actual deficit with reassessment |
| Heart failure or cirrhosis with edema | Low effective arterial filling despite excess total-body fluid | Treat the underlying congestion/circulatory disorder; do not give saline solely for low sodium |
| Apparently euvolemic patient with concentrated urine | Inappropriate antidiuresis, adrenal deficiency, medication effect, or other impaired water excretion | Confirm the exclusion criteria before diagnosing SIAD |
| Very high water intake or very low food/solute intake | Water delivery exceeds excretory capacity | Assess nutrition and intake; correction can accelerate when solute is restored |

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD or SIADH) requires exclusion of adrenal deficiency, clinically relevant thyroid disease, renal dysfunction, drugs and volume abnormalities. Pulmonary/CNS disease, malignancy, pain, nausea and medicines may promote antidiuresis. Do not attribute severe sodium disturbance to mild isolated hypothyroidism without further assessment. Suspected adrenal crisis requires emergency treatment; see 04E.

### Separate emergency sodium rescue from subsequent correction

Severe symptoms plausibly attributable to hypotonic hyponatremia require monitored hypertonic saline rather than fluid restriction alone. The Society for Endocrinology adult emergency pathway uses **150 mL of 3% saline over 20 minutes**, reassessing sodium and symptoms and repeating according to its protocol to obtain an initial rise of about **5 mmol/L**. This is limited rescue, not immediate normalization. Its general correction ceilings are **10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 8 mmol/L in each subsequent 24 hours**. Count the initial rescue rise within the total. [Society for Endocrinology adult emergency sodium pathway](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/xhrhxhxm/emergency-management-of-severe-and-moderately-severely-symptomatic-hyponatraemia-in-adult-patients-2022.pdf)

An important high-risk refinement comes from the U.S./Irish expert-panel approach, summarized by its authors: with very low sodium, malnutrition, alcohol use disorder, hypokalemia, or advanced liver disease, aim for only **4-6 mmol/L per day and do not exceed 8 mmol/L in any 24 hours**. This reduces the risk of **osmotic demyelination**, a potentially devastating neurologic injury after excessive correction. Acute rescue for seizures must still occur. During active treatment, check sodium frequently and follow urine output; a sudden water diuresis can cause overshoot even after saline stops. Expert-supervised desmopressin and electrolyte-free water may be needed to prevent or reverse excessive correction. [Authored expert-panel discussion of correction safeguards](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10843202/)

Potassium replacement, restored perfusion, thiazide withdrawal, cortisol treatment or resumed nutrition can accelerate sodium correction unpredictably. Monitor active correction and rising urine output without waiting for routine morning testing. New neurologic deterioration after initial recovery requires urgent reassessment. Track cumulative correction across transfers and calendar days.

After stabilization, treat the cause: replace true volume deficits, remove SIAD triggers and tailor fluids. Persistent disease needs specialist assessment before vaptans, salt tablets or fluid restriction; restriction may harm depleted patients. Explain fluid limits, repeat testing and emergency symptoms.

**Clinical Case: cumulative sodium correction.** A 72-year-old recently started on a thiazide has eaten little during respiratory illness. Sodium is 118 mmol/L with increasing drowsiness. Arrange urgent assessment of symptoms, volume, drugs, glucose, osmolality and adrenal context. Rescue from 118 to 123 is already a **5 mmol/L** rise; subsequent aquaresis and potassium replacement add to that total.

### High sodium: distinguish lack of water from ongoing renal loss

Hypernatremia, generally above 145 mmol/L, reflects water deficiency relative to sodium/potassium, sometimes with sodium gain. Assess thirst, water access, swallowing and communication. Frailty, cognitive impairment, fever, caregiver dependence and tube feeding may impair access. Investigate GI/skin losses, osmotic diuresis and renal concentration.

Measure urine volume and interpret urine osmolality alongside serum findings. Large volumes of unexpectedly dilute urine raise concern for **arginine vasopressin deficiency or resistance**, previously termed central or nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Lithium, hypokalemia, and hypercalcemia are relevant causes of renal resistance. Hyperglycemia can instead cause a solute-driven diuresis. Frequency with small voids is not the same as polyuria. Supervised diagnostic testing is distinct from asking an already dehydrated, hypernatremic person to stop drinking at home. [NIDDK water-balance disorders](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/diabetes-insipidus)

Restore circulation first if there is hemodynamic compromise, then replace water in a controlled plan that includes ongoing losses. Enteral water may be suitable when safe; intravenous fluid choice depends on circulation, electrolyte needs, and access. For established vasopressin deficiency, omission of desmopressin can cause dangerous dehydration, while uncoordinated replacement of both desmopressin and water can swing sodium the other way. The Society for Endocrinology inpatient guidance emphasizes coordinated fluid, sodium, urine-output, and desmopressin monitoring. Do not extrapolate a particular adult correction approach to children. [Society for Endocrinology inpatient cranial diabetes insipidus guidance](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/scplexuf/guidelines-for-the-inpatient-management-of-cranial-diabetes-insipidus.pdf)

In that **adult inpatient vasopressin-deficiency pathway**, no or mild symptoms call for a sodium fall no faster than **0.5 mmol/L/hour and no more than 10 mmol/L/24 hours**. Check sodium every four hours during fluid resuscitation, then at least every 12 hours until clinically and biochemically stable. Acute symptomatic disease requires an endocrine-led rescue plan. These limits are a named pathway for known disease, not a validated universal rule for every cause of adult hypernatremia or for children. [Society guidance, fluid replacement and correction recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6013691/)

The free-water-deficit estimate is **total body water x (measured sodium / desired sodium - 1)**. It is an estimate, not a fluid order. For an illustrative adult with estimated body water 30 L and sodium 154 mmol/L, using 140 as the eventual comparison value gives 30 x (154/140 - 1) = **3 L**. This excludes ongoing losses and does not mean administering 3 L immediately. Chronic or unknown-duration hypernatremia needs planned, monitored correction rather than an attempt to normalize sodium immediately. Uncertain duration, changing renal output, or impaired cardiac function makes serial measurement more important than the apparent precision of the calculation.

### Potassium: identify loss, shift, impaired excretion, or artifact

Most potassium is inside cells. A serum value reflects cellular movement as well as whole-body balance. Insulin and beta-adrenergic stimulation shift potassium inward; tissue breakdown or insulin deficiency can increase circulating potassium. A patient can therefore have a high serum value despite depleted total-body stores, as in diabetic ketoacidosis. Treatment of that underlying disorder can reveal substantial hypokalemia. Do not estimate a replacement dose from a single serum concentration without considering the mechanism. [National Kidney Foundation potassium overview](https://www.kidney.org/kidney-topics/hyperkalemia-high-potassium)

Hemolysis, difficult collection, delayed handling or marked blood-cell abnormalities can falsely elevate potassium. Repeat promptly with ECG/clinical risk assessment. Possible artifact does not make severe hyperkalemia with AKI or relevant drugs safe to ignore; assign urgent collection and review.

Hypokalemia can reflect diarrhea, vomiting-related renal losses, diuretics, mineralocorticoid effects, magnesium deficiency or intracellular shifts. Check magnesium, renal function, bicarbonate, pressure and exposures. Urine potassium may distinguish renal wasting from conservation. Hypertension with renal wasting differs from diarrhea with low bicarbonate; inherited tubular disease requires a compatible timeline, urine pattern and family history.

**Hypokalemia below 2.5 mmol/L**, significant symptoms, or arrhythmia warrants hospital-level evaluation and usually monitored intravenous replacement. ECG findings can include flattened T waves, ST changes, and prominent U waves; electrical risk also depends on heart disease and other drugs. For a stable patient with mild or moderate deficiency and a functioning gastrointestinal tract, oral replacement is generally preferred. Correct magnesium and ongoing losses, select the actual salt/formulation, and reassess potassium and kidney function. [NHS SPS adult hypokalemia treatment](https://sps.nhs.uk/articles/hypokalaemia/)

Intravenous potassium must never be given by undiluted push. Use approved diluted products, an infusion pump, and the institution's concentration, access, rate, and monitoring policy. The SPS adult safety guidance ordinarily limits peripheral concentration to 40 mmol/L and infusion rate to 10 mmol/hour; faster or more concentrated regimens require the appropriate higher-acuity protocol. These are not instructions for an outpatient infusion or universal pediatric limits. Reduced excretion, resolution of a shift, and repeated unreviewed doses can turn replacement into hyperkalemia.

### High potassium: protect, redistribute, remove, and recheck

In the UKKA adult pathway, **potassium at least 6.5 mmol/L requires urgent hospital assessment**, even without characteristic ECG changes. Acute illness, AKI, a rapid rise, symptoms, or ECG abnormalities can require escalation at lower values. Peaked T waves, conduction delay, loss of P waves, bradycardia, or widening QRS may appear, but absence of a classic sequence does not exclude danger. Obtain ECG and monitoring appropriate to the severity rather than waiting for symptoms. [UKKA hospital assessment and treatment](https://www.ukkidney.org/sites/default/files/FINAL%20VERSION%20-%20UKKA%20CLINICAL%20PRACTICE%20GUIDELINE%20-%20MANAGEMENT%20OF%20HYPERKALAEMIA%20IN%20ADULTS%20-%20191223.pdf)

| Treatment task | What it accomplishes | Essential limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intravenous calcium for toxic ECG findings | Reduces immediate cardiac membrane toxicity | Does not lower potassium; calcium salts are not volume-equivalent |
| Insulin with glucose, with an inhaled beta-agonist as an appropriate adjunct | Moves potassium into cells temporarily | Does not remove body potassium; monitor for delayed hypoglycemia |
| Appropriate potassium removal | Dialysis, selected gastrointestinal binders, or renal excretion when feasible | Choice depends on urgency, renal output, volume status, and product restrictions |
| Serial reassessment | Detects response, rebound, hypoglycemia, and ongoing tissue release | A single improved potassium is not proof that the emergency has ended |

Follow emergency glucose and potassium monitoring after insulin. IV bicarbonate is not a routine substitute for core treatment, and binders cannot replace cardiac stabilization or indicated dialysis. Stop potassium intake and review contributing drugs. Reintroduce beneficial chronic medicines only after stabilization with monitoring.

The **UKKA adult hospital regimen** below requires monitored care; these doses are not for home treatment or children:

| Indication | UKKA example | Safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toxic ECG changes | 30 mL 10% calcium gluconate IV over 10 minutes | Calcium chloride 10 mL of 10% over 5 minutes is the resuscitation alternative; not volume-equivalent |
| Severe hyperkalemia | 10 units soluble/regular insulin with 25 g glucose by IV infusion | Check glucose before treatment and at 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300, and 360 minutes |
| Pretreatment glucose below 7 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) | Additional 10% glucose at 50 mL/hour for five hours after insulin-glucose | Continue surveillance; renal impairment and repeat insulin increase delayed hypoglycemia risk |
| Adjunct redistribution | Nebulized salbutamol/albuterol 10-20 mg | Not monotherapy for severe disease |

Recheck potassium at least at **1, 2, 4, 6, and 24 hours**, with more intensive care when needed. Arrange potassium removal and urgent dialysis when indicated while temporary shifting treatments work. [UKKA recommendations 16-18](https://www.ukkidney.org/sites/default/files/FINAL%20VERSION%20-%20UKKA%20CLINICAL%20PRACTICE%20GUIDELINE%20-%20MANAGEMENT%20OF%20HYPERKALAEMIA%20IN%20ADULTS%20-%20191223.pdf)

UKKA includes selected binders as hospital adjuncts, but the **U.S. sodium zirconium cyclosilicate label excludes emergency treatment of life-threatening hyperkalemia because of delayed onset**. An adjunctive hospital order must never delay calcium, insulin-glucose, or dialysis when indicated. Do not interpret guideline discussion of a binder as U.S. emergency label approval. [U.S. sodium zirconium cyclosilicate label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=8833d6e6-33ab-4ea9-b78f-3ec30a904ef8)

**Clinical Case: interacting potassium risks.** A 64-year-old with CKD takes an ARB, newly prescribed trimethoprim and a potassium salt substitute. Gastroenteritis accompanies potassium 6.7 mmol/L despite an unremarkable ECG. Arrange urgent hospital assessment and monitored treatment, addressing acute renal illness and interacting exposures rather than focusing dietary advice only on fruit. [UKKA recurrence and medicine review](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/hyperkalaemia/11-1-ii-hyperkalaemia-in-hospital/11-1-9-ii-hyperkalaemia-in-hospital-21-1-21-4/)

### Calcium: verify the biologically important abnormality

Total calcium includes protein-bound calcium; ionized calcium is the biologically active component. Altered albumin can change total calcium without the same ionized change, and alkalemia increases protein binding, potentially lowering ionized calcium despite a less striking total value. Albumin-adjustment equations are estimates and can misclassify patients. When protein, pH, kidney disease, or critical illness makes interpretation uncertain, obtain an appropriately handled ionized measurement rather than allowing a correction formula to overrule the patient. [Endotext calcium measurement](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK279037/)

With confirmed hypercalcemia, pair calcium with PTH. A PTH inside its laboratory reference interval can still be **inappropriately nonsuppressed** for high calcium. Consider primary hyperparathyroidism, lithium effects, and familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH) in the appropriate setting. Low urinary calcium supports FHH but is not diagnostic by itself: kidney impairment, thiazides, and low intake can confound the result. Suppressed PTH points toward a different differential, including malignancy, vitamin-D-related causes, granulomatous disease, medicines, and other selected endocrine disorders. [Endotext approach to hypercalcemia, updated 2026](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK279129/?report=printable)

PTH-related peptide can cause malignancy-associated hypercalcemia; increased calcitriol is a separate mechanism in some lymphomas or granulomatous disease. Secondary hyperparathyroidism is compensatory, usually with low/normal calcium. Tertiary disease is autonomous secretion after longstanding secondary stimulation. Interpret calcium, phosphate, renal function and treatment history; see 04E and 06C.

Hypercalcemia may cause thirst, polyuria, constipation, weakness, confusion and renal injury. Severe or rapidly symptomatic disease needs hospital care. Correct intravascular depletion with isotonic fluid while monitoring cardiac/renal tolerance; avoid fixed large-volume prescriptions or loop diuretics solely to force calciuresis. Stop relevant exposures and identify the cause. [Society for Endocrinology acute hypercalcemia guidance](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/4277/emergency-guidance_acute-hypercalcaemia-in-adults.pdf)

For **hypercalcemia of malignancy**, the Endocrine Society recommends denosumab or an intravenous bisphosphonate; severe disease above 14 mg/dL can warrant initial calcitonin alongside antiresorptive treatment. Calcitonin is limited to 48-72 hours because its effect wanes. Calcitriol-mediated disease has a distinct glucocorticoid-based pathway. Agent selection requires renal, calcium/vitamin D, dental, and product-specific assessment. These treatments are not interchangeable with the long-term evaluation of mild primary hyperparathyroidism. [Endocrine Society malignancy-specific pathway](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/hypercalcemia)

Low ionized calcium may cause perioral tingling, cramps, carpopedal spasm, seizures, laryngospasm, or QT prolongation. Consider neck surgery, hypoparathyroidism, magnesium deficiency, vitamin D deficiency, CKD, acute systemic illness, or citrate exposure. Measure magnesium, phosphate, renal function, and appropriate PTH/vitamin D tests. Symptomatic hypocalcemia or severe biochemical disease requires monitored intravenous calcium and treatment of the cause. Check the actual elemental-calcium content: equal volumes of calcium gluconate and calcium chloride are not equivalent. [Society for Endocrinology hypocalcemia guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5314808/), [Dose-equivalence addendum](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32022081/)

Mild stable disease may be managed with an appropriate oral calcium/vitamin D plan and repeat testing, but the cause determines the product and monitoring. Activated vitamin D is not simply substituted for nutritional vitamin D in every patient. The FDA warns of severe hypocalcemia with osteoporosis-dose denosumab in advanced CKD, especially with CKD-MBD or dialysis; prevention and post-treatment monitoring need specialist coordination. [FDA denosumab kidney-disease warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-adds-boxed-warning-increased-risk-severe-hypocalcemia-patients-advanced-chronic-kidney-disease)

### Magnesium and phosphate connect otherwise puzzling findings

Magnesium deficiency can accompany poor intake, diarrhea, alcohol-related illness, or renal/drug losses. Loop/thiazide diuretics, proton pump inhibitors, aminoglycosides, and tacrolimus are relevant exposures. It can sustain hypokalemia and interfere with calcium regulation, so apparently adequate potassium or calcium treatment may fail until magnesium is addressed. A serum concentration does not precisely measure total-body stores. High supplemental intake, especially with impaired renal clearance, can instead cause toxicity with weakness, hypotension, and cardiac or respiratory compromise. [NIH magnesium physiology and toxicity](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)

Severe or symptomatic magnesium deficiency needs hospital replacement; mild stable deficiency may permit oral treatment. Oral magnesium can worsen diarrhea; IV treatment requires renal, pressure, preparation and laboratory monitoring. SPS advises specialist input for AKI, advanced CKD, dialysis or transplantation. Preparations differ in elemental content. [NHS SPS magnesium replacement and monitoring](https://sps.nhs.uk/articles/treating-acute-hypomagnesaemia-in-adults/)

Phosphate becomes particularly important during malnutrition/refeeding, diabetic emergencies, tissue breakdown, and kidney disease. A marked fall can impair muscle and respiratory function; replacement itself can disturb calcium or potassium, depending on the formulation. Refeeding prevention requires a coordinated nutrition, thiamine, electrolyte, and monitoring plan rather than waiting for symptoms after feeding begins. See 04D for the complete nutritional framework and 06C for persistent CKD mineral abnormalities. [NICE nutrition and refeeding safeguards](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg32/chapter/Recommendations)

**Clinical Case: linked deficits.** A 47-year-old with prolonged diarrhea, a loop diuretic and long-term acid suppression has potassium 2.9 mmol/L despite replacement, low magnesium and falling calcium. Correct linked deficits and reassess losses, renal function and medicines. Weakness, ECG changes or other severe findings requires urgent care.

### Interpret acid-base results in a reproducible sequence

**Acidemia** describes arterial pH below 7.35; **acidosis** describes a process lowering pH. An opposing process can conceal it. The same distinction applies to alkalemia above 7.45 and alkalosis. Interpret pH, carbon dioxide, and bicarbonate together, check whether the values are internally plausible, then assess compensation and the anion gap. A normal pH does not exclude a serious mixed disorder. [ATS clinical education: ABG interpretation](https://member.thoracic.org/professionals/clinical-resources/critical-care/clinical-education/abgs.php)

| Primary process | Primary change | Expected compensatory direction | Representative causes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | Lower bicarbonate | Lower PaCO2 through ventilation | Lactic or ketoacidosis, retained acids, diarrhea, tubular disease |
| Metabolic alkalosis | Higher bicarbonate | Higher PaCO2 through reduced ventilation | Vomiting, diuretics, mineralocorticoid effects, alkali load |
| Respiratory acidosis | Higher PaCO2 | Higher bicarbonate through renal adaptation | Impaired ventilation from lung, neuromuscular, drug, or CNS disease |
| Respiratory alkalosis | Lower PaCO2 | Lower bicarbonate through renal adaptation | Hypoxemia, sepsis, pain, pregnancy, selected CNS/toxic causes |

Compensation is a predicted response, not a second diagnosis simply because both numbers are abnormal. In metabolic acidosis, **Winter's formula gives expected PaCO2 = 1.5 x bicarbonate + 8, with a range of +/-2 mm Hg**. A higher measured PaCO2 indicates an additional respiratory acidosis; a lower value indicates additional respiratory alkalosis. This equation is not the compensation rule for metabolic alkalosis. Renal adaptation to respiratory disorders takes time, so acute and chronic patterns differ.

For respiratory acidosis, a **10-mm Hg increase in PaCO2 above 40** predicts about a **1-mmol/L bicarbonate rise acutely**, versus about **3.5 mmol/L after several days** of renal adaptation, using 24 mmol/L as the reference bicarbonate. Thus PaCO2 60 with bicarbonate 26 fits an acute response better than a chronic one; chronic isolated disease would predict about 31. These are approximate physiologic checks, not stand-alone diagnostic thresholds. [ATS expected compensation table](https://member.thoracic.org/professionals/clinical-resources/critical-care/clinical-education/abgs.php)

Calculate the anion gap as **sodium - (chloride + bicarbonate)** when potassium is omitted. Use the laboratory's reference interval and account for albumin: low albumin lowers the expected gap and can conceal accumulated unmeasured acids. A high gap prompts evaluation for lactate, ketones, impaired renal acid clearance, and clinically plausible toxic exposures. An unremarkable osmolal gap does not safely exclude a toxic alcohol late in its course. Obtain targeted urgent testing and toxicology advice when the history or physiology warrants it rather than relying on a mnemonic alone.

**Worked example:** An adult with hypotension and depressed breathing has sodium 140, chloride 104, and bicarbonate 12 mmol/L, with PaCO2 40 mm Hg. The anion gap is 140 - (104 + 12) = **24 mmol/L**. Expected respiratory compensation is 1.5 x 12 + 8 = **26 mm Hg**, with an approximate range of 24-28. The measured value of 40 therefore indicates **additional respiratory acidosis**, not adequate compensation. The immediate questions concern perfusion, acid production, and ventilation. Neither a diagnosis of isolated diarrhea nor routine outpatient oral bicarbonate explains this combination.

### Use the pattern to investigate the cause, not to prescribe blindly

A normal-gap metabolic acidosis often reflects gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss or impaired renal acid handling, and chloride-rich fluid can contribute. Consider renal tubular acidosis (RTA) when the pattern persists without an adequate gastrointestinal explanation. Distal, type 1 disease impairs acid secretion and can accompany hypokalemia and stones. Proximal, type 2 disease involves bicarbonate wasting, sometimes with other proximal solute losses. Type 4 disease links impaired aldosterone action or availability, hyperkalemia, and reduced acid excretion. Urine pH alone cannot classify every case; interpret it in the current acid-base and treatment context. [NIDDK renal tubular disorders](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/renal-tubular-acidosis)

Vomiting or gastric suction removes acid and chloride; diuretics can create renal chloride and potassium loss. Volume depletion, potassium depletion, and hormonal effects may maintain the alkalosis after its initiating event. Urine chloride helps distinguish chloride-depleted from other patterns, but recent diuretic exposure changes its interpretation. Hypertension and persistent renal potassium loss suggest investigating mineralocorticoid effects rather than treating all alkalosis with saline. In a congested patient, additional saline may be harmful; treatment must fit the actual volume state. [AJKD authored core curriculum on metabolic alkalosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272638622005169)

Respiratory acidosis reflects inadequate ventilation relative to carbon dioxide production. Assess airway, effort, oxygenation, drugs, lung and neuromuscular disease. Rising PaCO2 in a tiring patient with metabolic acidosis can signal failing compensation. Respiratory alkalosis may reflect hypoxemia, pulmonary vascular disease, sepsis, pregnancy, CNS disease or salicylates, not just anxiety. Salicylates can also cause high-gap metabolic acidosis. Treat the cause and protect ventilation.

For a high-gap disorder, compare the rise in gap with the fall in bicarbonate when looking for another metabolic process, while allowing for the baseline gap, albumin, and timing. Vomiting may conceal part of a concurrent acidosis; diarrhea can add a normal-gap process. No calculation replaces the exposure history and serial response. Alkali is appropriate in selected disorders, not a universal remedy for every low bicarbonate. Chronic CKD management, acute poisonings, ketoacidosis, and respiratory failure each have different pathways.

**A normal pH can mislead:** If bicarbonate is 12 mmol/L and PaCO2 is 20 mm Hg, the resulting arterial pH can be approximately 7.40. Expected PaCO2 for an isolated metabolic acidosis is still about 26, not 20. The near-normal pH therefore does not show that the patient is well compensated; it reflects opposing metabolic acidosis and respiratory alkalosis. Investigate the clinical context, including sepsis or a relevant ingestion, rather than dismissing both abnormal components because their effects on pH partly cancel.

### Prevent medication-related recurrence and close the follow-up loop

Review why each drug is prescribed before altering it. Thiazides can contribute to hyponatremia, hypokalemia, and alkalosis while reducing urinary calcium; loops can cause potassium/magnesium loss and alkalosis. Potassium-sparing agents can produce hyperkalemia. Acetazolamide promotes bicarbonate loss, and topiramate can produce a tubular acid-base disorder and increase stone risk. Lithium can impair urine concentration and alter calcium regulation. SSRIs and some antiseizure drugs can contribute to antidiuresis. The same medicine may be useful in one circumstance and harmful in another; its name alone does not decide the plan. [Current topiramate labeling: bicarbonate monitoring and stones](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=2f5b1b0b-fd8a-4c4e-a85a-519ca841a0db&version=19)

**Clinical Case: alkalosis with congestion.** A 56-year-old with heart failure develops hypokalemia and alkalosis after intensified diuresis but remains breathless and edematous. Assess congestion, perfusion, renal function, magnesium, losses and diuretic need. Replace deficits while treating overload; the matching bicarbonate value of a vomiting-depleted patient does not justify saline here.

### Adapt the assessment across the lifespan

Children need weight-based prescriptions, age-specific laboratory interpretation, and separate calculations for resuscitation, maintenance, and replacement of ongoing losses. Adult fixed-volume hypertonic saline and replacement rules are not pediatric prescriptions. NICE's pediatric hospital-fluid pathway requires documented weights, intake/output, examination, electrolytes, and glucose, with more frequent testing when disturbances are present. New symptomatic hyponatremia during intravenous therapy requires immediate expert advice and active treatment rather than fluid restriction alone. In infants, a feeding history includes actual formula preparation and intake, not just the statement that feeds were offered. [NICE pediatric intravenous fluid safeguards](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng29/chapter/Recommendations)

Pregnancy alters baseline ventilation and acid-base measurements: physiologic respiratory alkalosis means that nonpregnant reference expectations cannot be applied mechanically. This normal adaptation does not explain marked symptoms, severe electrolyte abnormalities, or an elevated anion gap. Persistent vomiting, poor intake, diabetes, and an acute illness require an obstetric-aware assessment of maternal circulation, nutrition, electrolytes, and the underlying cause. Replacement choices and long-term medicines must also be checked for pregnancy and lactation suitability rather than continued simply because the dose was appropriate before pregnancy. [ACOG respiratory physiology in pregnancy](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2020/04/physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period)

In dependent adults, assess drink provision, swallowing and fluid avoidance due to incontinence. Frailty and low muscle mass may conceal prescribing risk despite modest creatinine. Heart failure and impaired excretion make indiscriminate replacement dangerous. In advanced kidney disease, coordinate fluids, replacement, potassium removal and dialysis with the renal team and caregiver.

Document mechanism, medication changes, replacement duration, repeat testing and result ownership. Use teach-back for fluid/supplement instructions. Give urgent precautions for inability to drink, recurrent vomiting, weakness, palpitations, confusion or reduced consciousness, and ensure testing is accessible.
