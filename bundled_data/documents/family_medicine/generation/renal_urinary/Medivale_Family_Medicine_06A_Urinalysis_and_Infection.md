# Medivale Family Medicine

## 06A. Urinalysis and Infection

Use the IDSA pathway for systemic/complicated UTI, AUA/CUA/SUFU for recurrent localized cystitis in women, and ACOG for pregnancy. The cystitis dose examples below follow EAU 2026; the acute-prostatitis review schedule follows NICE. A bladder regimen must not be extrapolated to kidney, prostate, pregnancy, or childhood infection.

### Begin with the syndrome, not the urine result

UTI requires a compatible clinical illness, not bacteriuria alone. Establish new symptoms, duration and location: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, visible blood, fever, rigors, flank pain, vomiting, discharge, genital symptoms or inability to void. Compare with baseline; chronic urgency or incontinence alone does not establish infection.

Most bladder infections ascend through the urethra. E. coli is common; prior antibiotics, catheters, procedures and impaired drainage alter pathogen and resistance risk. Infection can reach the kidney, prostate or bloodstream. Retention, stones, pregnancy, immune status and renal function modify treatment and consequences. [NIDDK bladder infection causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/bladder-infection-uti-in-adults/symptoms-causes)

Cystitis produces lower-tract symptoms without systemic illness. Fever, rigors, flank pain or costovertebral tenderness, nausea, vomiting or systemic deterioration suggests pyelonephritis, with or without dysuria. Discharge, vulvar irritation, ulcers or pelvic symptoms suggests urethritis, cervicitis, vaginitis or another genital process despite a positive urine test. Ask privately about anatomy and sexual exposure sites.

IDSA 2025 classifies treatment syndromes primarily by whether infection appears confined to the bladder or extends beyond it. Male sex alone is not a sufficient shortcut to every treatment decision. Catheter-associated infection and systemic findings require particular attention, while diabetes, obstruction risk, immune status, and previous resistant organisms remain important modifiers. This classification guides treatment; it does not diagnose UTI in a person without an attributable illness. [IDSA current UTI classification](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/complicated-urinary-tract-infections/)

| Presentation | Leading clinical question | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New dysuria and frequency, no systemic findings | Is this localized cystitis? | Assess lower-tract treatment eligibility |
| Fever, flank pain, vomiting | Has infection reached kidney or beyond? | Culture and assess severity, hydration, drainage |
| Dysuria with genital discharge or ulcers | Is this an STI or genital process? | Select appropriate examination and site-specific tests |
| Pelvic/perineal pain, fever, voiding difficulty | Is the prostate involved? | Use a prostate-directed assessment and treatment plan |
| Positive culture without attributable symptoms | Is this asymptomatic bacteriuria? | Check the limited treatment exceptions |

### Collect a sample that can answer the question

For routine voided bacterial culture, explain midstream collection into a suitable container without touching its interior. Record symptoms, collection method and recent antibiotics. Menstrual/genital contamination, delayed transport and prior treatment can distort results. Arrange prompt transport or laboratory-specified refrigeration/preservative. [MedlinePlus clean-catch collection](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007487.htm)

Routine bacterial culture uses midstream urine; urethral gonorrhea/chlamydia NAAT commonly uses first-catch urine according to assay instructions. Vaginal or exposure-site swabs may be preferable or additionally needed. If contamination could change care, repeat a coached specimen or use catheterization when appropriate. [CDC urethritis and cervicitis testing](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/urethritis-and-cervicitis.htm)

For catheterized patients, obtain a culture aseptically from the sampling port, never the collection bag. Review whether the catheter is still required and whether replacement before collection is appropriate. Transport the sample correctly and identify its source. CDC emphasizes that odor or cloudiness alone and pyuria without a clinical indication are not appropriate reasons for reflex culture. [CDC urine culture collection and indications](https://www.cdc.gov/uti/hcp/clinical-guidance/culture-stewardship-strategize.html)

**Clinical Case: an unreliable specimen.** A patient with chronic urgency, no fever and no new symptoms provides urine collected hours earlier in a reused container. Mixed growth does not prove resistant infection. Reconsider the indication and obtain a suitable specimen if testing remains necessary.

### Dipstick, microscopy, and culture measure different things

Urinalysis combines appearance, dipstick chemistry and often microscopy. Dipstick provides indirect markers; microscopy identifies cells, casts, crystals and organisms; culture detects growth and permits susceptibility testing. Interpret them with timing, specimen quality and pretest probability. [MedlinePlus urinalysis](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003579.htm)

Leukocyte esterase reflects white-cell activity, and microscopic pyuria supports urinary inflammation. Neither proves a conventional bacterial infection. Urethritis, stones, contamination, recent treatment, and inflammatory renal disease can produce white cells. Nitrite positivity supports nitrate-reducing organisms, but sensitivity is limited: some organisms do not produce nitrite, and frequent voiding can reduce the time available for conversion. A negative nitrite result alone does not exclude UTI. [AUA urinary testing curriculum](https://www.auanet.org/Documents/education/NMSC-Adult-UTI.pdf)

| Finding | What it supports | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leukocyte esterase or WBCs | Inflammation | Bacterial cause, site, or need for antibiotics |
| Nitrite | Compatible bacterial metabolism | Absence of infection when negative |
| Dipstick blood | Heme-containing material | Intact RBCs or an infectious cause |
| Bacteria on microscopy | Organisms in the sample | Symptomatic infection rather than contamination/colonization |
| Squamous epithelial cells with mixed growth | Possible collection contamination | A universal rule that every such sample is unusable |

A reassuring urinalysis lowers the likelihood of bacterial cystitis and should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis, especially in recurrent symptom presentations. Conversely, positive markers have limited specificity when asymptomatic bacteriuria is common. Do not use a single WBC or colony-count cutoff as a substitute for the syndrome. Important exceptions to ordinary test performance include immune suppression, very early illness, and sampling after treatment. Reassess a sick patient even when the first sample is nondiagnostic. [AUA/CUA/SUFU diagnostic framework](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/rUTI%20Update/rUTI%202025%20Unabridged%20Final.pdf)

Dipstick blood requires appropriate microscopic interpretation: it may reflect RBCs, free hemoglobin, or myoglobin. Substantial protein, dysmorphic RBCs, or RBC casts suggests a different renal question, not routine cystitis treatment. WBC casts can support an intrarenal inflammatory process but are not uniquely diagnostic of pyelonephritis. Urinary eosinophils are not a reliable stand-alone diagnosis of interstitial nephritis. Persistent blood or protein needs follow-up after an apparent infection resolves; detailed hematuria and proteinuria pathways follow in 06B.

### Use culture to refine a clinical diagnosis

Culture reports organism, quantity and susceptibility. Interpret these with symptoms, collection method and recent treatment. Low counts of a single plausible pathogen may matter in symptomatic patients; high counts without attributable illness may not require treatment. Mixed growth often reflects contamination but can represent true polymicrobial infection in complex settings; discuss discordant results with the laboratory.

Culture is particularly important for pyelonephritis, recurrent episodes, pregnancy, suspected resistant infection, treatment failure, and selected complex or male presentations. Obtain it before antibiotics when feasible, but do not delay urgent treatment in sepsis to achieve an ideal specimen. Blood tests and selected blood cultures assess systemic illness and renal function; routine blood cultures are not necessary for every stable bladder infection. Imaging answers questions about drainage, stones, abscess, or anatomy, not simply whether bacteria grew. [NIDDK bladder diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/bladder-infection-uti-in-adults/diagnosis), [NIDDK kidney infection diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-infection-pyelonephritis/diagnosis)

Assign culture review and patient contact. Discordant results require reconsidering both diagnosis and drug. In-vitro susceptibility does not ensure effective kidney, prostate or bloodstream concentrations.

### Treat localized cystitis with a population-specific regimen

For an otherwise appropriate nonpregnant adult with a convincing lower-tract syndrome and no systemic findings, use an effective narrow regimen selected for susceptibility, local resistance, allergies, renal function, interactions, and availability. AUA identifies nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX, and fosfomycin as first-line options for appropriate episodes in its recurrent-UTI population. The EAU 2026 adult-women cystitis regimens below provide common short-course examples, not a prescription for every urinary infection. [AUA episode treatment](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/rUTI%20Update/rUTI%202025%20Unabridged%20Final.pdf), [EAU adult cystitis regimens](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urological-infections/chapter/the-guideline)

| Selected nonpregnant adult-woman option | Common oral regimen | Key limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals | 100 mg twice daily for 5 days, with food | Guideline regimen; U.S. label duration and renal limits differ, as explained below |
| TMP-SMX | 160/800 mg twice daily for 3 days | Known susceptible isolate or local E. coli resistance below 20%; assess renal dosing and interactions |
| Fosfomycin trometamol | 3 g once | Lower-tract use; organism and susceptibility matter |

Nitrofurantoin does not provide adequate kidney- or prostate-tissue treatment. EAU excludes use below eGFR 30 mL/min/1.73 m2; the cited U.S. Macrobid label instead contraindicates creatinine clearance below 60 mL/min and specifies seven days. These are not equivalent renal measures or identical prescribing instructions. The five-day table is a guideline regimen, not the U.S. label regimen; renal use outside that label requires a deliberate local prescribing decision. Avoid nitrofurantoin in G6PD deficiency; its label contraindicates use at term (38-42 weeks), during labor, or when labor is imminent. [EAU cystitis and renal limits](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urological-infections/chapter/the-guideline), [U.S. Macrobid label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8f270a9f-12a1-44d4-bc7e-873613555801)

Oral fosfomycin is not routine initial pyelonephritis treatment. TMP-SMX can raise potassium, particularly with kidney impairment or ACE inhibitor/ARB treatment, and interacts importantly with warfarin and methotrexate. Fluoroquinolones are not routine first choices for simple cystitis when suitable alternatives exist. Do not choose empiric amoxicillin when resistance makes activity unreliable. [EAU antimicrobial selection](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urological-infections/chapter/the-guideline)

Pivmecillinam is approved for specified uncomplicated infections in adult females; check the U.S. product dose rather than substituting a European regimen. Gepotidacin has a specified female adult/adolescent indication; oral sulopenem etzadroxil/probenecid is reserved for adult women with limited or no alternative oral options. Organism, age/weight, interaction, renal/hepatic and cardiac restrictions are product-specific. [FDA pivmecillinam approval](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-new-treatment-uncomplicated-urinary-tract-infections), [FDA gepotidacin](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-approvals-and-databases/drug-trials-snapshots-blujepa), [FDA oral sulopenem](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-approvals-and-databases/drug-trials-snapshots-orlynvah)

Provide analgesia and enough fluid to avoid dehydration, respecting cardiac or kidney fluid limits. Explain the course, adverse effects and expected improvement. Urinary analgesics relieve symptoms without treating infection. Worsening fever, flank pain, vomiting, retention or systemic symptoms requires reassessment before the course ends. [NIDDK bladder treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/bladder-infection-uti-in-adults/treatment)

### Pyelonephritis and systemic UTI require tissue treatment and source assessment

Assess circulation, mental status, oral intake, urine output, pain, renal function, and risk of impaired drainage. Sepsis, persistent vomiting, pregnancy-associated kidney infection, significant obstruction, major immune compromise, or inability to obtain timely care can require hospital management. Not every stable pyelonephritis patient requires admission, but outpatient treatment needs an effective regimen, ability to take it, reliable follow-up, and a clear return plan. A patient living far from care may need a different arrangement from one able to return promptly. [NIDDK kidney infection treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-infection-pyelonephritis/treatment)

**Clinical Case: infection beyond the bladder.** A patient treated for dysuria develops fever, unilateral flank pain and repeated vomiting despite a nitrofurantoin-susceptible isolate. Reassess for pyelonephritis and obstruction, obtain cultures and use tissue-active treatment. Unreliable oral intake also changes the route and disposition.

IDSA's empiric cUTI approach uses severity, resistance risk, patient safety and, in sepsis, local susceptibility data. Review prior cultures and recent antibiotics. Severe illness may need broad IV coverage tailored to those risks; narrow promptly when organism and susceptibility results permit. [IDSA cUTI antimicrobial selection](https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/cuti/clinical-question-1-2025.pdf)

IDSA 2026 separates ESBL bladder infection from pyelonephritis/cUTI. Susceptible bladder isolates may still allow narrow oral treatment; systemic disease may require another agent. ESBL bacteriuria without symptoms does not create an antibiotic indication. Seek stewardship or specialist advice when options are limited or toxic. [IDSA 2026 resistance guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/amr-guidance/)

**Infected obstruction requires urgent source control.** Arrange imaging and urologic drainage assessment; antibiotics alone may fail in a blocked collecting system. Persistent illness, stones, renal deterioration or poor response suggests obstruction, abscess or an alternative diagnosis. Do not delay investigation of deterioration to finish antibiotics. Select imaging for the clinical question and pregnancy/renal context.

When initially treated IV, an improving patient who can take oral medication and has an effective oral option can often switch. For associated Gram-negative bacteremia, adequate bloodstream as well as urinary/tissue exposure matters; stability and source control are central. Evidence is less direct in severe sepsis, immune suppression, significant renal impairment, catheters, and structural disease, so those patients may need individualized plans. [IDSA IV-to-oral transition](https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/cuti/clinical-question-2.pdf)

For improving cUTI patients on effective treatment, IDSA generally favors 5-7 days of a fluoroquinolone or 7 days of a non-fluoroquinolone rather than routinely extending to 10-14 days. Count from the first effective day, not necessarily the first inactive prescription. Abscess, obstruction, bacterial prostatitis, severe illness, renal disease, and other excluded trial populations require individualization; do not export this duration to pregnancy or confirmed prostate infection. Lack of prompt improvement calls for reassessment of source control and diagnosis. [IDSA cUTI duration](https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/cuti/clinical-question-3-2025.pdf)

A concrete outpatient example is **ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally twice daily for seven days** for a suitable nonpregnant adult with pyelonephritis, normal renal function, an active organism, and no fluoroquinolone contraindication. EAU permits empiric ciprofloxacin only when local fluoroquinolone resistance is below 10%; previous resistant isolates or recent exposure may rule it out even then. If activity is uncertain, arrange a locally appropriate initial parenteral regimen rather than send the patient home with unreliable oral coverage. Review response and culture within **48-72 hours**, sooner for deterioration. This example does not define treatment for sepsis, pregnancy, obstruction, or prostatitis. [EAU pyelonephritis regimen and follow-up](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urological-infections/chapter/the-guideline)

### Asymptomatic bacteriuria usually should not be treated

ASB is bacterial growth without attributable UTI symptoms, whether or not pyuria is present. The conventional voided-sample definition uses at least 100,000 CFU/mL, confirmed in two consecutive specimens in women and one in men; the prenatal screening workflow is described separately below. IDSA recommends against routine screening or treatment in healthy nonpregnant adults, older long-term-care residents, people with diabetes, and many catheterized or spinal-cord-injury populations. Treatment can cause adverse effects, C. difficile infection, and resistance without improving outcomes. [IDSA asymptomatic bacteriuria](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/asymptomatic-bacteriuria/)

The main established exceptions are pregnancy and endoscopic urologic procedures expected to cause mucosal trauma. For the latter, obtain a culture and targeted treatment, generally **one or two doses beginning 30-60 minutes before the procedure**, not an extended course for every device or nonurologic operation. Very early transplant and high-risk neutropenia involve evidence gaps and specialist protocols, not an automatic treatment rule. Delirium or a fall alone, without local urinary symptoms or systemic infection signs, warrants assessment of other causes rather than reflex treatment of bacteriuria. Sepsis still requires prompt evaluation and treatment. [IDSA treatment exceptions and timing](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/asymptomatic-bacteriuria/)

**Clinical Case: bacteriuria after a fall.** An older adult falls after poor intake and a new sedating medicine. Urine grows E. coli, but there is no dysuria, flank pain, fever or instability. Assess volume, medicines, injury and other illness; the culture does not establish causation. Reassess promptly if localizing or systemic signs develop.

### Pregnancy has a separate screening and treatment pathway

ACOG recommends a urine culture once early in prenatal care and targeted treatment for ASB at 100,000 CFU/mL or more, generally for 5-7 days. Symptomatic cystitis also receives culture-guided, pregnancy-appropriate treatment, generally 5-7 days; avoid empiric ampicillin/amoxicillin when E. coli resistance is likely. **Fosfomycin 3 g orally once is a lower-tract exception to the multi-day duration**, when appropriate to the isolate and patient. Nitrofurantoin or TMP-SMX can be reasonable in the first trimester if no appropriate alternative exists; this does not override product-specific precautions, nitrofurantoin's G6PD restriction, or its term/labor contraindication. Neither nitrofurantoin nor fosfomycin completes pyelonephritis treatment.

Manage pyelonephritis initially in hospital, with renal-tissue-active treatment and a total 14-day course in this pathway. After cystitis, clinicians may repeat culture in one to two weeks or test if symptoms recur; evidence does not support inventing a universal repeated-screening schedule after every negative initial screen or treated ASB episode. Recurrent infection may justify a susceptibility-matched suppression plan. [ACOG pregnancy-associated UTI](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2023/08/urinary-tract-infections-in-pregnant-individuals)

Any **GBS bacteriuria during pregnancy** must be documented for intrapartum prophylaxis; low-count asymptomatic GBS does not automatically require antepartum antibiotics. A pregnant patient with rigors, flank pain, or poor intake needs urgent assessment, not the next screening culture. See 14C for the separate perinatal prevention pathway. [ACOG pregnancy UTI and GBS implications](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2023/08/urinary-tract-infections-in-pregnant-individuals)

### Recurrent symptoms need confirmation and prevention, not repeated assumptions

Recurrent UTI is commonly defined as two symptomatic episodes within six months or three within a year. Confirm that previous episodes were compatible illnesses with supporting urine evidence rather than counting every prescription or positive test. Review cultures, symptom-free intervals, sexual association, spermicide use, menopausal symptoms, emptying difficulty, stones, and prior procedures. Examination can reveal vulvovaginal or pelvic-floor conditions that change the diagnosis. AUA's recurrent-UTI pathway primarily addresses women with recurrent localized cystitis, not all febrile, catheterized, obstructed, or pregnant patients.

Routine cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging are unnecessary for typical recurrent localized cystitis. Rapid same-organism recurrence, persistent blood, suspected stones/obstruction or poor response warrants further assessment. Persistent symptoms after microbiologic resolution suggest another cause. Selected patients may use a clinician-agreed self-start plan while obtaining indicated cultures. [AUA/CUA/SUFU recurrent UTI guidance](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/rUTI%20Update/rUTI%202025%20Unabridged%20Final.pdf)

Prevention is individualized. The 2025 update supports vaginal estrogen for appropriate peri-/postmenopausal patients, cranberry as an option, increased water intake when habitual intake is low, and selected methenamine use. D-mannose alone should not be presented as reliably effective. Discuss costs, product variability, contraindications, and the difference between prevention and treatment of a current infection. Continuous or intercourse-associated antibiotic prophylaxis can be considered after discussing benefits and harms, with regular reassessment rather than an unreviewed permanent prescription. [AUA authors' implementation discussion](https://auanews.net/issues/articles/2025/october-2025/updates-to-the-aua/cua/sufu-guideline-on-recurrent-uncomplicated-urinary-tract-infections-in-women)

Methenamine's activity depends on urine chemistry; it is not a substitute for treating an acute systemic infection. The U.S. label contraindicates use with renal insufficiency, severe hepatic insufficiency, or severe dehydration and warns against combining it with sulfonamides. Review the actual product and patient rather than assuming all nonantibiotic prevention is risk-free. [Methenamine prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=c8ddaa11-5753-46eb-8bd4-8f1d874a3ec4)

**Clinical Case: recurrent burning without recurrent infection.** A postmenopausal patient reports three burning episodes, only one supported by pyuria and culture, with vaginal dryness between episodes. Count confirmed infections separately, assess genitourinary syndrome of menopause and other causes, and choose prevention accordingly.

### Catheter-associated illness requires more than bacteriuria

Catheter biofilm makes bacterial detection increasingly common with time. Differentiate symptomatic catheter-associated infection from colonization by assessing the clinical change and other possible sources. Chronic bacteriuria is not proof that a catheter needs antibiotic sterilization, and repeated treatment seldom keeps it sterile. Review removal when no longer needed, obstruction, drainage-system problems, and collection technique. Fever or systemic illness without another source warrants evaluation; odor alone does not establish infection. [CDC catheter culture stewardship](https://www.cdc.gov/uti/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

With impaired sensation, autonomic dysreflexia, increased spasticity or new malaise may signal illness without ordinary dysuria. Interpret these changes clinically. Maintain closed drainage and use specimen-appropriate diagnostic criteria; facility surveillance definitions do not replace bedside assessment.

Yeast in urine is also not automatically an antifungal indication. In an asymptomatic patient, remove predisposing factors such as an unnecessary catheter when possible. IDSA reserves treatment of asymptomatic candiduria for defined high-risk settings, including neutropenia, very-low-birth-weight infants, and planned urologic manipulation. Fever, obstruction, or suspected invasive disease needs a different assessment. [IDSA candiduria recommendations](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/candidiasis/)

### Consider urethritis, cervicitis, and pelvic disease

Discharge, meatal irritation, exposure history, cervicitis, or pyuria without a conventional urinary pathogen should prompt appropriate STI testing. Obtain gonorrhea/chlamydia NAAT using the correct specimen and exposure sites; persistent or recurrent disease may need additional pathogen evaluation. Consider genital herpes with ulcers or marked external dysuria. Pelvic pain, cervical-motion/adnexal findings, or systemic illness requires assessment for pelvic inflammatory disease rather than a bladder-only plan. Partner evaluation and prevention matter as much as the initial prescription. [CDC urethritis/cervicitis guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/urethritis-and-cervicitis.htm)

For uncomplicated gonococcal infection, CDC's standard ceftriaxone regimen is weight-specific: 500 mg IM once below 150 kg and 1 g at 150 kg or above. Add appropriate chlamydia treatment if it has not been excluded, with pregnancy-specific selection when relevant. Treat the identified syndrome rather than assuming a urinary antibiotic will cover it. Arrange partner care, indicated additional testing, and follow-up according to the diagnosed infection. [CDC gonococcal treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-adults.htm)

### Prostate infection needs a different examination and course

Acute bacterial prostatitis can cause fever, dysuria, pelvic/perineal pain, painful ejaculation, or retention. A gently performed indicated rectal examination may find tenderness, but do not perform prostatic massage in suspected acute bacterial disease: it can precipitate bacteremia. Obtain urine culture, assess systemic severity and drainage, and investigate abscess when the course is concerning. A short bladder-only regimen is inadequate if the prostate is infected. [EAU prostatitis assessment](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urological-infections/chapter/the-guideline)

Choose a prostate-penetrating agent based on culture, safety, and severity. For an appropriate adult with normal renal function, the NICE oral pathway includes **ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 14 days, then review**; stop or extend another 14 days according to response. A specialist-discussed susceptible alternative is **TMP-SMX 160/800 mg twice daily for 14 days, then review**. These are not universal empiric choices, and fluoroquinolone toxicity, renal adjustment, and potassium/interactions can change selection. Severe illness, retention, or suspected abscess needs hospital/urologic care; **failure to improve within 48 hours** also warrants hospital reassessment. This prostate pathway is distinct from short-course cUTI without established prostate involvement. [NICE final acute-prostatitis recommendations and Table 1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng110/chapter/Recommendations)

Persistent pelvic pain is not always chronic bacterial prostatitis. Repeated negative cultures, noninfectious features, or poor response should prompt assessment for chronic pelvic pain and other urologic, pelvic-floor, or anorectal causes. Avoid repeated prolonged antibiotics without evidence of a bacterial target. Acute scrotal pain also demands consideration of torsion; a presumed UTI must not delay urgent surgical evaluation. Lower urinary symptoms, retention, epididymitis, and scrotal conditions receive fuller treatment in 06E.

### Adapt to children and arrange deliberate follow-up

Infants and children may have fever, vomiting, or poor feeding instead of dysuria. Obtain a reliable age-appropriate specimen before antibiotics when safe, without delaying care for a seriously ill child. The **AAP 2026 pathway covers ages eight days to five years**; neonatal/febrile-infant evaluation and the need for hospitalization remain separate safety decisions. Within scope, infants 8-28 days initially receive parenteral treatment; beyond 28 days, oral treatment is appropriate when tolerated and hospitalization is not otherwise needed. Do not transfer the adult three-day prescription to every child. See 13C for diagnosis, age/site-specific durations, collection thresholds, and recurrent-disease management. [AAP final 2026 UTI guideline](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2026-078565/209460/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis-and)

AAP recommends **renal/bladder ultrasound after a first febrile UTI in children younger than five**; a normal ultrasound does not exclude reflux. The separately named NICE pathway instead images selected first episodes according to age/atypical features, including ultrasound within six weeks below six months even with a good response. Do not mix their age boundaries into one schedule. Poor flow, sepsis, renal impairment, recurrence, or poor response requires prompt pediatric reassessment. Address constipation and bladder-bowel dysfunction. [AAP imaging recommendation 4](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2026-078565/209460/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-the-Diagnosis-and), [NICE NG224 imaging](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng224/chapter/Recommendations)

At reassessment, review symptoms, intake, function, medication access/tolerance and culture support. Persistent illness suggests an inactive drug, impaired drainage, wrong site or alternative diagnosis, not automatically a need for longer treatment. Routine test-of-cure is usually unnecessary after resolved nonpregnant uncomplicated UTI; pregnancy and complex settings differ.

Assign pending-culture review, give urgent return precautions, and ensure follow-up of hematuria or other unexplained abnormalities.
