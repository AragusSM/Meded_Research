# Medivale Family Medicine

## 06B. Hematuria, Proteinuria, and Stones

Blood and protein in urine are findings, not diagnoses. Establish the specimen abnormality, assess urgency, then choose renal, urologic or combined evaluation. Normal creatinine does not exclude a bladder lesion, early glomerular disease or unilateral obstruction; with stones, safe drainage is the immediate concern.

The **2025 AUA/SUFU adult microhematuria framework**, KDIGO CKD guidance and stone recommendations guide evaluation. Adult cancer-risk assessment does not replace pediatric or emergency care. See 06A for infection/specimens and 06C for longitudinal CKD treatment.

### Confirm blood and identify the immediate danger

Gross hematuria is visible blood; microscopic hematuria requires laboratory examination. Foods, drugs, bilirubin, hemoglobin and myoglobin can also discolor urine. Confirm dipstick heme with microscopy. Few or no RBCs suggests pigment, cell lysis or specimen problems. Severe muscle pain after heat exposure warrants rhabdomyolysis and kidney/electrolyte assessment. [NIDDK hematuria overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/hematuria-blood-urine)

The AUA definition is **at least 3 RBCs per high-power field on one properly collected microscopic specimen**. Dipstick alone is insufficient. Obtain a history of visible bleeding, pain, dysuria, menstruation or other genital bleeding, exercise, trauma, recent instrumentation, medications, and previous urinary findings. Measure blood pressure and assess renal function. Smoking exposure and occupational or treatment-related carcinogen exposure matter even when the patient has no pain. An anticoagulant can uncover bleeding from an underlying lesion; its use does not remove the need for appropriate evaluation. [AUA/SUFU microhematuria guideline, amended 2025](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/MH%20Unabridged%2002.2025FINAL3-1.pdf)

Assess hemodynamic status, ability to void, urine output, pain, fever, and evidence of acute kidney injury before arranging routine outpatient testing. Heavy bleeding with instability or symptomatic anemia requires emergency care. **Clots with inability to void are an emergency**, because retained clots can obstruct the bladder outlet. Management may require a suitable large-bore catheter, clot evacuation/irrigation, and urgent urologic help; persistent obstruction is not managed by simply encouraging more water. Suspected urethral injury changes catheter decisions. [AUA emergency curriculum](https://www.auanet.org/Documents/education/medical%20student%20curriculum/Urologic-%20Emergencies-AUA-Med-Student.pdf)

### Distinguish a temporary finding from an unresolved problem

Menstrual contamination, vigorous exercise, urinary instrumentation, or a documented symptomatic infection may explain an episode. Obtain a better specimen or repeat microscopy after the identified cause resolves. A repeat test is a deliberate checkpoint, not a way to defer unexplained bleeding indefinitely. After treated infection, verify that associated hematuria resolves. The AUA discussion allows recovery time after a nonmalignant cause, generally at least three weeks and no more than three months after resolution; this is not a reason to delay evaluation of gross bleeding, obstruction, or concerning renal findings. [AUA repeat-testing guidance](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/MH%20Unabridged%2002.2025FINAL3-1.pdf)

Persistent blood after presumed UTI requires diagnostic reassessment, not repeated antibiotics. Culture when infection is clinically suspected; avoid treating asymptomatic bacteriuria. A small nonobstructing stone may not explain later painless bleeding.

**Clinical Case: transient confounders.** A 42-year-old cyclist provides urine during menstruation after a prolonged ride, without urinary symptoms, hypertension, renal impairment or proteinuria. Repeat a properly collected specimen after these confounders resolve. Persistent microscopic blood needs risk-based evaluation; visible clots or inability to void requires urgent care instead.

### Decide whether bleeding points to the kidney filter

The glomerular barrier normally retains blood cells and most proteins. Dysmorphic RBCs, especially acanthocytes, and RBC casts support glomerular bleeding; albuminuria, hypertension, edema and declining function strengthen concern. Their absence does not exclude glomerular disease. Specimen quality and examiner expertise affect microscopy; no single RBC-shape percentage is universally diagnostic. [NIDDK glomerular disease](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/glomerular-disease)

| Finding pattern | Main interpretation | Consequence for evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dysmorphic RBCs or RBC casts with albumin/protein and renal findings | Glomerular injury is important | Assess severity and arrange nephrology; retain indicated urologic evaluation |
| Blood with colicky flank pain, often relatively uniform RBCs | Stone or another urinary-tract source is possible | Assess drainage, infection, imaging need, and alternative causes of pain |
| Painless visible blood or clots | A urologic lesion must be considered | Prompt urologic evaluation; emergency care if retention or significant bleeding |
| Dipstick heme with few or no RBCs | Pigment, lysis, or a specimen problem is possible | Reconcile microscopy with symptoms and targeted blood testing |

IgA nephropathy may cause visible blood during or near a respiratory illness; infection-related glomerulonephritis may follow infection. Timing suggests a differential, not histology. Family kidney disease, hearing loss or eye abnormalities suggests inherited basement-membrane disease, although family history may be unknown. Purpura, arthritis, neuropathy, sinus or pulmonary symptoms suggests systemic inflammation. [NIDDK IgA nephropathy](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/iga-nephropathy)

**Rising creatinine with an active sediment is not a routine repeat-urine problem.** Reduced urine output, severe hypertension, pulmonary edema, or hemoptysis warrants urgent assessment. Pulmonary hemorrhage with renal injury can represent a pulmonary-renal syndrome even when initial respiratory symptoms appear modest. Anti-GBM disease is one important cause: antibodies target basement-membrane components, and kidney and lung injury can progress quickly. Initial evaluation commonly includes repeat renal/electrolyte studies, urine microscopy and protein quantification, blood count, and targeted complement, autoimmune, or infection testing guided by the syndrome. Nephrology determines biopsy and disease-specific treatment; an indiscriminate panel is not a substitute for recognizing deterioration. Do not begin an unmonitored immunosuppressive course for an unexplained urine result. [NIDDK anti-GBM disease](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/glomerular-disease/anti-gbm-goodpastures-disease)

### Use adult cancer risk to choose urologic testing

After addressing a plausible temporary cause, classify persistent or unexplained adult microhematuria using the complete AUA risk framework. Degree/persistence of blood, age, smoking, prior gross hematuria, and additional urothelial risk factors contribute. Examples include prior pelvic radiation, cyclophosphamide exposure, certain industrial chemical exposures, and relevant family or hereditary cancer history. **Women are not high-risk on age alone.** A renal explanation does not cancel risk-based evaluation for a coexisting urologic lesion. [AUA diagnostic algorithm](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/MH%20Diagnostic%20Algorithm%20updated%203.12.25.pdf)

| Adult microhematuria category | Selected defining features | Usual next pathway |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low/negligible risk: all low-risk criteria required | Women under 60; men under 40; 3-10 RBCs/HPF; never smoker or under 10 pack-years; no added risk factor | Repeat urinalysis within six months rather than immediate cystoscopy/imaging |
| Intermediate risk | Examples include women 60 or older, men 40-59, 11-25 RBCs/HPF, 10-30 pack-years, or other qualifying risk/persistence | Cystoscopy and renal ultrasound |
| High risk | Examples include over 25 RBCs/HPF, over 30 pack-years, prior gross hematuria, or men 60 or older; female age alone is insufficient | Cystoscopy and axial upper-tract imaging, usually CT urography if appropriate |

Apply the full risk table and special-history exceptions; persistent blood can change classification. Renal cell carcinoma family history, hereditary renal tumor syndromes or Lynch syndrome concern can warrant upper-tract imaging independently of the usual category. Cystoscopy examines the bladder lining; ultrasound and CT assess different upper-tract questions and do not automatically replace it. [AUA amendment authors' clinical discussion](https://auanews.net/issues/articles/2025/july/august-2025/aua2025-plenary-recap-microscopic-hematuria-guideline-amendment-refined-risk-stratification-and-introduction-of-urinary-biomarkers)

Urine cytology and tumor-marker tests are not routine substitutes for cystoscopy in every patient. Selected, appropriately counseled intermediate-risk adults who wish to avoid cystoscopy may use cytology or a validated urine marker to inform that decision, while still receiving renal/bladder ultrasound. If cystoscopy is deferred on that basis, repeat urinalysis within 12 months and pursue cystoscopy for persistent microhematuria. A negative marker is not permission to dismiss new visible bleeding. [AUA current marker pathway](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2025%20Guidelines/MH%20Unabridged%2002.2025FINAL3-1.pdf)

CT urography uses a contrast/excretory protocol designed to assess the upper urinary tract; it is not the same study as a noncontrast stone CT. When contraindications prevent the preferred examination, urology and radiology can select MR urography or another combination, including retrograde evaluation with noncontrast imaging or ultrasound. Contrast decisions depend on the actual renal function, acute injury, agent, prior reaction, and expected benefit, not the word "CKD" alone. MRI eligibility depends on the exact implanted device and a safe institutional protocol, not a blanket rule that every pacemaker prohibits MRI. [ACR contrast resources](https://www.acr.org/Clinical-Resources/Clinical-Tools-and-Reference/Contrast-Manual), [ACR/RSNA cardiac MRI safety](https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/cardiacmr)

After a negative risk-appropriate evaluation, use shared decisions about future urinalysis rather than assign every patient indefinite annual procedures. New gross hematuria, materially increased blood, or new urinary symptoms warrants reassessment. For example, a 67-year-old taking apixaban reports two painless episodes of red urine. The medicine is relevant to bleeding severity, but does not identify its source. Arrange appropriate urologic evaluation while assessing stability and the antithrombotic plan. Do not independently stop essential anticoagulation without balancing bleeding and thromboembolic risks.

### Measure protein loss rather than relying on a dipstick grade

Proteinuria reflects glomerular leakage, impaired tubular reabsorption, excess filtered low-molecular-weight proteins or urinary inflammation/bleeding. Dipsticks preferentially detect albumin and depend on concentration, missing some low-level albumin and nonalbumin protein. Disproportionate total protein relative to albumin warrants consideration of tubular disease or a monoclonal process.

Use a quantitative **urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)** for initial albumin assessment, preferably from a first-morning midstream specimen. A protein-to-creatinine ratio measures total protein and answers a related but different question. Confirm a positive screening strip quantitatively and confirm an elevated random UACR with an appropriate subsequent specimen. Ratios reduce the effect of urine dilution, but variation in creatinine generation and excretion can still distort estimates. Timed collection is reserved for situations where it adds useful precision or resolves discordance. [KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline, albumin evaluation](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf)

Use the ADA/KDIGO monitoring-grid convention consistently: **A1 below 30 mg/g, A2 from 30 to below 300 mg/g, and A3 at least 300 mg/g**. These describe albuminuria, not total daily protein or the entire CKD stage. Albumin in mg/dL alone is not the ratio. For example, urine albumin 9 mg/dL and urine creatinine 150 mg/dL give 0.06 mg/mg, or **60 mg/g**, an A2 result. A separate eGFR is needed to characterize filtration. One abnormal sample during illness does not by itself establish chronic kidney disease; chronicity requires evidence extending at least three months. This classification requirement must not delay urgent assessment. [KDIGO classification, chronicity and Figure 13](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf)

Fever, vigorous activity, infection, marked hyperglycemia and contamination can transiently increase albumin excretion. Recheck under suitable conditions. In diabetes, repeated abnormal specimens establish persistence; abrupt heavy loss, active sediment or rapid decline suggests another or additional cause. [ADA 2026 kidney assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690176/)

Albuminuria signals kidney and cardiovascular risk even with preserved eGFR. It reflects filter leakage, not simply dietary protein. Assess cause, blood pressure and medicines and coordinate kidney protection. ACE inhibitor/ARB and SGLT2 eligibility depends on indication and safety; never combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB. See 06C for monitoring, pregnancy restrictions and sick-day decisions. [National Kidney Foundation albuminuria](https://www.kidney.org/kidney-topics/albuminuria-proteinuria)

### Recognize nephrotic presentations and age-specific exceptions

Heavy protein loss accompanied by low serum albumin and edema suggests nephrotic syndrome; hyperlipidemia may accompany it. Adult nephrotic-range proteinuria is conventionally **more than 3.5 g total protein per 24 hours**, not a UACR of 3.5 mg/g or a dipstick grade. Nephrotic-range loss alone is not the complete syndrome. Consider primary glomerular disease and secondary causes such as diabetes, immune disease, infection, drugs, and malignancy. Evaluate thrombosis, infection, and acute kidney injury as well as edema; new dyspnea or unilateral leg swelling needs its own assessment. [KDIGO glomerular-disease guideline, Chapter 1 definitions](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/KDIGO-2021-Glomerular-Diseases-Guideline_English_2024-Chapter-Updates.pdf), [NIDDK adult nephrotic syndrome](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/nephrotic-syndrome-adults)

**Clinical Case: edema with protein loss.** Facial and leg swelling develops over three weeks, with low albumin, marked urinary protein and rising creatinine. A previously normal echocardiogram does not settle the cause. Arrange prompt renal assessment, quantify protein and review systemic symptoms and medicines. Diuresis may relieve overload; biopsy, immune treatment and thrombosis prevention depend on the disease and risk profile.

In an otherwise well adolescent, protein detected during the day but absent in a properly obtained first-morning sample can represent orthostatic proteinuria. Temporary increases also occur during fever or exercise. Reassurance depends on a compatible pattern without hypertension, hematuria, impaired function, or other concerning findings. Persistent first-morning protein, edema, abnormal blood pressure, or systemic illness requires pediatric evaluation; adult cancer-risk tables do not apply. Do not prescribe protein restriction to a growing child merely because a strip is positive. [AAP/ASPN/NKF childhood proteinuria](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/genitourinary-tract/Pages/Proteinuria.aspx)

In pregnancy, new hypertension and proteinuria require obstetric assessment, especially with headache, visual symptoms or upper-abdominal pain. Preeclampsia may occur without proteinuria when qualifying organ injury is present, and can coexist with renal disease. See the reproductive-care chapter for diagnosis and management. [ACOG pregnancy hypertension](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/preeclampsia-and-high-blood-pressure-during-pregnancy)

### Recognize renal colic without anchoring on it

Stones form when concentrated urinary constituents crystallize and persist. Low volume, urine chemistry, infection, stasis and inherited/metabolic disease contribute. Ureteral stones may cause abrupt flank-to-groin pain, nausea, restlessness and hematuria; distal stones may cause urgency or dysuria. Renal stones can be painless, and absent hematuria or atypical pain does not exclude a stone. [NIDDK stone symptoms and causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/symptoms-causes)

Assess vital signs, hydration, renal function, pregnancy possibility, urine findings, and the relevant abdominal/genital examination. Obtain culture when infection is suspected. Consider aortic disease, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian or testicular torsion, appendicitis, pyelonephritis, and other abdominal causes when the history or examination is not convincing. Pain that previously accompanied stones does not guarantee that today's episode is another stone. A solitary functioning kidney, anuria, systemic illness, or deteriorating function lowers the threshold for immediate evaluation. [NIDDK stone diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/diagnosis)

For acute suspected stone disease in a nonpregnant adult when diagnostic imaging is needed, ACR rates **noncontrast abdominal/pelvic CT** as usually appropriate; low-dose techniques can reduce exposure when suitable. It establishes size, location, obstruction, and possible alternative diagnoses. Ultrasound avoids radiation and is particularly useful in pregnancy, childhood, and selected recurrent presentations, but may not visualize a ureteral stone. Plain abdominal radiographs can help follow a known radiopaque stone, but a negative film does not exclude stones. Uric acid stones may be invisible on plain radiographs yet visible on CT. [ACR acute flank-pain criteria, revised 2023](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69362/narrative/)

NICE's acute-colic pathway explicitly recommends urgent imaging, with ultrasound first in pregnancy and in children/young people; persistent pediatric diagnostic uncertainty can justify low-dose CT. Imaging choice should answer the clinical question without delaying emergency treatment. Physiologic collecting-system dilation in pregnancy can complicate interpretation and calls for experienced assessment. Do not repeatedly obtain CT simply because an earlier episode had a stone; review prior images, cumulative exposure, and the current risk. [NICE NG118 assessment and pain recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations)

### Separate observation from the need for drainage

**Infection behind an obstructing stone requires emergency antibiotics and drainage**, usually by ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy. Antibiotics alone may not control an infected closed collecting system. Send appropriate cultures without delaying resuscitation and treatment. Definitive stone fragmentation/removal is generally deferred until the infection is controlled. Anuria or obstruction threatening a solitary kidney also needs urgent specialist care. Fever is not an expected feature to explain away as ordinary colic. [EAU 2026 obstruction and sepsis recommendations](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/guidelines)

In a stable patient without infection, threatened function, uncontrolled symptoms, or another urgent indication, observation can be reasonable for a stone likely to pass. Passage depends on size and location; smaller, more distal stones are generally more favorable. Provide analgesia, nausea management, a strainer if useful, follow-up, and explicit return precautions. NSAIDs are a common first-line colic treatment when safe, but acute kidney injury, volume depletion, bleeding risk, and other contraindications can change the choice. Restore normal hydration rather than forcing large volumes to push a stone through an obstruction. [NIDDK stone treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/treatment)

Selected adults with distal ureteral stones 5-10 mm may be offered an alpha-blocker to facilitate passage under the EAU pathway. This is off-label, is not useful for every renal stone, and can cause dizziness or hypotension. Stop observation and reassess if infection, refractory pain, or renal deterioration develops. Ureteroscopy, shock-wave lithotripsy, and percutaneous removal have different stone-free, retreatment, and complication tradeoffs; size, location, composition, anatomy, and patient circumstances guide specialist selection. [EAU conservative and procedural care](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/guidelines)

**Clinical Case: a small stone with infection.** A patient with a 4-mm ureteral stone returns with rigors, fever, tachycardia and worsening hydronephrosis. Arrange emergency antibiotics and urologic drainage assessment. Small size does not make infected obstruction safe for home passage or expulsive treatment alone.

Pregnancy changes analgesic and procedural decisions. Avoid NSAIDs from about **20 weeks** unless specifically necessary under clinician supervision; avoid at **30 weeks and later** because of added fetal ductal-closure risk. See 07A for monitoring and the prescribed low-dose aspirin exception. Do not transfer an ordinary adult colic prescription to pregnancy. Children, kidney impairment, and anticoagulation also require individual plans. After passage or treatment, confirm that symptoms and obstruction have resolved; pain relief alone does not prove normal drainage. [FDA pregnancy NSAID safety](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic), [EAU stone follow-up](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/followup-of-urinary-stones)

### Prevent recurrence according to the stone and urine chemistry

Retrieve and analyze a passed or removed stone when possible. Review prior events, family history, bowel disease or bariatric surgery, chronic diarrhea, gout, medicines/supplements, infections, and dietary/access constraints. Basic evaluation includes serum testing and relevant urine assessment; elevated serum calcium can prompt parathyroid evaluation. Recurrent stones, early onset, bilateral disease, unusual composition, a solitary kidney, and systemic or inherited risk justify more intensive evaluation. [NIDDK stone types and risk](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/definition-facts)

High-risk stone formers benefit from a specific metabolic workup. EAU recommends two consecutive 24-hour urine collections once clinically stable, free from infection, and on usual intake, rather than interpreting the chemistry of an acute dehydrating episode as baseline. Collections assess volume and constituents such as calcium, oxalate, citrate, uric acid, sodium, and pH; completeness and handling matter. Repeat studies assess response to prevention. Increase fluid intake to produce **more than 2.5 L urine daily** in the EAU prevention pathway, adjusting for heat losses and for heart/kidney conditions that restrict fluids. This is a urine-output goal, not a fixed prescription to drink that amount. [EAU 2026 metabolic evaluation and prevention](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/metabolic-evaluation-and-recurrence-prevention)

| Stone type or setting | Mechanistic clue | Prevention direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcium oxalate | Low volume, excess urinary calcium/oxalate, or low citrate | Preserve appropriate food calcium, reduce excess sodium, and target measured abnormalities |
| Calcium phosphate | Higher urine pH; consider distal tubular acidification defects or hyperparathyroidism | Treat the cause; monitor urine chemistry when using alkali |
| Uric acid | Persistently acidic urine, sometimes excess uric acid | Monitored urine alkalinization is central; address metabolic and dietary contributors |
| Struvite/infection stone | Urease-producing infection and alkaline urine; potentially large branching stones | Specialist clearance and infection/source control, not diet alone |
| Cystine | Inherited amino-acid transport defect, often early/recurrent stones | Intensive dilution, sodium reduction, alkalinization, and selected specialist medicines |

Dietary calcium binds intestinal oxalate, reducing absorption; broad dairy avoidance can worsen calcium oxalate risk and bone health. Reduce excessive sodium and animal protein and tailor oxalate advice to measured excretion. Fat malabsorption may require calcium with meals and coordinated bowel/nutrition care. Avoid high-dose supplements promoted as stone cures. [NIDDK stone nutrition](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/eating-diet-nutrition)

For an appropriate adult, EAU's general diet examples are **1,000-1,200 mg food calcium/day** and **4-5 g salt (NaCl)/day**, not 4-5 g sodium. The latter is about 1.6-2.0 g sodium. Adjust intake to nutritional, growth, kidney, and cardiac needs; the urine-output goal is checked against actual urine collection rather than assumed from the volume drunk. [EAU general preventive measures](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/metabolic-evaluation-and-recurrence-prevention)

Medication prevention should address a documented problem. Thiazides reduce urinary calcium but are not an automatic prescription after every first calcium stone; recurrent-stone benefit and harms require individualized discussion. In the NOSTONE trial, hydrochlorothiazide did not substantially improve the composite recurrence outcome over placebo in the studied recurrent calcium-stone population. Do not confuse a favorable laboratory effect with guaranteed prevention of clinical events, or generalize that trial to every drug and phenotype. [NOSTONE primary trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/nejmoa2209275)

Potassium citrate can help selected hypocitraturic or uric-acid stone formers: citrate binds calcium, while its alkali effect changes urine chemistry. Raising pH can favor calcium phosphate crystallization in a susceptible patient. **Do not use it with hyperkalemia or contraindicated renal impairment.** Active urinary infection and certain gastrointestinal conditions also preclude the cited extended-release formulation. Review the actual label and interactions, and monitor renal function and potassium. A nonprescription potassium product is not a safe substitute for a monitored prescription. [Potassium citrate prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a9cc636d-3e26-450c-9df8-665d530d626f)

Allopurinol is selected for documented hyperuricosuria, not as a universal replacement for correcting acidic urine. Uric acid stones can form without dramatic hyperuricosuria because acidity itself reduces solubility. For adult cystinuria, the EAU prevention pathway uses **urine volume above 3 L/day and monitored urine pH above 7.5**, with specialist-directed thiol treatment when dilution and alkalinization are insufficient. These are cystine-specific goals, not targets for every calcium stone. Renal function, potassium, and volume tolerance may prevent use of an otherwise appropriate alkali/fluid regimen. [EAU uric-acid and cystine prevention](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/metabolic-evaluation-and-recurrence-prevention)

**Clinical Case: calcium avoidance.** A warehouse worker with two calcium oxalate events has low 24-hour urine volume and high sodium intake but has eliminated dairy. Preserve adequate food calcium with meals, reduce sodium, and arrange water access and breaks. Use subsequent urine results and clinical events to decide whether medication is needed.

At follow-up, address water/toilet access, food substitutions, collection logistics, medicine cost and adverse effects. Record stone analysis, residual burden, renal function and prevention, with responsibility for repeat imaging/urine testing. Persistent hematuria requires its own evaluation.
