# Medivale Family Medicine

## 16A. The Primary Care Encounter

### Begin with the patient's agenda and establish urgency

Family medicine combines first-contact assessment with continuity. Ask about the patient's priorities and other concerns before committing the visit to its scheduled purpose. Identify what needs action today, what can safely wait, and how deferred problems will be addressed.

Chest discomfort, severe breathlessness, or a new focal neurologic deficit may turn a preventive visit into an acute assessment. Determine urgency from onset, trajectory, associated findings, function, and risk rather than the appointment label. Longstanding symptoms do not automatically require emergency testing, but reassurance needs a reasoned assessment and plan.

A focused visit answers a defined clinical question; a comprehensive visit addresses broader preventive, chronic, and functional concerns. Both require relevant context and priorities, not indiscriminate testing. Arrange another encounter for nonurgent issues that cannot be completed safely.

**Clinical case.** A 61-year-old scheduled for knee pain and disability paperwork reports new breathlessness crossing the parking lot. Assess the breathlessness first, then arrange explicit follow-up for the knee and paperwork.

### Develop a problem representation without erasing the person's context

Build a concise clinical summary from the time course, relevant findings, important exposures, medications, and the features that distinguish plausible alternatives. Separate observations from interpretation. "The patient reports dizziness after standing" preserves information; "anxiety-related dizziness" prematurely assigns a cause. Ask what the patient thinks is happening, what is most worrying, and what the symptom prevents them from doing. Their explanation can reveal a missed exposure, an unspoken fear, or a practical treatment goal.

A biopsychosocial assessment considers illness mechanisms, emotional responses, relationships, work, housing, and resources together. This does not mean attributing unexplained symptoms to stress. Social or emotional difficulties can coexist with serious organic disease. Equally, repeated normal tests do not make suffering unreal. Explain what is known, what remains uncertain, and which change in the course would warrant a different assessment.

Continuity reveals changes in function and treatment response. Reconsider diagnoses when the expected course fails to occur; do not promote a provisional label to fact by copying it forward. After hospital or specialist care, verify treatment changes and pending tasks.

Useful documentation links each problem to the reasoning and next action: the working explanation, dangerous alternatives considered, targeted evaluation, treatment, and reassessment plan. A long copied history is not a substitute for this connection. Record the patient's priorities and the reason for a decision, especially when a usual pathway is modified by a preference, contraindication, competing illness, or access barrier.

### Shared decisions require a recommendation and understandable choices

Shared decision-making compares reasonable options, including observation when appropriate. Explain benefits, harms, and uncertainty; offer a reasoned recommendation, elicit preferences, and agree on a plan. Listing options without guidance is insufficient. [AHRQ SHARE framework](https://www.ahrq.gov/sdm/share-approach/index.html)

Patients differ in how much numerical detail they want, but the language should remain interpretable. Use a consistent time horizon and denominator when discussing absolute benefit. Distinguish improvement in a laboratory value from prevention of a meaningful outcome. If an option is not appropriate because of a safety concern, explain that concern rather than presenting it as an equally acceptable choice. Urgent stabilization and the capacity to consent require their own assessment; detailed ethics follows in section 16C.

Use plain language in manageable portions. **Teach-back** asks patients to explain the plan in their own words; demonstration checks device or dose-measurement skills. Re-explain and check again when needed. The purpose is to improve communication, not test or blame the patient. [AHRQ teach-back](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)

**Clinical case.** Teach-back reveals that a patient plans to measure blood pressure only during headaches. Clarify the monitoring schedule, demonstrate the process, and check understanding again.

### Understand treatment use before calling it treatment failure

When control is poor, first determine what the patient is actually doing. Ask about recent doses in a specific, nonjudgmental way. Distinguish an informed decision not to use treatment from an inability to obtain it, forgetfulness, a misunderstood schedule, an adverse effect, a device problem, or a competing daily demand. Each needs a different response. A refill history can provide clues, but neither a prescription nor a filled bottle proves correct use.

Simplify the plan when clinically appropriate and connect administration to a feasible routine. Confirm that the patient can read the label, open the container, measure a liquid, use the device, and store the product as required. Involve a caregiver with the patient's agreement when help is needed. Teach-back and demonstration are particularly useful when there has been a change of formulation or device. [AHRQ support for correct medication use](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool16.html)

Ask about cost explicitly. Coverage, transportation, pharmacy availability, food, and time away from work can determine whether a plan is possible. Check suitable lower-cost alternatives and available assistance rather than assuming a generic is always affordable. A less expensive option must still fit the indication, patient factors, and required monitoring. [AHRQ medication affordability](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool19.html)

Create a small, observable action with the patient: what will happen, when, and what could interfere. Adjust an unrealistic goal before the patient leaves. Follow-up should assess both the clinical response and whether the agreed action was feasible. A plan is not collaborative merely because the patient nodded during its explanation. [AHRQ action planning](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool15.html)

**Clinical case.** A worker cannot collect medication during pharmacy hours and is mislabeled as refusing treatment. Arrange accessible dispensing and follow-up rather than increase a dose the patient has never taken.

### Use qualified language support and include family deliberately

Ask which language the patient prefers for conversation and written information. Arrange a qualified interpreter or appropriately qualified bilingual clinician rather than relying on basic conversational ability. Speak directly to the patient, use short portions of speech, and allow interpretation and questions. Written translation, spoken interpretation, and communication accommodations for hearing or vision impairment are related but distinct services. [AHRQ language access](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool9.html)

Family members can provide support and collateral history with permission, but their presence may affect privacy or the patient's willingness to discuss sensitive concerns. Offer private conversation and a qualified interpreter even when a relative is present. In covered programs, required language assistance is **free, timely, and accurate**; do not require patients to bring or pay for their own interpreter. Reliance on a child is restricted to a temporary emergency measure when an imminent threat exists and no qualified interpreter is immediately available; the qualified interpreter must subsequently confirm or supplement that communication. Adult-companion exceptions also have specific safeguards, not merely a relative's willingness to help. Language assistance extends to results, scheduling, and instructions. Verify the covered setting and applicable requirements. [HHS language access information](https://www.hhs.gov/civil-rights/for-individuals/special-topics/limited-english-proficiency/index.html) and [45 CFR 92.201](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-C/section-92.201)

**Clinical case.** An adult arrives with a teenage daughter offering to interpret. Arrange qualified language support, agree on the daughter's role with the patient, and allow private questions.

### Safety-netting makes uncertainty actionable

When outpatient observation is appropriate, explain the working assessment and what change would challenge it. Specify which symptoms require emergency help, which require same-day contact, what improvement is expected, and when reassessment is needed if improvement does not occur. "Return if worse" leaves the patient to decide what worse means and how urgently to act. Choose instructions relevant to the actual problem, not an indiscriminate list of every possible emergency.

Telephone and video encounters require explicit attention to what cannot be assessed remotely. Confirm a usable contact method and the patient's location when an urgent disposition may be necessary. If a reliable history, examination, or monitoring plan cannot be obtained, arrange the appropriate in-person assessment. Document the limitations rather than recording unobserved findings as normal. The medium changes the available evidence, not the obligation to recognize risk.

For tests, establish who reviews the result, how the patient will receive it, and what happens if it is not received. A normal result also needs interpretation in light of the original problem; it does not automatically exclude every concerning diagnosis. AHRQ follow-up guidance emphasizes clear communication and systems that identify unfinished work. [AHRQ patient follow-up](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool6.html)

A referral should communicate the question, urgency, relevant records, and the role of the referring team while the patient waits. Confirm that the referral was received and completed, obtain the consultant's findings, and identify who will act on them. Ask about barriers when a referral remains incomplete. Ordering it is the start of the process, not evidence that specialty care occurred. [AHRQ referral coordination](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool21.html)

| Before the patient leaves | The practical question |
| --- | --- |
| Working assessment and uncertainty | What do we think is happening, and what is not yet established? |
| Agreed action | Can the patient describe and realistically complete the next step? |
| Monitoring or pending result | Who checks it, when, and how will the patient hear? |
| Safety-net | Which change needs emergency help, earlier contact, or planned reassessment? |
| Deferred concern | When and with whom will it be addressed? |
