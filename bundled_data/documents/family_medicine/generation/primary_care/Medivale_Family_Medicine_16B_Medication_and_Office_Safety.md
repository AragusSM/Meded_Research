# Medivale Family Medicine

## 16B. Medication and Office Safety

Apply these safety principles alongside the disease-specific regimen and current product labeling. Dose calculations alone do not establish that a treatment is appropriate.

### Reconciliation establishes what the patient is actually exposed to

Medication reconciliation compares intended treatment with actual use and resolves discrepancies. Include prescriptions from all clinicians, nonprescription combinations, vitamins, supplements, topical agents, injections, and borrowed medicines, whether scheduled, occasional, recently stopped, or changed. Check for the same ingredient under different brand names.

Record the product, formulation, strength, route, amount per dose, frequency, indication, and actual use. Clarify the last dose when it affects an acute decision. Compare patient or caregiver accounts with bottles, pharmacy information, discharge records, and the chart; each can be incomplete. Ask patients to bring their medicines when the list is uncertain. An electronic list copied forward is not proof of an accurate regimen. [AHRQ brown-bag medication review](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool8.html)

At transitions, check for replaced drugs still being refilled and temporary treatments continuing without indication. Clarify intentional changes, resolve discrepancies, and communicate the updated list to patients and clinicians. [AHRQ shared medication-list process](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/reports/engage/medlist.html)

**Clinical case.** A 76-year-old with postdischarge lightheadedness takes both a replacement antihypertensive and the old prescription. Assess stability, clarify the intended regimen, and communicate the corrected list to the patient and pharmacy.

### Separate adverse effects, allergy, and error

An adverse effect can occur during appropriate prescribing. An error is a preventable failure in the medication-use process; it may or may not cause injury. These categories overlap but are not interchangeable. A harmful interaction may reflect a missed safety check, while a new reaction despite appropriate checks does not by itself establish an error. Immediate care should depend on the patient's condition, not on waiting to settle that classification. [AHRQ medication errors and adverse events](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/medication-errors-and-adverse-drug-events)

For a suspected drug reaction, establish the sequence: starting or stopping a product, dose or formulation change, symptom onset, concurrent illness, and other exposures. Consider both medication-related and unrelated causes. Improvement after withdrawal supports an association but does not prove it. Deliberate re-exposure is not an acceptable diagnostic experiment when a serious reaction is possible. Record the phenotype and timing rather than a vague entry that the patient is "allergic."

For a penicillin-allergy label, ask what happened, after how many doses, how long ago, what treatment was needed, and whether related drugs were later tolerated. Isolated gastrointestinal intolerance, an unclear childhood rash, immediate anaphylaxis, and a severe delayed reaction imply different risks. Selected low-risk patients may undergo a protocol-based evaluation in an appropriately equipped setting. **Severe delayed reactions**, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS, or drug-related organ injury, are not indications for routine skin testing or direct oral challenge; avoid the offending drug and obtain specialist assessment. [CDC penicillin allergy assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-signs/)

**Clinical case.** Nausea alone and hospitalization for blistering with oral lesions imply different antibiotic risks. Clarify the first label; do not use a routine low-risk challenge pathway for the second.

### Predict interactions by mechanism, then check the actual pair

**Pharmacokinetic interactions** change drug exposure through absorption, metabolism, transport, or elimination. An inhibitor of a metabolic pathway may increase exposure to an active drug using that pathway; an inducer may reduce it. Prodrugs can behave differently because activation itself may be affected. The clinical consequence depends on the actual product, pathway, exposure change, and therapeutic margin. FDA's interaction resource is a guide to selected CYP and transporter interactions, not a complete interaction checker or a substitute for product labeling. [FDA interaction mechanisms and examples](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-interactions-labeling/healthcare-professionals-fdas-examples-drugs-interact-cyp-enzymes-and-transporter-systems)

For example, grapefruit can inhibit intestinal **CYP3A4**, increasing exposure to **simvastatin** and the risk of muscle toxicity. The effect is product-specific, not a reason to label every statin or every fruit unsafe. Severe muscle pain, weakness, or dark urine warrants assessment for rhabdomyolysis and kidney injury; see 02C. [FDA grapefruit interactions](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/grapefruit-juice-and-some-drugs-dont-mix)

**Pharmacodynamic interactions** change the combined effect without necessarily changing concentrations. Opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, and other central nervous system depressants can produce dangerous additive sedation and respiratory depression. A patient taking familiar individual doses may still develop toxicity from a new combination. Difficulty arousing the patient or impaired breathing requires urgent assessment, not a routine medication review appointment. [FDA benzodiazepine safety warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-requiring-boxed-warning-updated-improve-safe-use-benzodiazepine-drug-class)

**Warfarin plus aspirin or an NSAID** adds bleeding risk through platelet and gastrointestinal effects as well as any effect on anticoagulation. A therapeutic INR does not exclude serious bleeding. Assess melena or hematemesis urgently rather than waiting for an INR to become abnormal; overt bleeding does not require a positive screening stool test. Major-bleeding and reversal pathways belong in 02F and 05C. [Current warfarin labeling, bleeding and interactions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8ee94789-72d2-4c42-9dbd-0501e9767d97), [NLM warning symptoms](https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a682277.html)

Kidney-related interactions are especially relevant during volume depletion. NSAIDs can contribute to acute kidney injury when a patient is dehydrated or has low blood pressure, including patients taking medicines that affect renal hemodynamics. Ask about nonprescription ibuprofen or naproxen, not just prescription pain treatment. Establish an individualized illness plan rather than giving a blanket instruction to stop all chronic medicines whenever someone feels unwell. [NIDDK medication and kidney safety](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/keeping-kidneys-safe)

| Safety problem | What must be clarified before the next prescription |
| --- | --- |
| Duplicate active ingredient or therapeutic duplication | Whether both products are intended, including nonprescription combinations. |
| Altered exposure from an interaction | The specific pair, current labeling, patient factors, and monitoring or alternative needed. |
| Additive sedation or respiratory effects | All sedating products, alcohol exposure, current symptoms, and overdose risk. |
| Kidney risk during acute illness | Hydration, blood pressure, kidney-function trend, nephrotoxic exposure, and the individualized plan. |

### Adjust for kidney function, liver disease, and changing physiology

For renally cleared medicines, use current kidney function and the method relevant to the product's dosing instructions. A laboratory eGFR indexed to **1.73 square meters** and an absolute estimate in **mL/min** are not automatically interchangeable in people with markedly different body size. Near a critical cutoff, or when creatinine is unreliable, a more accurate assessment may be needed. Low muscle mass can make creatinine deceptively reassuring. NIDDK notes that labeling has used different kidney-function methods; there is not one universal equation mandated for every drug. [NIDDK drug dosing in CKD](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/research-funding/research-programs/kidney-clinical-research-epidemiology/laboratory/ckd-drug-dosing-providers)

During acute illness, reassess kidney-function trends, hydration, and course rather than rely on a previously stable value. Product-specific adjustment may change dose, interval, monitoring, or suitability; it does not uniformly mean halving the dose.

Liver dosing depends on diagnosis, severity, clinical findings, interactions, and product-specific hepatic-impairment instructions. Aminotransferases reflect injury, not universal drug clearance. Obtain pharmacy or specialist input when needed rather than invent a proportional adjustment.

Older adults may have reduced clearance, greater drug sensitivity, orthostasis, cognitive effects, and treatment burden. Base prescribing on function, physiology, and indication, not age alone. See chapter 13 for prescribing in older adults and chapter 6 for kidney disease.

### Pregnancy, lactation, and pediatric dosing are separate safety assessments

Pregnancy assessment considers the indication, gestational timing, evidence for fetal effects, maternal consequences of untreated disease, and reasonable alternatives. FDA prescription labeling uses narrative **pregnancy, lactation, and reproductive-potential** information rather than the former pregnancy letter categories. A single letter is not an adequate current prescribing assessment. Lactation adds questions about transfer into milk and effects on the specific infant; a pregnancy risk does not automatically imply the same lactation risk. [FDA pregnancy and lactation labeling](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/labeling-information-drug-products/pregnancy-and-lactation-labeling-resources)

Do not abruptly withdraw essential therapy on discovering pregnancy without considering the consequences and arranging a safer plan. Some exposures need urgent specialist input, but the immediate task is a defined benefit-risk assessment, not a promise that any medicine is universally safe. Drug-specific counseling and preconception changes belong in chapter 12.

For children, verify weight in kilograms, indication, age eligibility, dose units, concentration, maximum allowed dose, interval, and administration device. **mg/kg/day** is not the same as **mg/kg/dose**. Check the total daily amount as well as the individual dose. In liquid prescriptions, write the volume in milliliters with the concentration and ensure the caregiver can demonstrate measuring it. A household spoon is not a reliable measuring device. Check for duplicate ingredients in other products. [FDA pediatric medicine safety](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/safe-use-over-counter-otc-medicines-children/got-sick-kid-dont-guess-read-label-make-sure-youre-giving-your-children-right-medicine-and-right)

**Dose calculation.** A prescription specifies **10 mg/kg per dose** for a **20 kg** child using **100 mg/5 mL** liquid: 200 mg per dose, or 10 mL. Separately verify indication, age, schedule, and maximum dose. An instruction of 10 mg/kg **per day** gives a different daily total; administering 10 mL repeatedly could be excessive.

### Deprescribing is a monitored treatment decision

At each structured review, establish which medicines still have an indication, whether benefit is occurring, what burden or harm is present, and what matters to the patient. Consider a **prescribing cascade** when a new medicine treats a symptom that may have been caused by an earlier one. The answer may be to change the original treatment rather than add another drug. However, a suspected medication effect must not become a reason to ignore a dangerous alternative diagnosis.

Prioritize the change with the clearest expected benefit and agree on what will be monitored. Update the patient-facing list and communicate changes across prescribers and pharmacies. Symptoms after a reduction may represent withdrawal, recurrence of the treated illness, or a separate condition. The response and timeline matter. Removing a medicine from the electronic list without discussing actual use is not deprescribing. [AHRQ deprescribing principles](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/deprescribing-patient-safety-strategy)

Benzodiazepine physical dependence can occur with prescribed use; abrupt discontinuation or rapid reduction can cause serious withdrawal. FDA advises an individualized gradual taper rather than a standard schedule suitable for everyone. This does not mean continuing an unsafe sedative combination without assessment. Balance current toxicity, withdrawal risk, and the support needed for a safer plan. [FDA benzodiazepine withdrawal precautions](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-requiring-boxed-warning-updated-improve-safe-use-benzodiazepine-drug-class)

**Clinical case.** Before renewing a sedative started during a bereavement years ago, assess current benefit, daytime sedation, other depressants, and concerns about change. If reducing treatment, arrange an appropriate taper, symptom support, and reassessment rather than stop abruptly.

### Respond to harm before debating blame

First assess and treat the patient, stop ongoing unsafe exposure when appropriate, and obtain urgent assistance if needed. Preserve an accurate timeline and relevant medication information. Notify the appropriate clinical team and institutional safety process. Document what occurred and the patient's condition objectively; do not alter the record to hide an event or speculate about another person's motives.

Explain the facts known so far, the immediate care plan, what remains uncertain, and how further information will be shared. Offer an honest acknowledgment and appropriate apology without unsupported claims about causation. Disclosure and investigation are ongoing processes, not a one-time conversation postponed until every detail is known. AHRQ's CANDOR framework supports timely communication after harm. [AHRQ communication after adverse events](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/candor/grand-rounds.html)

Investigate contributing systems as well as individual actions: confusing labels, interrupted workflows, duplicate orders, poor handoffs, or unclear responsibility. A reminder to be careful is rarely a complete corrective action. Define a change that addresses the identified failure and check whether it works. [AHRQ systems-focused event investigation](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/candor/modules/guide4.html)

FDA MedWatch accepts reports of serious suspected reactions and certain product or use problems; reporting does not establish causation or replace clinical care and local incident reporting. Use the appropriate reporting system for the product, including the separate vaccine pathway where applicable. [FDA serious-problem reporting](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medwatch-fda-safety-information-and-adverse-event-reporting-program/reporting-serious-problems-fda)

### Office procedures require selection, preparation, and aftercare

Before an office procedure, confirm indication, appropriate setting and expertise, allergies, bleeding-risk medicines, comorbidities, and ability to complete aftercare. Verify patient, procedure, site, consent, and equipment. These safety checks adapt operating-team principles to the office setting. [WHO procedural safety framework](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/safe-surgery-saves-lives-frequently-asked-questions)

Standard precautions apply to all patients. Use hand hygiene, appropriate protective equipment, and properly cleaned, disinfected, or sterilized equipment according to its use. Gloves do not replace hand hygiene. Anticipate respiratory symptoms or other exposures that require additional precautions rather than waiting until after close contact. [CDC standard precautions](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/basics/standard-precautions.html)

Use a new sterile needle and syringe for each patient. Changing the needle does not make a used syringe safe for another patient or for re-entering a shared vial. Do not share single-dose containers or pool their leftovers. Prepare medications in a clean area and handle multidose products according to the applicable requirements. [CDC injection safety](https://www.cdc.gov/injection-safety/hcp/clinical-safety/index.html)

For a wound, assess mechanism, contamination, bleeding, function, sensation, perfusion, possible foreign material, and deeper structure involvement before choosing office care. Review tetanus protection using wound type and immunization history, as outlined in volume 1B. Tendon, nerve, vascular, joint, or other deep injury may require specialist assessment rather than simple closure. Detailed wound examination, closure, bites, and infection management are developed in chapters 7-9.

Plan specimen handling and result follow-up before sending tissue or fluid. Aftercare should explain the expected course, dressing or activity instructions, how and when to reassess, and specific concerning changes. Document the procedure, findings, complications, and follow-up responsibility. A technically completed procedure with an untracked pathology result is not completed care.

### Postoperative fever and deterioration: assess the patient, not a calendar mnemonic

Ask what operation occurred, when, whether implants or drains are present, what happened during admission, and which medications changed. Establish the temperature history and symptoms, then assess vital signs and the relevant systems. Postoperative timing helps organize a differential but does not establish its cause. A normal-looking skin incision does not exclude a deeper problem, and the absence of a high fever does not prove stability.

Early fever can reflect the inflammatory response to tissue injury, but timing alone cannot establish a benign cause. Select investigations from the patient's trajectory and findings rather than ordering the same cultures and images for every temperature elevation. Important patterns include:

| Postoperative findings | Concern and immediate direction |
| --- | --- |
| Increasing abdominal pain, guarding, tachycardia, or hypotension after abdominal surgery | Leak or intra-abdominal infection; urgent surgical assessment and resuscitation, even with a clean skin incision. |
| New dyspnea, pleuritic pain, hypoxemia, or unilateral leg swelling | VTE is possible; urgent evaluation, not attribution to routine postoperative fever. See 02F. |
| New cough, hypoxemia, or focal lung findings | Assess pneumonia and other respiratory causes. Witnessed aspiration alone does not establish bacterial infection; see 03D. |
| Catheter use with new flank/suprapubic symptoms or otherwise unexplained systemic illness | Evaluate urinary infection and obstruction; obtain an appropriate culture. Pyuria or bacteriuria alone does not diagnose symptomatic infection. See 06A. |
| Purulent drainage, worsening wound pain, or spreading inflammation | Assess superficial versus deep infection and need for drainage/source control; antibiotics alone may be inadequate. |

Remove unnecessary devices and communicate findings directly to the operative team. Do not assume a pending surgical follow-up appointment will address new deterioration. [CDC catheter culture stewardship](https://www.cdc.gov/uti/hcp/clinical-guidance/culture-stewardship-strategize.html), [IDSA surgical-site infection care](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/skin-and-soft-tissue-infections/)

During or shortly after triggering anesthesia, unexpected hypercarbia, tachycardia, rigidity, and rising temperature raise concern for **malignant hyperthermia**. Activate the anesthesia emergency response, stop volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine, provide oxygen and **IV dantrolene**, and arrange critical care. This is not an outpatient fever-observation pathway. [MHAUS emergency treatment](https://www.mhaus.org/healthcare-professionals/managing-a-crisis/)

New dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, collapse, significant bleeding, or systemic deterioration requires urgent assessment. Wound redness, drainage, separation, and worsening pain need prompt, severity-based review. Tailor follow-up to the procedure and patient. [ACS postoperative warning signs](https://www.facs.org/for-patients/the-day-of-your-surgery/breast-cancer-surgery/after-your-operation/call-your-surgeon/)

For an established surgical-site infection, drainage or other source control may be necessary; antibiotics alone do not substitute for assessing the wound and deeper involvement. Systemic illness, rapidly progressive infection, or suspected necrotizing infection requires escalation and surgical involvement. Antibiotic selection depends on the operation, likely organisms, severity, and patient factors, not simply the word postoperative. [IDSA surgical-site and soft-tissue infection guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/skin-and-soft-tissue-infections/)
