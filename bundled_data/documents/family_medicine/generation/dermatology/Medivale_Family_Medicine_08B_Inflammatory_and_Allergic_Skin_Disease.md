# Medivale Family Medicine

## 08B. Inflammatory and Allergic Skin Disease

Inflammatory skin disease often needs long-term control rather than a short curative prescription. Identify the process, restore the barrier, treat inflammation safely, and address exposures and extracutaneous disease. Separate flare treatment from maintenance and tailor the regimen to the patient.

A small affected area can still cause major disability when it involves hands, eyelids, genitals, or sleep-disrupting itch. Treatment strength, vehicle, and duration must fit the site and age; a class recommendation does not make every preparation interchangeable.

### Establish the inflammatory pattern and identify dangerous exceptions

Identify eczema, persistent plaques, transient wheals, follicular lesions, or itch without primary change. Ask how long each lesion lasts. Review medicines, topicals, work/wet exposure, cosmetic practices, household symptoms, and treatment. Examine scalp, nails, folds, and relevant untreated sites.

Assess itch, pain, sleep, attendance, and priorities as well as surface area. In darker skin, violaceous/gray-brown change, scale, edema, warmth, and palpation may reveal activity. Pigment can persist after control and should not alone prompt stronger steroids. Adapt safety assessment for infants, older adults, pregnancy, and immune suppression.

Skin pain, rapidly progressive blistering, extensive detachment, mucosal erosion, fever with systemic illness, or suspected infection requires a different urgent pathway. Tongue or throat swelling, stridor, wheeze, circulatory compromise, or rapidly evolving multisystem symptoms should not be managed as routine outpatient dermatitis or uncomplicated hives. Do not wait for a perfect label before protecting the airway or treating suspected anaphylaxis.

| Dominant pattern | Useful context | Important alternative or complication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recurrent itchy dry plaques | Age pattern, barrier impairment, atopic history | Contact allergy, scabies, tinea, secondary infection |
| Exposure-linked dermatitis | Wet work, products, gloves, adhesives | Mixed irritant/allergic disease; immediate latex allergy |
| Persistent scaly plaques | Extensor/scalp/nail findings | Psoriasis, fungal disease, atypical persistent lesion |
| Transient wheals or deep swelling | Individual-lesion duration and systemic symptoms | Anaphylaxis, bradykinin swelling, vasculitis |

### Atopic dermatitis involves barrier failure and inflammatory itch

**Atopic dermatitis (AD)** is relapsing inflammation with barrier dysfunction, water loss, irritant entry, and an itch-scratch cycle. Personal/family atopy supports but is not required for diagnosis. AD is neither contagious nor proof of food allergy. Sleep loss, distress, and infection can make limited disease important. [NIAMS atopic dermatitis mechanisms and burden](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/atopic-dermatitis)

Infants commonly have facial and extensor involvement, with relative diaper-area sparing; older children often develop flexural disease. Adults may have hand, eyelid, flexural, or more generalized patterns. Distribution is not absolute: papular or follicular variants and different patterns occur across skin tones. Acute disease may weep or form vesicles; chronic rubbing produces lichenification and fissures. The diagnosis is usually clinical. A high total IgE neither establishes the diagnosis nor identifies a food that must be eliminated. [DermNet age-dependent AD presentation and diagnostic limitations](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/atopic-dermatitis)

Use regular fragrance-free emollient, gentle washing, irritant avoidance, and treatment of active inflammation. Ointments suit very dry skin; other vehicles may fit different sites or seasons. Demonstrate quantity/application rather than saying 'use sparingly,' and check access to adequate supplies before assuming nonadherence. [AAP practical eczema treatment and skin care](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/atopic-dermatitis/treatment-of-atopic-dermatitis/)

**Topical corticosteroids** remain central for flares, chosen by site, age, severity, and preparation. A pediatric starting example is **hydrocortisone 1% or 2.5% ointment twice daily** for an infant or facial flare, stopping when the active inflammation improves, usually within days to two to three weeks; persistent disease needs reassessment, not indefinite application. Other sites or more severe disease may need a different potency. **Tacrolimus or pimecrolimus** can provide steroid-sparing control, especially on sensitive sites; burning can occur. Topical PDE-4 and JAK inhibitors have their own restrictions. Wet wraps are selected adjuncts: occlusion increases absorption. Routine topical antimicrobials or antihistamines are not anti-inflammatory treatment for uncomplicated adult AD. [AAP practical flare treatment](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/atopic-dermatitis/treatment-of-atopic-dermatitis/), [AAD adult topical-therapy guideline](https://www.jaad.org/article/S019096222300004X/pdf)

Maintain barrier care and prescribed intermittent anti-inflammatory treatment at recurrent sites, reviewing response, adverse effects, and feasibility. The AAD adult update supports topical **tapinarof** and **roflumilast** and selected biologics including lebrikizumab and nemolizumab with concomitant topical treatment. Confirm diagnosis, formulation/indication, and safe use when selecting alternatives. [AAD focused adult AD update](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0190962225021255)

The **2026 AAD pediatric treatment guidance** uses age-, weight-, body-area-, and product-specific safety limits, not a scaled-down adult schedule. Use prescribed bleach-bath/wet-dressing protocols without improvising concentrations or potent-steroid occlusion. The guideline recommends against systemic corticosteroids for pediatric AD; repeated bursts are not maintenance care. [AAD pediatric AD guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41949508/)

Persistent severe itch, frequent flares, extensive disease, sensitive-site disability, or major sleep impairment warrants dermatologic assessment despite optimized topical care. Dupilumab and other biologics or oral JAK inhibitors are selected according to disease, age, comorbidities, access, and risk. For example, current dupilumab labeling includes inadequately controlled moderate-to-severe AD from **6 months of age**, with age/weight-dependent administration; conjunctivitis or keratitis symptoms require assessment. Do not abruptly stop a patient's other corticosteroid treatment when starting a biologic. Infection, vaccination, reproductive, laboratory, thrombotic, and other safety considerations differ among systemic agents. [Current dupilumab prescribing information](https://www.regeneron.com/downloads/dupixent_fpi.pdf)

For refractory AD, reassess diagnosis, quantities applied, irritant exposure, contact allergy, tinea, infestation, and infection before escalating immunosuppression. The **2026 AAD refractory-AD diagnostic guideline** supports targeted patch testing, scraping, or biopsy. A persistent atypical adult eruption may represent another inflammatory disease or cutaneous lymphoma; an inadequately sampled negative test is not reassuring. [AAD diagnostic testing guideline for refractory presumed AD](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42678322/)

Rapidly worsening painful lesions, clusters of similar vesicles, or punched-out erosions with systemic symptoms raise concern for **eczema herpeticum**, not just a more itchy flare. Suspected disease needs prompt systemic antiviral treatment and same-day specialist assessment; periocular involvement adds urgent ophthalmic assessment. Treat clinically significant bacterial infection when present, but do not prescribe antibiotics for every weeping patch or positive colonization swab. Related cutaneous infections are discussed in 08C. [NICE eczema-herpeticum emergency recommendation](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg57/chapter/Recommendations)

### Contact dermatitis requires removal of the cause, not only suppression

**Irritant contact dermatitis** results from direct barrier injury. Repeated hand washing, detergents, solvents, friction, or prolonged moisture can exceed the skin's capacity to recover. Burning, soreness, dryness, and fissures may dominate, although itch also occurs. No prior sensitization is required. **Allergic contact dermatitis** is a delayed T-cell-mediated response after sensitization; nickel, fragrances, preservatives, rubber accelerators, adhesives, hair dyes, and topical medicines are relevant possibilities. A product tolerated for years can become a problem after sensitization. [DermNet irritant contact dermatitis](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/irritant-contact-dermatitis), [DermNet allergic contact dermatitis](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/allergic-contact-dermatitis)

Relate distribution to exposure. Eyelid allergens may transfer from hands. Gloves can cause irritation, delayed additive allergy, or immediate latex allergy, requiring different tests and risk assessment. Irritant/allergic dermatitis and AD can coexist, and disease may extend beyond the original contact site. [CDC/NIOSH occupational skin-exposure mechanisms](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/skin-exposure/about/)

Reduce exposure, restore the barrier, and treat inflammation by site. Match gloves to chemicals and allergy status; trapped moisture/occlusion can worsen dermatitis. Arrange task modification or occupational assessment and address contaminated tools/clothing/products. Worsening with topical antibiotics or eczema products may indicate contact allergy.

**Patch testing** evaluates delayed contact allergy. It is different from skin-prick testing or a serum specific-IgE panel, which addresses immediate sensitization. Allergen selection, appropriate concentrations, later readings, and the relevance of a positive reaction matter. A positive nickel reaction does not explain every rash unless exposure fits; a negative limited series does not exclude an untested occupational allergen. Persistent, recurrent, atypically distributed, or treatment-resistant dermatitis may justify referral. Do not patch-test a caustic workplace substance undiluted on the patient's skin. [DermNet patch-test interpretation and limitations](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/patch-tests)

**Clinical Case:** A dental assistant with childhood eczema develops fissured hands during frequent glove changes and disinfectant use. Steroids help briefly, but workweek recurrence warrants assessment of wet exposure, glove additives, cleansers, and product allergy, with exposure reduction and patch testing. Immediate glove-related wheeze or generalized hives needs a separate immediate-allergy pathway.

### Seborrheic dermatitis has a different distribution and treatment emphasis

**Seborrheic dermatitis** affects sebaceous sites: scalp, brows, nasolabial folds, ears, and presternal skin. Malassezia and host inflammation contribute; it is not simply contagious fungus. Greasy or fine scale may overlap psoriasis. Cradle cap may lack AD's intense itch. Severe/refractory disease warrants diagnostic and immune/neurologic reassessment without automatically assuming HIV.

Antifungal shampoo suits scalp disease; cream suits facial or truncal disease. One labeled adult option is **ketoconazole 2% cream twice daily for four weeks or until clinical clearing**; reconsider the diagnosis if it does not improve. Avoid the eyes. A short course of a suitable low-potency anti-inflammatory may help a flare, but repeated potent facial steroids are inappropriate. Explain recurrence and a feasible maintenance plan. Gentle infant scalp care differs from this adult regimen, and systemic antifungals are not routine first-line care. [Ketoconazole cream dosing and nonresponse assessment](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=bf3e48fd-e063-42e6-acd1-580ddc6e93be)

### Prescribe topical steroids by preparation, site, quantity, and duration

Potency is not determined by the printed percentage alone. The molecule, vehicle, formulation, and delivery system matter. **Hydrocortisone 1% or 2.5%** is a low-potency example; selected triamcinolone preparations provide mid-potency options, while clobetasol preparations can be superpotent. Ointments, creams, solutions, foams, and lotions are not automatically interchangeable. U.S. seven-class and other countries' classification systems differ, so specify the actual preparation instead of transferring a class number between systems. [DermNet topical steroid pharmacology and potency](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/topical-steroid)

| Clinical setting | Usual prescribing direction | Safety issue to address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Face, eyelids, folds, genital skin | Lower potency and brief, defined use; consider steroid-sparing care | Atrophy, striae, ocular exposure, periorificial eruption |
| Thick localized trunk or limb plaques | An appropriate stronger preparation may be needed | Correct diagnosis, course length, treated area |
| Infant or young child | Age/site-specific lower exposure and caregiver instruction | Greater systemic absorption per body mass |
| Occlusion or large treated surface | Explicit supervised plan rather than automatic intensification | Increased absorption and adrenal suppression |
| Repeated recurrence | Reassess diagnosis and prescribe maintenance deliberately | Avoid both uncontrolled disease and indefinite high-potency use |

Specify site, frequency, quantity, duration, and the next step. Thick plaques may need stronger treatment; thin skin is vulnerable to atrophy, telangiectasia, striae, and acneiform/rosacea-like eruptions. Large quantities, prolonged exposure, occlusion, and childhood use increase systemic absorption and adrenal suppression. Demonstrate use and review response to prevent both toxicity and fear-driven undertreatment. [BAD topical corticosteroid application and counseling](https://www.bad.org.uk/pils/topical-corticosteroids/)

A **fingertip unit (FTU)** is a practical quantity guide from an adult fingertip to the distal crease, dispensed from a standard tube. One adult FTU is approximately **0.5 g**, with variation by finger and nozzle, and covers roughly two adult hand-sized areas including fingers. Use age- and site-specific instructions for children, and check that the quantity dispensed can cover the prescribed area. This is an estimate of amount, not an instruction to use the same steroid everywhere. [DermNet FTU quantity guide](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/fingertip-unit)

**Example:** One adult 0.5-g FTU per application, twice daily for seven days, requires approximately **0.5 x 2 x 7 = 7 g**. First establish product, site, frequency, duration, and all treated areas; arithmetic alone does not establish a suitable prescription.

Product limits override a generic course length. This **clobetasol propionate cream 0.05%** product's label limits treatment to **two consecutive weeks** and **no more than 50 g per week**; it should not be used on the face, groin, or axillae, and use below age 12 is not recommended in that label. Do not extend these exact limits to every clobetasol formulation or interpret the maximum as a target. Persistent disease needs reassessment rather than repeated refills beyond the plan. [Clobetasol cream preparation-specific labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0ccf8ddd-5fd8-48c7-97c9-5450a39c1bcb)

**Clinical Case:** A parent applies a relative's clobetasol 0.05% cream to a toddler's eyelids, believing it weaker than hydrocortisone 1%. Percentages cannot compare different molecules. Stop the inappropriate preparation, assess exposure/adverse effects, and provide a child- and site-specific plan without discouraging treatment of inflammation.

### Psoriasis is a skin and systemic inflammatory disorder

**Psoriasis** involves immune-driven inflammation and accelerated epidermal turnover. Plaque psoriasis commonly produces well-demarcated thickened scaly lesions on extensor surfaces, scalp, or the sacral region, but presentation varies. Inverse psoriasis affects folds and may have little scale; guttate disease produces smaller widespread lesions, sometimes after streptococcal infection. Nail pitting, onycholysis, and subungual change can support the context without being perfectly specific. Erythrodermic or generalized pustular disease with systemic illness requires urgent specialist care, not routine outpatient escalation of a cream. [NIAMS psoriasis mechanisms and presentations](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/psoriasis)

Assess high-impact hand, foot, genital, scalp, and nail disease regardless of BSA. Ask about morning stiffness, swollen digits, enthesitis, inflammatory back pain, and lost function. **Psoriatic arthritis** may precede extensive psoriasis and needs timely evaluation despite negative RF. Review BP, metabolic/cardiovascular risk, smoking/alcohol, mood, IBD, and uveitis symptoms without an indiscriminate test panel. [AAD psoriasis and comorbidity guidance](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/guidelines/psoriasis)

For localized plaques, use site-appropriate topical corticosteroids, often with a vitamin D analog or another steroid-sparing agent. Calcineurin inhibitors may be off-label at sensitive sites. Tailor vehicle/potency to thickness, site, response, and routine; oral vitamin D is not a topical analog substitute. Reassess refractory disease for maintenance changes, phototherapy, or systemic care while respecting product duration limits. [AAD adult topical-psoriasis clinical pathway](https://assets.ctfassets.net/1ny4yoiyrqia/56bNCO2qMLxBtICdr9biM6/204ac5f5d98243e116bce0b5725b9f92/AAD-Guideline-Topical-Psoriasis-Clinical-Pathway-May-2023-Update.pdf)

Extensive, high-impact, or refractory disease may require phototherapy or systemic treatment selected with dermatology. Methotrexate, cyclosporine, retinoids, oral targeted agents, and biologics are not interchangeable. Selection depends on joint disease, pregnancy plans, kidney/liver function, infections, other inflammatory illness, and monitoring feasibility. Cyclosporine requires renal and blood-pressure monitoring; biologics need product-specific infection/vaccination assessment. Coordinate changes to another clinician's systemic treatment. [NIAMS systemic psoriasis treatment considerations](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/psoriasis/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

**Methotrexate for psoriasis is a weekly medicine, not a daily one.** Daily administration in error can be fatal. Reconcile the actual schedule, provide folate as directed, and arrange blood-count, liver, and renal monitoring; pregnancy and important drug interactions must also be addressed. [Methotrexate psoriasis schedule and medication-error warning](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=874c7f43-a3be-4236-89cc-24c44e3c5813)

**Clinical Case:** A delivery driver with two small elbow plaques develops a swollen toe and movement-responsive morning heel pain. Limited skin disease does not establish mild overall disease. Treat the plaques and arrange timely psoriatic-arthritis assessment, including cardiometabolic risk and work limitations.

### Urticaria is defined by transient lesions, not simply itch

**Wheals** are transient raised areas of dermal edema, generally itchy and usually resolving individually within **24 hours** without a persistent scar. New wheals may appear elsewhere. Angioedema is deeper swelling, often involving lips, eyelids, or other soft tissues, and can take longer to resolve. Document photographs if no lesions are present at the visit. Acute episodes may follow infection, medicines, or a specific allergic exposure; chronic spontaneous disease is not usually explained by an undiscovered food allergy. Painful fixed lesions, bruising, systemic symptoms, or a mismatched duration should prompt reconsideration. [DermNet urticaria recognition and differential](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/urticaria-an-overview)

The international framework defines **acute urticaria as six weeks or less** and **chronic urticaria as more than six weeks**, distinguishing spontaneous from reproducibly stimulus-induced disease. Ask about NSAID aggravation and cold-water risks. For chronic spontaneous disease, use history/examination and limited baseline tests such as differential count and inflammatory markers, not broad allergy panels.

Use a **second-generation H1 antihistamine** regularly for persistent disease. If control is inadequate, supervised increase of one agent **up to fourfold its standard dose** is supported before escalation. This is off-label and requires age-, renal-, and product-specific assessment; do not exceed the ceiling or combine agents arbitrarily. Persistent uncontrolled disease warrants specialist add-on assessment. Chronic systemic corticosteroids are not maintenance treatment. [2026 international urticaria guideline](https://doi.org/10.1111/all.70210)

Add-on options include omalizumab, dupilumab, and oral remibrutinib, with different eligibility and risks. U.S. remibrutinib labeling covers **adults with chronic spontaneous urticaria or symptomatic dermographism** despite H1 treatment, not all inducible subtypes. Assess bleeding/antithrombotics, procedures, CYP3A interactions, hepatic impairment, and live vaccines. Dupilumab covers symptomatic CSU from **age 2 years**, distinct from its AD threshold and other urticarias. [Current remibrutinib prescribing information](https://www.novartis.com/us-en/sites/novartis_us/files/rhapsido.pdf), [Current dupilumab indications](https://www.regeneron.com/downloads/dupixent_fpi.pdf)

### Swelling without wheals requires a mechanism and an airway plan

Mast-cell-mediated swelling may accompany hives and can be part of anaphylaxis. **Bradykinin-mediated angioedema** often occurs without wheals or itch and may involve the tongue, upper airway, or abdomen. Consider ACE inhibitors even when taken uneventfully for years, and hereditary or acquired C1-inhibitor disorders when the history fits. Lack of a family history does not exclude hereditary disease. C4 and C1-inhibitor quantity/function testing can help, but a normal isolated screening value does not exclude every form of hereditary angioedema. [DermNet angioedema mechanisms and investigation](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/angioedema)

| Presentation | Mechanism to consider | Immediate implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wheals with otherwise stable symptoms | Often mast-cell-mediated urticaria | Check for systemic progression; appropriate H1 treatment |
| Rapid swelling with respiratory or circulatory symptoms | Possible anaphylaxis | Immediate epinephrine and emergency care |
| Tongue swelling without hives on an ACE inhibitor | Possible bradykinin-mediated disease | Urgent airway assessment; stop causative ACE inhibitor |
| Recurrent swelling/abdominal attacks without wheals | Hereditary/acquired bradykinin pathway possible | Specialist assessment and an attack-specific plan |

For suspected anaphylaxis, give **intramuscular epinephrine promptly** and activate emergency support. Antihistamines do not treat shock or upper-airway obstruction and should not delay epinephrine; absence of hives does not exclude anaphylaxis. Weight/age-based IM dosing and auto-injector selection are addressed in the allergy emergency pathway. In established bradykinin-mediated disease, antihistamines and corticosteroids generally do not reverse the mechanism; urgent airway care and a disease-specific rescue plan are needed. When the mechanism is uncertain, do not withhold treatment for plausible anaphylaxis while investigating. [AAAAI/ACAAI anaphylaxis guidance](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Anaphylaxis-Practice-Paramaters-2023.pdf)

Late-onset ACE-inhibitor tongue swelling still requires emergency airway assessment and drug discontinuation. Section 10C (reading volume 9F) provides the worked case and recurrence counseling.

### Acne therapy should prevent scars and minimize antibiotic exposure

**Acne vulgaris** arises in the pilosebaceous unit through follicular obstruction, sebum-related changes, microbial interactions, and inflammation. Open and closed **comedones** distinguish ordinary acne from many other follicular eruptions; papules, pustules, nodules, scarring, and postinflammatory pigment may coexist. Assess face and trunk, painful lesions, distress, and scars, not just lesion count. Sudden onset, virilization, or other endocrine findings may require a separate evaluation. A uniform medication-related eruption without comedones, folliculitis, and rosacea belong in the differential. [DermNet acne presentation and assessment](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/acne-vulgaris)

For comedonal or mixed disease, a topical retinoid and benzoyl peroxide provide complementary actions. A concrete option is **adapalene 0.1%/benzoyl peroxide 2.5% gel**, labeled from age **nine**, as a thin film to affected face/trunk areas **once daily** after gentle washing; avoid eyes, lips, and mucosa. Moisturizer and reduced frequency when irritation occurs can improve tolerance. Benzoyl peroxide can bleach hair and fabrics. Treat the acne-prone region, not only visible spots, and judge benefit over weeks, not days. Avoid topical retinoids during pregnancy or pregnancy planning. Azelaic acid is another option, including for associated pigment change. [Adapalene/benzoyl peroxide gel: age, application, and irritation precautions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a0610f87-3b39-458a-afa3-66be1fefd1de)

The **2024 AAD acne guideline** supports multimodal topical therapy and limiting systemic antibiotics. Do not use a topical antibiotic alone; pair antibiotic treatment with benzoyl peroxide and appropriate other topical care to reduce resistance. More extensive inflammatory acne may need an oral agent such as doxycycline. AAD patient guidance describes a usual **three-to-four-month** antibiotic course, not a universal mandatory duration: arrange response review and an explicit stop/maintenance plan, rather than automatic refills. Severe acne, accumulating scars, substantial psychosocial burden, or failure of standard treatment can justify **oral isotretinoin** assessment. [AAD acne guideline](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/guidelines/acne), [AAD antibiotic duration and maintenance guidance](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/derm-treat/antibiotics)

Combined oral contraceptives and spironolactone are options for selected patients. Consider thrombotic and other contraceptive contraindications, pregnancy potential, renal function, potassium-related medicines, and the patient's goals. Potassium monitoring is not routinely necessary for every otherwise healthy patient taking spironolactone for acne, but that does not apply unchanged to an older patient with CKD or a potassium-raising drug. Topical clascoterone and other agents have separate evidence and product restrictions. [AAD primary acne guideline: treatment selection and safety](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaad.2023.12.017)

Isotretinoin requires a prescriber-led safety program. It is **contraindicated in pregnancy** and requires the active **iPLEDGE REMS** process, pregnancy prevention/testing when applicable, and counseling. Review lipids and liver tests, relevant mental-health symptoms, mucocutaneous effects, and other patient-specific risks. Avoid concurrent tetracyclines because of intracranial-hypertension risk and avoid additional vitamin A. Products differ in administration and food instructions; do not substitute a generic dosing or monitoring schedule across preparations. [Current isotretinoin capsule labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=28948c32-a598-4bb5-bdb5-efbd87214d98)

As of **October 20, 2026**, FDA lists **November 15, 2026** as the delayed implementation date for the approved iPLEDGE modifications. Until implementation, the existing process and FDA's stated pregnancy-testing enforcement discretion apply; pretreatment pregnancy tests must still occur in a medical setting. Verify the live program at prescribing and dispensing, including testing, authorization windows, and pregnancy-prevention requirements. An approved future change is not permission to bypass today's process. [FDA iPLEDGE implementation notice and interim testing rules](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/ipledge-risk-evaluation-and-mitigation-strategy-rems)

Avoid isotretinoin, spironolactone, and topical retinoids during pregnancy. Review the entire regimen with obstetric/dermatology teams; azelaic acid and selected other topicals may be considered. Tetracyclines require age- and pregnancy-specific assessment and are not routine pregnancy acne treatment. [AAD acne treatment during pregnancy](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/derm-treat/pregnancy)

**Clinical Case:** A 17-year-old with persistent truncal acne and new scars receives repeated antibiotic refills and avoids swimming. Confirm actual use, institute combined rather than antibiotic-only therapy, and assess for isotretinoin. Ask about distress without presuming drug causation, and address preferences and access.

### Rosacea is treated by its manifestations, including the eyes

**Rosacea** causes centrofacial color change, flushing, telangiectasia, papules/pustules, phymatous change, and ocular disease. Comedones suggest coexisting acne. In darker skin, burning, sensitivity, and subtle color changes may aid recognition. Consider seborrheic/contact or steroid-induced dermatitis, acne, and lupus. Nasal appearance or flushing does not establish alcohol misuse. [DermNet rosacea phenotypes and differential](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/rosacea)

Use gentle skin care, photoprotection, and individualized trigger avoidance. Papulopustules may respond to ivermectin, metronidazole, or azelaic acid; selected patients need anti-inflammatory-dose doxycycline or specialist treatment. Persistent erythema may need brimonidine, oxymetazoline, or a procedure. Discuss irritation, rebound/worsening redness, contraindications, and symptom control rather than cure. Avoid routine facial steroids. [AAD manifestation-directed rosacea treatment](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/rosacea/treatment/diagnosis-treat)

Ask about gritty eyes, lid inflammation, recurrent styes, dryness, photophobia, pain, and blurred vision. Mild lid symptoms may benefit from appropriate lid care and coordinated treatment, but **pain, photophobia, or reduced vision needs urgent eye assessment** because corneal disease can threaten sight. Ocular involvement can be important even when skin findings seem limited. Do not prescribe ophthalmic steroids without the relevant eye-care assessment. [DermNet ocular rosacea and corneal complications](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/ocular-rosacea)

### Investigate persistent itch without assuming allergy or a psychiatric cause

**Pruritus** can arise from inflammatory skin disease, infestation, xerosis, systemic disease, medication, neuropathy, or several mechanisms together. Establish whether a primary eruption preceded scratching. Examine beyond excoriations, including scalp, finger webs, nails, and relevant mucosa; household itch and nocturnal symptoms can redirect the evaluation. A patient with only scratch marks may still have an important underlying dermatosis. Older adults commonly have dryness, but age alone should not close the differential. [DermNet pruritus causes and examination](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/pruritus)

Ask about distribution, timing, water or temperature triggers, medicines, constitutional symptoms, jaundice, renal disease, thyroid symptoms, and neurologic sensations. Localized burning or tingling itch may suggest a neuropathic mechanism. Generalized itch with weight loss, fever, lymphadenopathy, or drenching sweats needs directed systemic assessment. In pregnancy, new generalized itch, especially of palms/soles without a primary rash, warrants timely obstetric evaluation for cholestasis and other causes; visible jaundice is not required.

The **2025 European S2k chronic-pruritus guideline** uses history, complete skin/general examination, and staged investigation rather than a universal allergy panel. For persistent unexplained generalized itch, its first-step laboratory framework includes a differential blood count, inflammatory markers, renal and hepatic tests including cholestatic markers/bilirubin, TSH, glucose assessment, ferritin, and LDH. Select further testing from findings and persistence, and interpret abnormalities in context; this does not mean ordering every imaging study or parasite test for every patient. Reassess if the first evaluation is unrevealing, and consider biopsy for a suspected subtle inflammatory or blistering disorder. [Current European S2k pruritus diagnostic framework](https://medicaljournalssweden.se/actadv/article/download/44220/51149)

Treat the cause and relieve symptoms with emollients, cooling, gentle bathing, irritant reduction, and scratch control. Emollient-contaminated fabrics remain a fire hazard even after washing. Antihistamines suit histamine-driven wheals, not every itch; sedating agents risk cognitive impairment, falls, and urinary problems in older adults. Tailor neuropathic, renal, cholestatic, and prurigo treatment to organ function and adverse effects. Offer psychological support and follow-up without dismissing symptoms or repeatedly applying steroids to normal skin. [BAD current pruritus symptom and self-care guidance](https://www.bad.org.uk/pils/pruritus-itching)

**Clinical Case:** A 74-year-old with generalized itch, excoriations, fatigue, and weight loss takes nightly OTC sedating antihistamines and has nearly fallen twice. Reduce unsafe symptom treatment, examine fully, pursue directed systemic investigation, and follow results. Do not attribute constitutional symptoms to xerosis or anxiety.
