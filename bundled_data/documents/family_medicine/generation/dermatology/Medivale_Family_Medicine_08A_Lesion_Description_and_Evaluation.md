# Medivale Family Medicine

## 08A. Lesion Description and Evaluation

Skin findings may reveal local or systemic disease. Use structure, distribution, symptoms, and time course rather than photograph matching: adhesive dermatitis, a changing papule, and widespread painful blisters require different decisions. Establish a differential, test or observation plan, and responsibility for follow-up.

Describe the lesion before naming it. Morphology, distribution, palpation, and change over time determine whether observation, office testing, biopsy, or urgent referral is appropriate.

### Decide whether the patient needs urgent assessment before naming the rash

Assess general appearance, vital signs, pain, progression, mucosal involvement, and relevant immune suppression. Fever with rapidly spreading nonblanching lesions, systemic illness with severe skin pain, rapidly progressive swelling, extensive blistering or detachment, and eye or airway involvement require urgent evaluation. A rash need not look intensely red to be dangerous. Hemodynamic instability, altered mental status, or a threatened airway takes priority over office photography, scraping, or biopsy. The detailed infection and severe drug-reaction pathways are in 08C.

In a stable patient, reconstruct the first lesion and its evolution. Did itch precede the lesion? Do individual spots disappear or persist? Ask about pain, spontaneous bleeding, drainage, ulcers, fever, joint/mucosal symptoms, and sleep/work effects. Build a medication timeline including intermittent antibiotics, OTC products, supplements, injections, and topicals; medicines used for months may still fit a reaction.

Review work, wet exposure, gloves, adhesives, cosmetics/hair products, plants, animals, travel, household itch, relevant sexual exposure, and UV. Ask about skin cancer, immune suppression, radiation/scars, family melanoma, and pathology. Prior steroids can obscure infection; antibiotics and scratching can alter specimens. Distinguish recurrence from a new lesion near an old scar.

With consent and good lighting, inspect relevant sites beyond the presenting lesion. Compare uninvolved skin and the opposite side; measure and palpate depth/consistency. Include scalp, nails, palms, soles, and mucosa as indicated. Record an exact site and, for suspected cancer, surrounding skin and relevant nodes. Biopsy complements clinical description. [DermNet clinician-authored skin-cancer assessment overview](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/skin-cancer)

### Describe the primary structure, then its secondary changes

The **primary lesion** is the basic visible/palpable structure; **secondary changes** follow evolution, scratching, breakdown, treatment, or healing. Record number, size, color, surface, border, configuration, distribution, and palpation. 'Sharply bordered scaly plaques on both extensor elbows' is more informative than 'eczema-like rash.'

Approximately **1 cm** separates several small and large lesion terms in this convention. Terminology varies; actual dimensions and depth matter more at a boundary. Nodules have a deeper component, not simply a large surface area. [ILDS consensus terminology](https://ilds.contentfiles.net/media/documents/ILDS-Glossary-Cutaneous-Lesions.pdf)

| Finding | Structural description | What to record next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Macule / patch | Flat area of altered color; patch denotes a larger area | Pigment, border, scale, measured extent |
| Papule / plaque | Solid palpable lesion; plaque has a broad surface | Height, texture, borders, whether papules merge |
| Nodule | Solid lesion extending into deeper skin or subcutis | Depth, mobility, tenderness, growth |
| Vesicle / bulla | Fluid-filled blister, smaller / larger | Roof, tension, fluid, surrounding skin |
| Pustule | Visible collection of purulent material | Follicular location and associated inflammation |
| Wheal | Transient elevation from dermal edema | Duration of each lesion, itch, associated swelling |
| Erosion / ulcer | Epidermal loss / loss extending into dermis or deeper | Base, edge, depth, tenderness, perfusion |

**Scale** is accumulated surface keratin, whereas **crust** is dried exudate or blood. **Excoriations** are scratch-related injury; **lichenification** is thickening with accentuated skin lines after chronic rubbing. A fissure is a linear crack. Atrophy reflects tissue thinning; a scar records repaired injury. These distinctions affect sampling: an intact active lesion may reveal the initiating disease, while a repeatedly scratched center may show only nonspecific injury. Purpura represents blood outside vessels and does not blanch like ordinary vascular erythema; palpable purpura raises concern for an inflammatory vascular process but is not, by itself, a complete etiologic diagnosis. [DermNet morphology and secondary-change reference](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/terminology)

**Clinical Case:** A warehouse worker's 'hives' persist at the same sites for days and leave bruiselike marks. Assess individual-lesion duration, blanching/palpability, pain, systemic symptoms, and medicines. Fixed bruising lesions require reconsideration and directed investigation rather than indefinite escalation of urticaria treatment.

### Distribution and skin tone modify the interpretation

Record localized/generalized, symmetric/asymmetric, follicular, flexural, extensor, intertriginous, photodistributed, acral, dermatomal, linear, or annular patterns. Configuration describes arrangement; distribution describes body sites. Annular lesions are not necessarily fungal. Linear lesions may reflect exposure, scratching, or development. Compare disease boundaries with the proposed exposure or light-related mechanism.

Distinguish transient wheals, recurrent same-site blisters, slowly enlarging plaques, and rapidly growing nodules. Inspect a new untreated lesion and prior photographs when available. Consented photographs should include overview, close view, scale, lighting, and site. Images document evolution but cannot replace necessary palpation/examination; differing exposures can mimic color change.

In deeply pigmented skin, inflammation may appear violaceous or gray-brown rather than pink. Assess warmth, edema, induration, tenderness, scale, and surface change. Residual hyperpigmentation can outlast inflammation; ongoing itch, thickening, or new lesions suggests activity. Do not escalate treatment for pigment alone. Inspect evolving acral/nail lesions regardless of skin tone. [AAD clinical education on skin findings across skin tones](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/skin-overall-health)

Assess itch, pain, embarrassment, alopecia, and pigment change regardless of affected area. Clarify whether the priority is diagnosis, relief, damage prevention, or cosmetic treatment, and discuss relevant scarring, recurrence, and dyspigmentation.

### Choose office tests to answer a specific question

**Dermoscopy** reveals pigment/vascular structures and, in trained hands, helps characterize lesions and select biopsy sites. It does not make every atypical lesion safe to observe. Selected high-risk patients may benefit from defined photographic/dermoscopic surveillance; a concerning lesion still needs prompt sampling.

For possible superficial fungal disease, collect scale from an appropriate active area, or affected hair/nail material when those structures are involved. **KOH microscopy** clears keratin so fungal structures can be recognized. Hyphae support dermatophyte infection; yeasts and pseudohyphae suggest different organisms in the right context. The result depends on sampling and interpretation. A negative preparation does not reliably exclude infection when the sample is scant, the site is wrong, or prior therapy has reduced yield. Repeat sampling, culture, nail histology, or another test may be needed if the result would change treatment. [DermNet fungal specimen collection and microscopy](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/laboratory-tests-for-fungal-infection)

A **Wood lamp** can accentuate depigmentation and reveal selected fluorescence patterns. Coral-red fluorescence supports erythrasma; some scalp dermatophytes fluoresce, but many do not. Cosmetics, lint, and topical products can fluoresce, and a negative examination does not exclude fungal disease. Interpret the finding in the clinical distribution rather than prescribing from the color alone. [DermNet Wood-lamp examination and limitations](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/wood-lamp-skin-examination)

Use lesion PCR for suspected herpesvirus and the correct microbiology container. Patch testing assesses delayed contact allergy; skin-prick/specific-IgE testing assesses immediate mechanisms. Broad food panels do not diagnose routine contact dermatitis. Surface bacterial growth may reflect colonization. Choose tests according to the suspected process, severity, and consequences of error. [DermNet dermatologic investigations and specimen pathways](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/dermatological-investigations-and-tests)

**Clinical Case:** A cyclist's unilateral expanding scaly plaque has a smooth center after repeated combination-steroid use. A negative central scraping does not exclude fungus. Reconsider the differential and sample the active margin before escalating steroids.

### A biopsy must preserve the information needed for the decision

Plan biopsy technique, site, depth, and handling together. Superficial tissue can miss invasion or deep inflammation; a full-thickness punch may miss heterogeneity across a tumor. Incisional biopsy samples part, excisional biopsy the whole clinical lesion. 'Shave' may mean superficial tangential sampling or deep saucerization, so specify adequacy rather than relying on the name. [DermNet skin-biopsy techniques and sampling limitations](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/skin-biopsy)

| Diagnostic question | Sampling principle | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possible melanoma | Complete narrow biopsy of sufficient depth when feasible | Superficial transection or destructive treatment |
| Possible keratinocyte cancer | Representative tissue adequate to assess the suspected process | Necrotic center only or inadequate depth |
| Inflammatory eruption | Active representative lesion at the appropriate stage | Scratched or heavily treated tissue alone |
| Autoimmune blistering disease | Coordinate routine histology and a separate DIF specimen | Putting every specimen in formalin |

For a lesion suspicious for melanoma, the **AAD diagnostic approach** favors a complete narrow biopsy with **1-3 mm clinical margins**, extending deeply enough to avoid transecting the lesion. A properly performed deep saucerization can be an excisional method; it is not equivalent to a superficial shave that removes only the top. A partial biopsy may be appropriate when complete removal is impractical, such as a large facial, acral, or nail lesion, but site selection and subsequent clinicopathologic correlation require expertise. Preserve Breslow assessment and communicate whether sampling was partial. [AAD primary melanoma guideline: diagnostic-biopsy recommendations](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S019096221832588X)

For suspected autoimmune blistering disease, routine histology and **direct immunofluorescence (DIF)** usually require different samples and handling. Perilesional skin is often selected for DIF, while routine histology samples an appropriate intact blister or its edge. A vasculitic eruption has different timing and site requirements. Discuss the suspected diagnosis with the laboratory before the procedure; DIF tissue should not be placed in formalin. Tissue intended for culture likewise needs a microbiology specimen rather than a formalin-fixed sample. [DermNet disease-specific DIF sampling and transport](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/direct-immunofluorescence)

Discuss purpose, incomplete removal, bleeding, infection, scarring/pigment change, and further treatment. Review anticoagulants/healing risks without automatically stopping essential therapy. Label sites precisely and provide the differential, duration, treatment, and extent of removal. Assign result communication and discordance review; a small unrepresentative benign specimen does not clear an enlarging concerning lesion. [DermNet clinicopathologic correlation](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/dermatopathology)

### Recognize melanoma without waiting for every classic sign

**Melanoma** is a malignancy of melanocytes. UV exposure contributes to many cutaneous melanomas, but a tumor may arise on protected skin, a sole, a nail unit, or a mucosal surface. Personal or family melanoma, numerous or atypical nevi, relevant inherited syndromes, and immune suppression affect risk. Many melanomas arise without a previously recognized mole. Neither a dark skin tone nor an office-based occupation eliminates risk. [NCI melanoma biology and risk assessment](https://www.cancer.gov/types/skin/hp/melanoma-treatment-pdq)

Use **ABCDE**: asymmetry, border irregularity, color variation, diameter, and especially evolution. Small size, symmetry, or uniform color does not exclude melanoma. Investigate an outlier lesion or enlarging firm nodule even without a classic flat pigmented appearance; act on the complete presentation, not a letter count. [AAD melanoma signs and visual variants](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/types/common/melanoma/symptoms)

Superficial spreading melanoma often has a conspicuous irregular expanding pigmented component. Nodular melanoma can grow vertically early. **Lentigo maligna** is melanoma in situ on chronically sun-damaged skin; once invasive, it is lentigo maligna melanoma. The distinction is histologic, not simply the patient's age or speed of growth. Acral melanoma involves palms, soles, or the nail apparatus and cannot be dismissed as a sun-exposure problem only. Pattern names help recognition, but the stage, thickness, ulceration, and spread guide prognosis and treatment. [DermNet lentigo maligna and invasive disease](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/lentigo-maligna-and-lentigo-maligna-melanoma)

**Amelanotic melanoma** may appear pink or red rather than brown-black and can resemble a benign vascular growth, inflammatory lesion, or keratinocyte cancer. Repeated bleeding, progressive enlargement, or failure of an assumed benign lesion to behave as expected should prompt reassessment. Lack of pigment is a reason the usual visual shortcut may fail, not evidence against melanoma. [DermNet amelanotic melanoma recognition](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/amelanotic-melanoma)

Pathology and site guide definitive excision. Breslow thickness is vertical depth, not surface diameter. Sentinel-node biopsy evaluates selected patients for occult spread, not every draining node or every thin lesion. The melanoma service directs stage-specific imaging, systemic care, and risk-based surveillance rather than one universal annual schedule. [AAD melanoma treatment and follow-up framework](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/guidelines/melanoma)

In the **AAD surgical framework**, invasive melanomas up to **1 mm** thick use a **1-cm clinical excision margin**; those over 1 through 2 mm use **1-2 cm**, and those over 2 mm use **2 cm**, with anatomy and function considered by the surgeon. **Melanoma in situ uses 0.5-1 cm**, aiming for histologically clear margins; lentigo maligna may need wider clearance or staged margin control because of subclinical extension. These are definitive-treatment margins, not the **1-3 mm** used for initial diagnostic biopsy. [AAD melanoma guideline: diagnostic biopsy and surgical-management tables IV and X](https://turkdermatoloji.org.tr/public/media/pdf/swetter2019.pdf)

The **ASCO/SSO nodal-staging framework** does not routinely recommend SLNB for a nonulcerated melanoma **under 0.8 mm** thick. Discuss possible SLNB for **0.8-1.0 mm**, or a thinner ulcerated tumor; it is recommended for intermediate-thickness tumors **over 1 through 4 mm**, with individualized discussion in thicker disease. Apply the complete pathology and patient's goals, not surface diameter alone. Clinically suspicious nodes need their own diagnostic assessment rather than being treated as an occult-node staging problem. [ASCO/SSO melanoma sentinel-node guideline](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2017.75.7724)

**Clinical Case:** A 32-year-old pregnant patient has a new 4-mm pink papule that doubled in height and bled twice spontaneously. Small size, absent pigment, and pregnancy do not justify delay. Arrange diagnostic assessment and appropriate biopsy; local-anesthetic skin biopsy can be performed during pregnancy. [AAD melanoma evaluation during pregnancy](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/types/common/melanoma/during-pregnancy)

### Keratinocyte cancers require risk assessment, not just a visual label

**Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)** is a keratinocyte malignancy that commonly presents as a slowly enlarging shiny papule, a recurrently crusted or bleeding erosion, or a thin plaque. Telangiectasia and a rolled border are helpful when present. Superficial BCC can resemble dermatitis, pigmented BCC can resemble a melanocytic lesion, and an infiltrative lesion may look scar-like. Metastasis is uncommon, but local destruction can be substantial. A lesion that repeatedly "heals" and breaks down again still needs evaluation. [DermNet BCC clinical variants](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/basal-cell-carcinoma)

**Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)** often forms a firm scaly or crusted papule, plaque, or nodule, sometimes painful or ulcerated. It can arise on sun-damaged skin, within chronic wounds or scars, and in immunosuppressed patients. A keratin-filled crater can suggest a keratoacanthoma-type lesion but is not a reliable reason to watch an unbiopsied growing tumor disappear. Look for regional nodes and concerning neurologic symptoms when appropriate; pain or numbness near a tumor may raise concern for deeper or perineural involvement. [DermNet cSCC clinical features and risk contexts](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/cutaneous-squamous-cell-carcinoma)

| Process | Common clinical clue | Decision that changes care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BCC | Recurrent bleeding, shiny or scar-like growth | Biopsy and assess site, subtype, recurrence risk |
| cSCC | Growing keratotic or ulcerated firm lesion | Assess invasion and high-risk host/tumor features |
| SCC in situ | Persistent localized scaly plaque | Confirm the diagnosis and exclude invasion when suspected |
| Actinic keratosis | Rough lesion within sun-damaged skin | Distinguish routine field care from a lesion needing biopsy |

For BCC, representative biopsy and risk stratification guide standard excision, Mohs surgery, or selected nonsurgical options. Histologic subtype, borders, recurrence, size, and anatomic site all matter. Nonsurgical approaches generally have lower cure rates and are not interchangeable with surgery for a high-risk tumor. The AAD advises annual checks after BCC for recurrence or another primary skin cancer; some patients need closer follow-up. [AAD BCC management recommendations](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/guidelines/bcc)

For cSCC, clinical treatment risk and prognostic staging are related but distinct. Surgery is usually the main treatment; Mohs or another appropriately planned approach may be needed for high-risk disease. A transplant recipient's rapidly growing ear lesion warrants expedited assessment even if its surface diameter is modest. Topical treatment intended for an in-situ lesion should not replace evaluation of possible invasive cancer. Follow-up includes recurrence, new cancers, and selected regional-node surveillance. [AAD cSCC treatment and follow-up framework](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/guidelines/scc)

**Mohs micrographic surgery** uses staged removal with microscopic margin assessment to conserve uninvolved tissue and achieve clearance. Selection depends on tumor risk and anatomy. Advanced/metastatic keratinocyte cancers may need multidisciplinary systemic or radiation treatment; primary care coordinates comorbidities, toxicity, and continuity. [NCI skin-cancer treatment and margin-control overview](https://www.cancer.gov/types/skin/hp/skin-treatment-pdq)

**Clinical Case:** A kidney-transplant recipient has a tender scaly ear bump enlarging over six weeks. Examine the lesion, nodes, and relevant symptoms and arrange prompt biopsy/specialist care. Do not assume a wart, repeatedly freeze it, or independently stop transplant immunosuppression.

### Actinic damage includes a field problem and individual lesions

**Actinic keratoses (AKs)** are UV-related keratinocyte lesions that often feel rougher than they look. They may be pink, pigmented, or nearly skin colored, with background photodamage. An individual AK may persist, regress, or progress to carcinoma; a clinician cannot guarantee its future behavior by appearance. Marked tenderness, thickening, rapid growth, ulceration, bleeding, or failure of treatment should prompt evaluation for invasive disease. A cutaneous horn describes keratin projecting from the surface; the underlying base determines the diagnosis. [DermNet AK recognition and concerning change](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/actinic-keratosis)

Treat an isolated appropriate lesion differently from a broad damaged field containing visible and subclinical lesions. The active **AAD AK guideline** supports UV protection, cryosurgery, topical fluorouracil, and imiquimod; its focused update adds tirbanibulin as a field option. Photodynamic therapy and diclofenac have separate conditional recommendations. Choose by diagnosis, site, extent, tolerance, treatment burden, and product restrictions rather than treating these methods as equivalent for every patient. Explain expected local reactions and arrange reassessment after healing. A field-treatment reaction does not prove that an unsampled invasive focus was cured. [AAD AK guideline and focused update](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/guidelines/actinic-keratosis)

### Reassurance should explain why a lesion is benign and when to return

A **melanocytic nevus** may be flat or raised and may lose pigment as it matures. A stable lesion consistent with the patient's other nevi often needs no removal. Document the basis for reassurance and changes that should trigger review. A congenital or atypical nevus requires its own risk assessment; removing every mole does not eliminate melanoma risk. Changing or uncertain lesions require reassessment rather than a permanent benign label based on an earlier visit. [DermNet nevi and indications for evaluation](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/melanocytic-naevus)

**Seborrheic keratoses** are common benign epidermal growths with waxy, verrucous, or adherent-looking surfaces. They can be deeply pigmented and can become inflamed. If clinical and dermoscopic assessment leave doubt about melanoma or keratinocyte cancer, obtain appropriate tissue rather than destroying the lesion solely because part of it looks keratotic. When the diagnosis is secure, removal is optional for symptoms or the patient's preference; explain scar and pigment-change risks. [DermNet seborrheic keratosis assessment and treatment](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/seborrhoeic-keratosis)

A **dermatofibroma** is typically a firm dermal papule that may dimple with lateral compression. The sign supports but does not prove the diagnosis; recent enlargement, ulceration, or atypical features warrant investigation. An **epidermoid cyst** contains keratin and often has a punctum. Rupture can cause sterile inflammation, so redness alone does not prove bacterial infection. A small uncomplicated cyst may be observed; definitive elective removal aims to remove its lining, while an acutely infected or fluctuant process has a different immediate pathway. [DermNet dermatofibroma](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/dermatofibroma), [DermNet epidermoid cyst](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/epidermoid-cyst)

Other common benign findings include soft pedunculated skin tags, small vascular papules such as cherry angiomas, and soft mobile subcutaneous lipomas. Their behavior should fit the diagnosis. A rapidly growing, deep, fixed, painful, or otherwise atypical mass needs assessment rather than reflex cosmetic removal. **Pyogenic granuloma** is a benign capillary proliferation, not a pus-forming infection despite its name. It can bleed readily, but amelanotic melanoma is an important mimic; an uncertain bleeding growth needs diagnostic tissue. [DermNet pyogenic granuloma and its differential](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/pyogenic-granuloma)

### Nail change can reflect infection, inflammation, injury, or a tumor

Inspect all nails and distinguish the nail plate, bed, matrix, and surrounding folds. **Onycholysis** means separation of plate from bed, not a particular cause. Pitting can accompany psoriasis or alopecia areata; transverse Beau lines can follow interruption of matrix growth. Chronic wet-work inflammation of the folds is not automatically a fungal infection. Clubbing is a change of the nail apparatus and distal digit that should prompt an appropriate systemic assessment, rather than a cosmetic diagnosis. [DermNet nail terminology and associated processes](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/nail-terminology)

Thick yellow or crumbly nails can be caused by fungus, trauma, psoriasis, or other disorders. Confirm suspected **onychomycosis before oral antifungal treatment** with an appropriate fungal test. The benefit, duration, interactions, liver risks, and alternatives should be discussed only after establishing the likely cause; a prolonged systemic course is not a diagnostic test. Limited disease may be managed differently from extensive matrix involvement. New normal nail grows slowly, so persistent distal damaged nail does not necessarily mean immediate failure. [AAD recommendation against unconfirmed oral nail-antifungal treatment](https://www.aad.org/member/clinical-quality/clinical-care/wisely), [DermNet fungal nail diagnosis and treatment selection](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/fungal-nail-infections)

**Longitudinal melanonychia** has several benign and malignant causes. Multiple stable bands can reflect physiologic pigmentation or medication exposure, but a new widening or irregular band in one nail, nail destruction, bleeding, or pigment extending to adjacent skin needs timely specialist assessment. **Hutchinson sign** is concerning in context, not perfectly specific, and its absence does not exclude melanoma. Trauma history is not definitive clearance. Matrix sampling can cause permanent dystrophy and requires precise site selection; do not replace it with a casual clipping when a melanocytic tumor is suspected. [DermNet nail-unit melanoma assessment](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/melanoma-of-the-nail-unit)

**Clinical Case:** A 61-year-old pianist has stable faint nail bands but one thumbnail band widening and becoming irregular over four months. A recalled drawer injury does not explain away progression. Compare nails/images and arrange nail-unit assessment rather than empirical antifungals or indefinite observation.

### Separate hair shedding, miniaturization, breakage, and scarring

Assess hair-loss onset, rate, distribution, scalp symptoms, illness, childbirth, weight/nutrition, medicines, family pattern, and hair practices. Inspect shafts, scale, inflammation, broken hairs, follicular openings, and other affected sites. Test for suspected causes such as iron deficiency or thyroid disease, not indiscriminate supplement panels. Hair-pull testing supports shedding but does not establish its cause.

**Telogen effluvium** reflects a shift of follicles into the resting phase, with diffuse shedding commonly becoming apparent **two to four months** after an illness, childbirth, major physiologic stress, weight loss, or another trigger. The scalp is usually nonscarred, and density can recover after the trigger resolves. Identify persistent nutritional, endocrine, medication, or scalp problems; reassure about the lag between the trigger, shedding, and visible regrowth. Continued progression or a mismatched examination warrants reconsideration rather than attributing all hair loss to stress. [DermNet telogen effluvium and recovery](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/telogen-effluvium)

**Pattern hair loss** produces progressive follicular miniaturization, often a widening part or frontotemporal/vertex pattern, rather than sudden uniformly shed hair. Topical minoxidil is an option after appropriate diagnosis; explain scalp irritation, possible early increased shedding, months before judging benefit, and the need for ongoing use to maintain results. Avoid it during pregnancy, planned pregnancy, or breastfeeding according to AAD advice. Oral minoxidil and antiandrogen therapy have distinct off-label and patient-specific safety considerations and should not be substituted casually. Evaluate rapid virilization or other endocrine warning signs rather than assuming ordinary hereditary thinning. [AAD pattern-hair-loss care](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/types/female-pattern)

**Alopecia areata** is immune-mediated nonscarring hair loss, often with smooth patches and sometimes nail changes. Localized treatment may use topical or intralesional corticosteroids selected for age and site; extensive disease may need specialist systemic therapy, including approved JAK inhibitors in eligible populations. These are not interchangeable with routine scalp treatments, and require product-specific safety assessment. Ask about distress and offer practical support as well as a regrowth plan. [AAD alopecia areata diagnosis and treatment](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/types/alopecia/treatment)

Traction can initially produce reversible loss but prolonged tension can destroy follicles. Discuss styling and practices without blame, and reduce the causative tension. Irregular patches with broken hairs of different lengths can occur with hair pulling; ask sensitively and consider behavioral care, while excluding infection and other causes. In children, scale, broken hairs, inflammation, or regional nodes raises concern for tinea capitis, which requires an infection-specific systemic treatment pathway rather than topical minoxidil alone. [DermNet traction alopecia](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/traction-alopecia), [DermNet hair-pulling disorder](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/trichotillomania)

**Scarring alopecia** is a time-sensitive distinction: loss of follicular openings, a smooth shiny scalp, perifollicular inflammation, pain, burning, or progressive focal loss warrants timely dermatologic assessment and often biopsy from an informative active site. Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia commonly begins near the crown and spreads outward; other inflammatory scarring disorders have different patterns. Do not attribute all crown loss to hairstyle or wait for complete baldness before referring. The aim is to preserve remaining follicles; established scar cannot simply regrow with a hair stimulant. [AAD central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia recognition](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/types/ccca)

**Clinical Case:** A 43-year-old teacher has crown thinning, burning, reduced follicular openings, and perifollicular scale. Stress does not adequately explain these findings. Refer promptly for possible inflammatory scarring alopecia, advise gentle care, and avoid promising regrowth from an OTC stimulant alone.

### Pigment disorders need a diagnosis before lightening treatment

Distinguish **hypopigmentation** from more complete **depigmentation**, and inspect for scale, altered sensation, inflammation, scars, and distribution. **Vitiligo** involves loss of functioning melanocytes, producing depigmented areas that can be accentuated by Wood-lamp examination. It may coexist with autoimmune disease, particularly thyroid disease; history and examination guide associated evaluation. It is not contagious. Discuss psychological impact, sun protection, camouflage if desired, and site-appropriate treatment rather than implying that pigment change is medically or socially trivial. Topical anti-inflammatory treatment and supervised phototherapy are options selected by extent, activity, and site. [DermNet vitiligo mechanisms, assessment, and treatment](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/vitiligo)

Pale patches with fine scale need a different differential. **Pityriasis alba** commonly produces poorly defined lighter patches on a child's face in association with dry or eczematous skin. Reassurance and moisturization may suffice when asymptomatic; treat associated inflammation appropriately rather than using an antifungal or a strong bleaching product solely for the color. **Pityriasis versicolor** can cause lighter or darker finely scaly patches, often on the trunk and upper arms, from Malassezia overgrowth. KOH can support the diagnosis; it is not a dermatophyte infection despite the common name tinea versicolor. Color can lag behind microbiologic improvement. Infection-specific treatment is in 08C. Altered sensation, neurologic findings, unusual distribution, or a progressive unexplained patch should prompt a broader evaluation, not automatic assignment to one of these common conditions. [DermNet pityriasis alba](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/pityriasis-alba), [DermNet pityriasis versicolor](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/pityriasis-versicolor)

For selected patients aged **12 years or older** with nonsegmental vitiligo, ruxolitinib **1.5% cream** is applied twice daily to affected areas totaling **no more than 10% BSA**. Do not exceed one **60-g tube per week or one 100-g tube per two weeks**. Repigmentation can take longer than 24 weeks; reassess at 24 weeks if the response is not meaningful to the patient. The separate AD indication begins at age two and has different area/quantity limits. Avoid use with active serious infection; assess the boxed infection, malignancy, cardiovascular, and thrombosis warnings, and perform skin examinations and clinically indicated CBC monitoring. Combination with therapeutic biologics, other JAK inhibitors, or potent immunosuppressants is not recommended. Pregnancy needs individualized assessment; do not breastfeed during treatment or for about four weeks afterward. [Ruxolitinib cream: current indication-specific dosing and safety](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=24da5509-6631-4795-9d42-273faecd08e7)

**Postinflammatory hyperpigmentation** can persist after inflammation or injury resolves. Control the cause, avoid irritation, and explain gradual improvement. Scrubbing, peels, and irritating combinations may worsen it. Individualize photoprotection and pigment treatment; darkening alone does not establish infection or justify stronger steroids. [DermNet postinflammatory pigment change](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/postinflammatory-hyperpigmentation)

**Melasma** usually causes symmetric facial hyperpigmented patches and is influenced by light exposure and hormonal factors. UV and visible-light protection, including suitable tinted products, can help; recurrence is common. Discuss pregnancy plans before prescribing retinoid-containing or other pigment-directed regimens, and do not prescribe oral tranexamic acid as a casual cosmetic remedy without a separate risk assessment. A focal changing lesion or a plaque with unexpected texture should not be hidden under the melasma label. [DermNet melasma evaluation and treatment limitations](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/melasma)

Track biopsy results and unresolved lesions; discordant benign pathology warrants clinicopathologic review. **USPSTF's insufficient-evidence statement for asymptomatic screening does not apply to suspicious lesions** or define surveillance after skin cancer.
