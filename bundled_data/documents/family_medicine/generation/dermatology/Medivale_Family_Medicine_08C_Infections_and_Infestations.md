# Medivale Family Medicine

## 08C. Infections and Infestations

Choose treatment from morphology, depth, distribution, time course, exposures, and general condition. Pustules may be nonbacterial and annular scale nonfungal. The key intervention may be drainage, an antimicrobial, contact treatment, drug withdrawal, or emergency transfer; empirical steroids can obscure the diagnosis.

Distinguish superficial infection, a drainable collection, and spreading or deep tissue disease before choosing an antibiotic. Genital infections and systemic fever-rash syndromes have separate pathways in chapter 9.

### Start with depth, host risk, and danger signs

Assess pain/itch, spread, fever, medicines, recurrence, contacts, travel, animal/water exposure, procedures, and antimicrobials. Examine beyond the main lesion for toe-web disease, scalp involvement, or burrows. Record vitals and function. Diabetes, neuropathy, poor circulation, edema, immune suppression, pregnancy, and age affect presentation and setting.

Across skin tones, assess warmth, tenderness, edema, induration, fluctuance, scale, and vesicles against unaffected skin. Photography cannot replace palpation or mucosal examination. Check access, application technique, review, and contact treatment. Apparent resistance may reflect an undrained focus, wrong diagnosis, untreated contacts, or an impractical regimen.

**Severe pain out of proportion, rapid progression, hypotension, confusion, dusky skin, bullae, anesthesia, or systemic toxicity** raises concern for necrotizing infection. Crepitus and visible necrosis are late findings, not prerequisites. Arrange emergency surgical assessment, resuscitation, and broad parenteral antibiotics; imaging must not delay exploration when suspicion is high. Absence of gas does not exclude streptococcal necrotizing disease. Perineal disease can represent Fournier gangrene even when the initial skin change seems small. [CDC necrotizing infection recognition and urgent management](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/necrotizing-fasciitis.html)

| Dominant finding | Clinical question | Direction of care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Superficial crusts or erosions | Few lesions, extensive disease, or deeper ulceration? | Local versus systemic treatment; assess transmission |
| Tender fluctuant collection | Is there pus requiring source control? | Appropriate drainage; consider culture and adjunct therapy |
| Spreading warmth without a collection | True cellulitis or an inflammatory mimic? | Streptococcal-active treatment when infection fits |
| Deep pain, toxicity, or rapid tissue change | Could this be necrotizing infection? | Emergency surgical pathway, not routine follow-up |

### Superficial bacterial disease differs from an abscess

**Impetigo** is an epidermal infection. Nonbullous lesions evolve into erosions with adherent crusts and may involve either *S. aureus*, group A streptococci, or both. Bullous disease involves toxin-producing *S. aureus*. Appearance does not reliably distinguish the organisms in nonbullous disease. A few localized lesions may be treated with a suitable topical antibiotic such as mupirocin; numerous lesions generally need oral treatment covering the likely organisms. Culture is useful for unusual, recurrent, or poorly responding disease, although a typical presentation need not wait for culture before treatment. Cover lesions, discourage picking and shared towels, and discuss contact precautions. [CDC impetigo assessment and treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/impetigo.html)

For localized impetigo, **IDSA recommends mupirocin 2% twice daily for five days**. This is a guideline regimen, distinct from this U.S. ointment product's labeled three-times-daily course for up to ten days; do not combine the schedules. Reassess if there is no response within three to five days, sooner if worsening. Keep ointment out of eyes and mucosa; its polyethylene-glycol base is unsuitable where large absorption is possible, especially with moderate/severe renal impairment. **Ecthyma**, numerous lesions, or an outbreak favors oral treatment; IDSA uses seven days of an appropriate antistaphylococcal agent, usually dicloxacillin or cephalexin when MSSA is expected. Suspected MRSA, allergy, age, pregnancy, and renal function change selection. Extensive bullae, marked pain, fever, or a sick infant needs more urgent assessment. [IDSA impetigo/ecthyma pathway](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/skin-and-soft-tissue-infections/), [Mupirocin ointment labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=6cef3abf-9f17-459e-86b9-95896d9620b7)

**Folliculitis** is follicle-centered inflammation from bacteria, yeast, occlusion, shaving, or medicines. *S. aureus* causes tender pustules and deeper furuncles; hot-water exposure suggests pseudomonas. Monomorphic itchy truncal lesions without comedones suggest Malassezia; shaving may cause pseudofolliculitis. Reduce the exposure and treat the cause. Recurrent, extensive, or atypical disease may need microscopy/culture rather than repeated antibiotics. [DermNet infectious and noninfectious folliculitis](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/folliculitis)

A **cutaneous abscess** is a pus collection; early/deep lesions may lack fluctuance. Ultrasound helps uncertain examinations. Drain in an appropriate setting with analgesia, anatomic precautions, and follow-up; complex face, hand, perirectal, or deep lesions may need specialist care. Antibiotics do not replace source control. [ACEP abscess examination and ultrasound](https://www.acep.org/sonoguide/procedures/abscess-evaluation)

After drainage of an uncomplicated abscess, the **BMJ Rapid Recommendation** weakly favors adjunct TMP-SMX or clindamycin after discussing modest cure/recurrence benefits and adverse effects. It favors these over cephalosporins and TMP-SMX over clindamycin because of less diarrhea. Deep disease and substantial immune suppression fall outside this scope. Systemic illness, surrounding infection, host compromise, complex anatomy, or poor response strengthens the need for antibiotics and possibly hospital care. [BMJ primary abscess guideline](https://www.bmj.com/content/360/bmj.k243)

**Clinical Case:** A 24-year-old warehouse worker has a fluctuant thigh lesion without systemic illness or immune suppression despite leftover cephalexin. Assess for drainage; failure without source control does not establish resistance. Then discuss adjunct antibiotics, wound care, review, and ability to cover the wound at work.

### Cellulitis requires evidence of tissue infection and reassessment

**Cellulitis** affects deeper skin/subcutaneous tissue, producing spreading warmth, tenderness, edema, and usually a poorly defined inflammatory border. Erysipelas is more superficial and often sharply demarcated. For uncomplicated nonpurulent cellulitis, use an agent active against streptococci; an oral beta-lactam such as cephalexin is an option when appropriate. Severe systemic illness needs parenteral treatment and assessment for a deeper source. Routine blood cultures or swabs of intact skin are not useful in a typical mild case, but severe illness, immune suppression, bites, and immersion exposure can justify targeted sampling and different coverage. [CDC cellulitis diagnostic and antibiotic pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/cellulitis.html)

Under this framework, **five days** is an initial duration for most improving cases, extended when clinical improvement is inadequate. Elevate the limb and address edema, fissures, and toe-web disease. Recovery of skin appearance can lag behind microbial control; ongoing pigment or residual swelling alone does not prove persistent infection. Arrange a practical early review and immediate return for rapid spread, worsening pain, systemic symptoms, or functional deterioration. Persistent or worsening disease calls for reconsideration of source control, diagnosis, organism, and treatment exposure. [CDC duration and recurrence prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/hcp/clinical-guidance/cellulitis.html)

Consider stasis/contact dermatitis, lipodermatosclerosis, lymphedema, thrombosis, and gout before diagnosing cellulitis. Bilateral chronic itchy scaly edema favors inflammatory/vascular disease, though infection can coexist. Distinguish joint-centered pain. Nonresponse warrants re-examination rather than automatic MRSA coverage. [DermNet cellulitis mimics](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/cellulitis-mimics)

Recurrent abscesses require assessment for a foreign body, pilonidal disease, or **hidradenitis suppurativa**, not an assumption of poor hygiene. Obtain appropriate cultures and address the actual cause. Any decolonization plan should be selected for recurrent staphylococcal disease rather than prescribed universally after one boil. [IDSA recurrent abscess assessment](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/skin-and-soft-tissue-infections/)

Recurrent painful nodules in **axillae, groin, buttocks, or inframammary folds**, especially with tunnels/scars, suggest hidradenitis: chronic inflammation rather than contagious boils. Examine other folds, assess pain/function/distress, and refer before progressive scarring. Use individualized anti-inflammatory therapy/procedures and treat genuine superinfection, not every recurrence as an isolated abscess. [AAD hidradenitis diagnosis and treatment](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/hidradenitis-suppurativa-treatment)

### Diagnose fungal disease before escalating treatment

**Dermatophytes** infect keratinized skin, hair, and nails. Corporis often has an advancing scaly edge; pedis may be interdigital, plantar, or vesicular, and cruris can mimic other fold disease. Obtain mycology when feasible, particularly before systemic therapy or for atypical lesions. Sample active edges; prior treatment and poor sampling can cause false negatives. Steroid-masked spreading disease is **tinea incognito**. [CDC ringworm recognition and diagnostic stewardship](https://www.cdc.gov/ringworm/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

Localized disease often responds to a topical azole or allylamine. This **terbinafine 1% cream** product's label for patients **12 years or older** uses **once daily for one week** for tinea corporis/cruris, **twice daily for one week** for interdigital tinea pedis, and **twice daily for two weeks** for the sole/sides of the foot. These instructions are product- and site-specific, not an oral or scalp/nail regimen. Apply to clean, dry skin and complete the course; children under 12 need clinician-directed care. Keep areas dry and treat other involved sites that may reseed infection. Avoid potent-steroid combinations for an uncertain eruption. **Nystatin treats susceptible Candida, not dermatophytes.** Persistent disease needs confirmation rather than indefinite switching. [Terbinafine cream Drug Facts](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/getFile.cfm?setid=1aa5e03a-432b-4154-ab6e-6dbce7a67109&type=pdf)

| Site or pattern | Diagnostic issue | Treatment implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scaly skin plaque or toe-web eruption | Dermatophyte versus dermatitis or another infection | Confirm when feasible; suitable topical therapy for localized disease |
| Scale with broken scalp hairs or alopecia | Hair-shaft/follicular involvement | Systemic antifungal needed; shampoo alone is insufficient |
| Thick or discolored nail | Fungus versus trauma, psoriasis, or another nail disorder | Confirm before oral treatment; recovery requires nail growth |
| Macerated fold with satellite lesions | Candida versus other intertrigo | Moisture/barrier care plus an appropriate antifungal |

**Tinea capitis** causes scale, broken hairs/black dots, alopecia, and nodes. A boggy inflammatory **kerion** may scar; do not routinely incise it as a bacterial abscess. Collect hair/scale for microscopy/culture without delaying convincing inflammatory disease treatment. Negative Wood-lamp examination does not exclude tinea. Systemic antifungals are required; choose by organism, age, formulation, interactions, and availability. Adjunct shampoo reduces transmission but does not cure hair infection; assess household/animal sources. [DermNet tinea capitis and kerion pathway](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/tinea-capitis)

**Clinical Case:** An eight-year-old with a boggy scalp patch, broken hairs, and cervical nodes has failed cream-only treatment; a sibling has scale. Assess for kerion, collect fungal specimens, and arrange systemic treatment and close review to preserve hair. Assess the sibling and shared hair items rather than using incision or isolated antibacterial therapy.

Confirm **onychomycosis** before treatment because dystrophy has other causes. Choose observation, topical, or oral therapy by symptoms, spread, expected benefit, duration, cost, and recurrence. Abnormal distal nail persists until healthy growth replaces it; immediate cosmetic change is not a treatment endpoint. Reconsider persistent focal abnormalities. [DermNet fungal nail diagnosis and treatment selection](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/fungal-nail-infections)

For selected confirmed dermatophyte onychomycosis, the oral **terbinafine** label uses **250 mg once daily for six weeks for fingernails or twelve weeks for toenails**. Do not apply these schedules to scalp disease or every organism. It is contraindicated in chronic or active liver disease; obtain pretreatment transaminases and monitor liver function during treatment. New jaundice, dark urine, pale stool, persistent nausea, or right-upper-quadrant pain warrants stopping treatment and prompt assessment. Review interactions, taste/smell change, and serious rash. Use with **CrCl <=50 mL/min has not been adequately studied**: do not assume an empirical half-dose is safe. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and childhood require separate assessment rather than extrapolation from an adult nail regimen. [Terbinafine tablet dosing and safety](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=4ee1b667-0cf6-46e4-a740-4fc27904d577)

**Candidal intertrigo** favors warm moist folds and often produces macerated inflammation with satellite papules or pustules. Moisture, friction, incontinence, diabetes, and immune suppression can contribute. Consider microscopy/culture when uncertain; inverse psoriasis, contact dermatitis, erythrasma, dermatophytes, and less common persistent lesions can mimic it. Treat the local environment and barrier as well as using an appropriate topical antifungal such as clotrimazole. Severe or refractory disease warrants diagnostic reassessment and selected systemic therapy, not routine oral fluconazole for every fold rash. Pityriasis versicolor is a separate **Malassezia** yeast disorder; its pigment changes can persist after treatment and do not by themselves justify repeated courses. [DermNet candidal fold disease](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/candidiasis-of-skin-folds), [DermNet pityriasis versicolor](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/pityriasis-versicolor)

**Extensive or treatment-refractory tinea** may require reference-laboratory identification and susceptibility assessment. CDC distinguishes *T. indotineae* and terbinafine-resistant *T. rubrum* from TMVII, which is associated with sexual transmission but is not generally antifungal-resistant. Do not infer resistance from severity alone or assume every emerging strain behaves identically. Ask about travel, close/intimate contacts, prior steroid combination creams, and actual treatment exposure. Coordinate dermatology/infectious-disease advice; itraconazole or another specialist-selected agent brings important interactions and monitoring requirements. [CDC current emerging-ringworm brief](https://www.cdc.gov/ringworm/hcp/clinical-overview/emerging-ringworm-facts-for-clinicians.html)

### Scabies treatment includes contacts, technique, and follow-up

**Scabies** is an infestation by *Sarcoptes scabiei*; the host response to mites and their products produces itch. Nocturnal symptoms, similarly affected close contacts, papules, and burrows at finger webs, wrists, waist, nipples, or genital areas are useful clues. Infants may have scalp, palm, and sole involvement. Symptoms can be delayed after initial infestation, so an asymptomatic contact may still transmit it. Microscopic identification of a mite, egg, or fecal material confirms the diagnosis, but a negative scraping does not exclude low-burden infestation. [CDC scabies presentation and diagnosis](https://www.cdc.gov/scabies/about/index.html)

The CDC pathway includes **permethrin 5% cream**, approved from **two months of age**, usually left on for **8-14 hours**. Adult application is generally neck down; children may require head/neck treatment under age-specific instructions, avoiding eyes and mouth. Cover all prescribed sites, not only visible papules, and explain handwashing/reapplication instructions. Repeat application about a week later may be necessary. Oral **ivermectin is off-label for scabies in the U.S.**; the CDC regimen is **200 micrograms/kg per dose, two doses 7-14 days apart**, taken with food. This scabies instruction differs from the fasting instruction for labeled intestinal indications. Safety in pregnancy and children below **15 kg** is not established; choose an appropriate alternative or specialist plan. [CDC scabies clinical treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/scabies/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

**Example:** For an eligible 60-kg adult at 200 micrograms/kg, **60 x 200 = 12,000 micrograms = 12 mg**: **four 3-mg tablets for one dose**, not daily. Confirm indication, pregnancy status, interactions, formulation, and the separately scheduled second dose; correct arithmetic does not establish eligibility. [Ivermectin tablet label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=681888c9-af79-4b7d-ae80-c3f4f6f1effd)

Treat household/close contacts **simultaneously**, including asymptomatic contacts, with individually suitable regimens. Wash/heat-dry items used during the **three days before treatment**; bag unwashable items for several days to a week. Avoid pesticide fogs and indiscriminate disposal. Address laundry access, cost, caregiving, and application help to prevent reinfestation. [CDC scabies contact and environmental control](https://www.cdc.gov/scabies/prevention/index.html)

Postscabetic itch may persist for weeks despite eradication. Provide symptom care and review rather than daily scabicide. New burrows/typical lesions or itch continuing **two to four weeks** after treatment warrants reassessment of technique, contacts, reinfestation, persistent mites, and alternative diagnoses. Assess secondary bacterial infection. [CDC post-treatment symptoms and retreatment assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/scabies/treatment/index.html)

**Crusted scabies** causes thick hyperkeratotic plaques, sometimes with little itch, especially in vulnerable/immunocompromised patients. High mite burden facilitates contact and fomite transmission. Coordinate specialist care, exposed contacts, and infection control. Combined oral/topical treatment uses severity-dependent repeated schedules, not an automatic ordinary two-dose regimen. [CDC crusted scabies recognition and management](https://www.cdc.gov/scabies/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

**Clinical Case:** An immunosuppressed care-home resident has thick hand/foot plaques with little itch, while two staff and a relative develop nocturnal itching. Consider crusted scabies before escalating psoriasis treatment. Arrange confirmation where possible, coordinated treatment, precautions, and contact assessment.

### Lice need a site-specific diagnosis, not the scabies prescription

**Head lice** spread mainly by hair contact, not poor hygiene. Confirm live nymphs/adults with good lighting and a fine comb; old nits or dandruff alone do not justify treatment. Nits may persist after cure. CDC permits school return after treatment begins and discourages no-nit exclusion or early dismissal solely for lice. [CDC head-lice diagnosis and school advice](https://www.cdc.gov/lice/about/head-lice.html)

Select a product for age, contraindications, local failure patterns, cost, and ability to apply it correctly. **Permethrin 1% lotion for head lice is not the 5% scabies cream.** Some products need a scheduled repeat because they do not reliably kill eggs; others often do not. CDC lists permethrin from two months, topical ivermectin and spinosad from six months, and malathion from six years. Malathion is flammable: avoid smoking and heated hair appliances while hair is wet with it. Do not combine products or repeatedly reapply a failed agent without reassessment. [CDC head-lice product-specific care](https://www.cdc.gov/lice/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

Check close contacts and treat affected people and bed-sharing contacts together as directed. Clean relevant recently used items and combs; extensive insecticide spraying is unnecessary and hazardous. Persistent crawling lice after a correctly completed course may require a different agent, but first check the diagnosis, adequate product quantity, application, and reinfestation. **Body lice** live and lay eggs mainly in clothing seams and can transmit infections, unlike ordinary head lice. Access to bathing, clean clothes, and laundry is central; assess systemic illness when relevant. Pubic lice require a separate sexual-contact and STI assessment. [CDC head-lice treatment troubleshooting](https://www.cdc.gov/lice/treatment/index.html), [CDC body-lice care](https://www.cdc.gov/lice/about/body-lice.html)

### Herpes simplex requires attention to site and host

**HSV** establishes sensory-ganglion latency and can reactivate. Grouped painful vesicles or erosions, sometimes preceded by tingling or burning, are suggestive, but lesions are not always classic. Oral infection may cause significant gingivostomatitis and poor intake, especially in children. Consider the distribution, recurrence, exposure, immune status, and hydration. Severe oral disease, suspected neonatal infection, altered mental status, eye symptoms, or widespread lesions in an immunocompromised patient requires a higher-acuity pathway. Antivirals suppress replication rather than eliminate latent virus. [DermNet HSV presentation and complications](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/herpes-simplex)

For uncertain active mucocutaneous lesions, a suitable lesion **NAAT/PCR** is more informative than nonspecific cytology. Sensitivity falls with poor or late sampling, and a negative lesion test does not prove lifetime absence of HSV. HSV IgM is not recommended. Genital first episodes need antiviral treatment; recurrence management can be episodic or suppressive, guided by burden and transmission concerns. A positive HSV-1 antibody alone does not identify the site of infection. Detailed genital and pregnancy pathways belong to chapter 9. [CDC HSV testing and clinical management](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/herpes.htm)

**Herpetic whitlow** causes a painful vesicular distal finger eruption that can look pustular. Do not incise it as if it were a bacterial felon; drainage can cause harm. Protect the site and assess antiviral need, occupational exposure, and secondary bacterial infection. Painful monomorphic erosions spreading over eczema suggest **eczema herpeticum**, requiring prompt systemic antiviral treatment and same-day specialist assessment, with urgent eye care for periocular involvement as described in 08B. [DermNet herpetic whitlow](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/herpetic-whitlow)

### Zoster is not just a painful skin rash

**Herpes zoster** represents VZV reactivation, usually with pain followed by grouped vesicles in one or adjacent dermatomes. It usually does not cross the midline, but an atypical or disseminated eruption is possible, especially with immune suppression. Lesion PCR can help when the diagnosis is uncertain. Begin an appropriate antiviral promptly; treatment is most effective early, generally within **72 hours**. Assess pain, function, hydration, immune status, and complications rather than making the decision from lesion count alone. [CDC zoster diagnosis and antiviral care](https://www.cdc.gov/shingles/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

After 72 hours, HSE supports considering antivirals up to one week for ongoing vesicles or high risk, including severe pain or immune suppression. Ocular, neurologic, disseminated, or severe disease needs urgent advice regardless of timing. Red/painful eyes, photophobia, or vision change with forehead/eyelid lesions requires urgent ophthalmology even without nasal-tip lesions. Ear lesions with facial weakness or hearing/vestibular symptoms also need prompt assessment. [HSE zoster timing exceptions and referral](https://assets.hse.ie/media/documents/Shingles_treatment_guideline.pdf)

For immunocompetent adults with zoster, the valacyclovir label uses a **seven-day course**, adjusted by **creatinine clearance (CrCl, mL/min)**: **>=50: 1 g every eight hours; 30-49: 1 g every twelve hours; 10-29: 1 g every twenty-four hours; <10: 500 mg every twenty-four hours**. On hemodialysis days, administer the recommended dose after dialysis. These are zoster doses, not genital-HSV or pediatric doses. Dehydration, nephrotoxins, older age, and excess dosing for renal function increase renal/CNS toxicity; new confusion needs assessment. Stop and urgently assess a progressive severe rash or painful rash with mucosal involvement: the label also warns about SCARs and contraindicates re-exposure after valacyclovir/acyclovir-associated SCAR. Disseminated or severe disease may need hospital-based therapy. [Valacyclovir label: sections 2.4, 4, and 5](https://gskpro.com/content/dam/global/hcpportal/en_US/Prescribing_Information/Valtrex/pdf/VALTREX-PI-PIL.PDF)

**Clinical Case:** A 61-year-old with CKD has day-five forehead vesicles, continuing new lesions, and a gritty red eye. Arrange urgent ophthalmology and renal-adjusted antiviral treatment. Neither elapsed time nor a memorized normal-renal dose is an adequate prescribing decision. Address pain and follow-up.

VZV from active zoster lesions can cause **varicella in a susceptible contact**, not directly give that person shingles. Cover lesions and avoid exposing susceptible pregnant people, newborns, and immunocompromised contacts until lesions have crusted; healthcare precautions depend on distribution and host immunity. Persistent neuropathic pain after skin healing needs its own management, with renal and fall-risk precautions. Discuss recombinant zoster vaccination after the acute episode according to the active prevention schedule; vaccination is not treatment for the current eruption. [CDC zoster transmission and prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/shingles/about/index.html)

### Warts and molluscum usually allow a measured approach

**Cutaneous warts** are HPV-associated keratinocyte proliferations. A rough surface, interruption of normal skin lines, and punctate capillaries can support the diagnosis; a corn/callus or an atypical malignancy may resemble a plantar or persistent lesion. Autoinoculation spreads lesions to traumatized skin. Rapid growth, bleeding, ulceration, immune suppression, or diagnostic uncertainty should prompt evaluation rather than repeated destructive treatment of an assumed wart. Ordinary hand/foot warts should not be equated with oncogenic genital HPV disease. [DermNet cutaneous wart recognition](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/viral-wart)

An asymptomatic uncomplicated wart may be observed for spontaneous clearance. Salicylic acid or clinician cryotherapy often needs repeated treatment. Protect surrounding skin and discuss pain, blisters, dyspigmentation, and scarring. Avoid home acids on face/genitals or neuropathic/poorly perfused feet; age and site constrain treatment. [AAD non-genital wart treatment and referral](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/warts-treatment)

Anogenital warts need a separate site-, pregnancy-, and patient-specific pathway. HPV testing is not used to establish the diagnosis of a visible wart, and treatment does not prove eradication of transmissible HPV. Atypical pigmented, indurated, fixed, bleeding, or ulcerated lesions may need biopsy, especially with immune suppression. Do not apply ordinary over-the-counter plantar-wart acid to genital tissue. Prevention counseling and vaccination remain important but are distinct from treating an existing lesion. [CDC anogenital wart assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/anogenital-warts.htm)

**Molluscum contagiosum** is a poxvirus causing smooth umbilicated papules spread by contact/autoinoculation; associated eczema promotes scratching. Spontaneous clearance may take months or longer. Discuss observation versus treatment by burden, pain, location, spread, and preferences. Extensive/atypical adult lesions may warrant immune assessment; genital lesions need sexual-health evaluation. Squeezing/scraping can scar. [CDC molluscum natural history and assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/molluscum-contagiosum/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

This **cantharidin 0.7% solution** is labeled from **age two** and applied only by trained healthcare personnel, with repeat sessions **every three weeks as needed**, using no more than two applicators per session. **Wash off with soap and water at 24 hours, or earlier for severe pain, blistering, or other severe local reactions.** Do not occlude or apply near eyes, mucosa, or healthy skin. Oral exposure can be life-threatening. It remains flammable even after drying: avoid fire, flame, and smoking near treated lesions until removed. These instructions do not apply automatically to compounded cantharidin. [Cantharidin solution administration and safety](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=7c9bdeaa-9822-4c69-a719-ea4a8643f59c)

This **berdazimer 10.3% gel** is labeled from **age one year**. It requires mixing equal amounts from its two tubes immediately before use, with once-daily lesion application for up to twelve weeks. Application-site irritation or allergic contact dermatitis can occur. It is not for ocular, oral, or intravaginal use. Neither product's molluscum approval establishes approval for all viral warts, and compounded formulations are not automatically interchangeable. [Current berdazimer product labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=76b17622-bdb9-41ea-b962-c2acc0df71f7)

**Clinical Case:** A five-year-old with eczema has itchy umbilicated papules. Explain molluscum, treat surrounding eczema, and discuss observation or appropriate treatment rather than a sibling's strong wart remover. Avoid unsupervised acid/cantharidin near eyes; inflamed lesions alone do not require antibiotics.

### A new rash on a medicine can be mild or life-threatening

Build a medication timeline including intermittent prescriptions, recently stopped drugs, supplements, and topical agents. **Morbilliform drug eruption** often begins after a new medicine and produces a symmetric maculopapular eruption, commonly starting on the trunk. A first reaction often appears after about one to two weeks, with a shorter interval on re-exposure, but timing alone is not diagnostic. Viral exanthems and other inflammatory diseases can look similar. A well patient with a mild eruption and no mucosal or organ findings may be managed with withdrawal/substitution of the likely cause, appropriate symptom care, and explicit follow-up. An essential drug should not simply be continued without clinical assessment. [DermNet uncomplicated drug eruption and warning signs](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/morbilliform-drug-reaction)

**Skin pain, mucosal erosion, blisters, dusky/purpuric lesions, facial edema, high fever, pustules, or organ dysfunction** requires escalation for possible severe reaction; an early mild examination does not guarantee a benign course. Assess deteriorating patients rather than sending them home with steroids. Anaphylaxis requires immediate epinephrine/airway care. Identify culprits precisely without labeling unrelated drugs as allergies.

| Pattern | Important distinguishing concern | Required response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild symmetric exanthem in a well patient | Can early severe disease be excluded clinically and followed? | Assess cause, stop/substitute appropriately, safety-net |
| Painful dusky rash with mucosal erosions or detachment | SJS/TEN spectrum | Immediate withdrawal of suspected cause and hospital assessment |
| Fever, facial edema, rash, and internal-organ injury | DRESS; eosinophilia may not yet be evident | Urgent systemic investigation and specialist care |
| Rapid widespread nonfollicular pustules after a drug | AGEP versus infection or pustular psoriasis | Stop suspected trigger; assess severity/organ involvement |
| Recurrent sharply localized lesion at the same site | Fixed drug eruption; generalized bullous forms can be severe | Identify culprit; escalate blistering/extensive disease |

**SJS/TEN** is epidermal necrolysis with painful skin and often severe mucosal involvement, distinct from ordinary erythema multiforme. Conventionally, detachment below **10%** of body surface is SJS, **10-30%** overlap, and above **30%** TEN; the percentage is not a threshold to wait for before referral. Stop suspected causative medicines and arrange immediate hospital care. [DermNet SJS/TEN recognition and classification](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/stevens-johnson-syndrome-toxic-epidermal-necrolysis)

The **2024 S3 epidermal-necrolysis guideline** calls for a medicine timeline, urgent histology/systemic investigation, and early dermatology/ophthalmology involvement. Mucosal and organ disease determine severity alongside skin loss; dangerous ocular involvement may occur without extensive detachment. Hospital care addresses barrier loss, fluids/electrolytes, temperature, pain, nutrition, wounds, and infection risk. Specialist immunomodulation must not delay withdrawal and support; no single steroid/IVIG/biologic regimen suits every case. [S3 epidermal-necrolysis diagnostic and initial-care recommendations](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ddg.15515)

**DRESS** is a delayed systemic drug reaction, often appearing **two to eight weeks** after the responsible medicine. Fever, facial swelling, widespread rash, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia/atypical lymphocytes, hepatitis, nephritis, or other organ involvement can occur. An early normal eosinophil count does not make systemic disease harmless. The skin's appearance does not reliably measure internal injury, and illness may progress after drug withdrawal. Check blood count/differential, liver and renal function, and symptoms directing cardiac, pulmonary, or other evaluation; arrange urgent specialist assessment. [DermNet DRESS presentation and systemic disease](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/drug-hypersensitivity-syndrome)

The international adult **DRESS consensus** bases treatment on severity and organ involvement: selected mild disease may use very high-potency topical corticosteroids, whereas severe disease requires systemic glucocorticoids under specialist care. A rapid unsupervised taper can cause relapse. Arrange follow-up **beginning within the first month after discharge and regularly during the first six months after onset**, then according to need. Review blood tests for initially involved organs, delayed thyroid/autoimmune problems, steroid adverse effects, and psychological burden. Record the culprit and a future-medicine plan; do not perform an office rechallenge after suspected severe reaction. This is adult expert consensus, not a universal pediatric dosing protocol. [International adult DRESS treatment and follow-up consensus](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamadermatology/fullarticle/2812063)

**AGEP** causes abrupt sterile **nonfollicular pustules**, often with fever/neutrophilia after a medicine, mimicking infection or pustular psoriasis. Stop the suspected trigger, assess systemic involvement, and seek specialist input with biopsy/laboratory follow-up as indicated. Broader antibiotics may worsen a reaction caused by the antibiotic itself. [DermNet AGEP assessment and management](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/acute-generalised-exanthematous-pustulosis)

**Clinical Case:** A 46-year-old develops fever, facial edema, rash, and liver-test abnormalities five weeks after starting an anticonvulsant without skin detachment. Arrange urgent systemic-reaction assessment, withdraw the suspected drug with an immediate alternative-condition plan, and evaluate organ injury. Absent blisters or improved itch does not establish recovery.

**Fixed drug eruption** recurs at the same site after re-exposure, often leaving pigment. Ask about intermittent analgesics and other occasional products, not only daily medicines. A localized lesion in a well patient differs from generalized bullous disease, which can be life-threatening. Identify and avoid the culprit; extensive blistering or mucosal disease needs urgent assessment rather than an office rechallenge. [DermNet fixed drug eruption and severe variants](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/fixed-drug-eruption)

After a severe reaction, communicate the drug, timing, phenotype, and organ involvement to the patient, pharmacy, and relevant clinicians. Ocular, oral, genital, skin, and psychological sequelae can persist after SJS/TEN, so discharge is not the end of care. Cross-reactivity and testing need specialist interpretation; a broad nonspecific allergy label is not a substitute for an individualized avoidance plan. [BAD recovery and long-term care after SJS/TEN](https://www.bad.org.uk/pils/sjs-ten)
