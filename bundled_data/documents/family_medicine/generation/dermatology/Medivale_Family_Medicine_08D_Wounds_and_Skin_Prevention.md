# Medivale Family Medicine

## 08D. Wounds and Skin Prevention

A wound's cause determines treatment: venous compression can harm severe ischemia, and stable black eschar should not be removed indiscriminately. Relate local care to perfusion, pressure, sensation, infection, nutrition, and feasibility. Dressings cannot compensate for arterial obstruction or repeated loading of an insensate ulcer.

Assess the wound's mechanism before choosing compression, offloading, debridement, or antibiotics. Diabetes surveillance is covered in 04B, and rabies and systemic exposure management in chapter 9.

### Assess the patient before choosing a dressing

Record onset, mechanism, course, prior ulcers/treatment, pain, drainage, and function. Assess smoking, diabetes, CKD, vascular procedures, immobility, incontinence, medicines, and nutrition. Document site, dimensions/depth, bed tissue, exudate, surrounding skin, undermining, and tracts, plus pulses, temperature, sensation, edema, and deformity. Examine both limbs when relevant. Atypical/nonhealing lesions may need biopsy or referral. [Chronic wound evaluation](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0201/p159)

**Rapid deterioration, disproportionate pain, systemic illness, spreading necrosis, or a newly cold or neurologically impaired limb requires urgent escalation.** Possible sepsis, necrotizing infection, acute ischemia, or deep infection cannot wait for a routine wound appointment. Fever may be absent in a frail or immune-suppressed patient. Determine whether surgery or vascular care is needed; a dressing cannot replace source control or restored blood flow. [Diabetic-foot infection severity and urgent referral](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/diabetic-foot-infections/)

Check dressing help, water/supply access, pain, vision, reach, and transport. Use consented photographs and consistent measurements. Set goals for closure when achievable and for pain, drainage, mobility, and procedure burden. Symptom-focused palliative care can still treat infection and reversible causes. [Assessment, healing goals, and symptom-focused care](https://www.internationalguideline.com/woundhealing)

### Distinguish the major chronic wound mechanisms

Mechanisms overlap: diabetes can coexist with neuropathy, venous disease, and ischemia, and heel ulcers can combine pressure and poor perfusion. Test the apparent explanation against vascular/neurologic findings and the course. Absent pain may reflect neuropathy; severe pain does not alone establish infection. [Lower-extremity wound assessment and differential](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0201/p159)

| Mechanism | Useful clinical clues | Main treatment direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure and shear | Bony prominence or device contact; reduced mobility; depth can exceed surface appearance | Relieve loading, assess tissue damage, correct contributing risks |
| Venous hypertension | Gaiter-region ulcer, edema, pigment change, venous dermatitis | Confirm adequate arterial supply before appropriate compression |
| Arterial insufficiency | Distal tissue loss, cool foot, reduced pulses, rest pain or gangrene | Vascular assessment and limb-preservation care |
| Neuropathic foot injury | Plantar loading site, callus, deformity, reduced protective sensation | Offload, assess perfusion and infection, prevent recurrence |

### Pressure injuries: recognize damage before skin opens

Pressure and shear injure tissue over prominences or beneath devices. Immobility, poor perfusion, sensory loss, moisture, and malnutrition increase risk. Distinguish incontinence dermatitis and skin tears. In darker skin, compare warmth, swelling, firmness, and pain when erythema is subtle. Classify by involved tissue rather than opening size; healing injuries retain their deepest stage. [Differentiation, skin-tone assessment, and classification](https://www.internationalguideline.com/classification)

In the **NPIAP staging system**, stage 1 is intact skin with persistent nonblanchable change; stage 2 is partial-thickness loss with exposed dermis but no visible fat or deeper tissue. Stage 3 reaches subcutaneous tissue without exposed deeper supporting structures. Stage 4 has exposed or directly palpable fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage, or bone. Obscuring slough or eschar makes a full-thickness injury unstageable. Persistent deep-red, maroon, or purple change or a blood-filled blister after pressure loading suggests deep tissue injury, not simply stage 1. **Mucosal pressure injuries are not staged** with this skin system. These categories do not classify venous ulcers, burns, or moisture damage. [International Guideline classification and NPIAP resources](https://www.internationalguideline.com/classification)

Use a **personalized repositioning plan** based on mobility, independent repositioning, tissue response, illness, comfort, sleep, support surface, and goals. Pressure-redistributing mattresses do not eliminate turning. Reduce shear with assistance/equipment, reassess skin, and adjust frequency for early damage. Train caregivers and arrange feasible support rather than prescribing an impractical universal interval. [Repositioning and mobilization](https://www.internationalguideline.com/repositioning)

Float vulnerable heels with a suitable device or calf support, avoiding popliteal pressure and improvised fluid-filled gloves. Recheck offloading after movement and remove devices periodically to inspect skin/perfusion. Prevent device-related injury and interference with transfers; consider walking and fall risk. [Heel pressure-injury prevention](https://www.internationalguideline.com/heels)

Protect fragile skin with gentle cleansing/drying, appropriate barrier products, and continence care rather than rubbing. Inspect pressure beneath tubing, masks, splints, and footwear. Prophylactic dressings do not replace device fit or skin checks. [Preventive skin care](https://www.internationalguideline.com/skincare)

Assess intake, weight change, swallowing, illness, and food access regardless of body size. Pressure injury warrants comprehensive individualized nutrition care, often with a dietitian. Adapt protein/fluid plans to CKD, heart failure, and dysphagia; supplements should meet identified needs rather than replace adequate intake or deficiency treatment. [Nutrition in pressure-injury prevention and treatment](https://www.internationalguideline.com/nutrition)

**Clinical Case:** A wheelchair user recovering from pneumonia has dry adherent heel eschar without drainage, fluctuance, inflammation, or systemic illness. Assess perfusion, offload, and review rather than automatically debriding. New drainage, inflammatory softening, or systemic decline requires prompt reassessment; address ischemia before aggressive debridement. [Stable heel eschar and perfusion precautions](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/wrr.13130)

### Venous and arterial ulcers require different priorities

Venous reflux or obstruction produces sustained venous hypertension, edema, and tissue inflammation. Ulcers often occur around the lower leg and ankle with brown pigment, eczema, or hardened surrounding tissue. Assess prior thrombosis, swelling, mobility, and recurrent ulceration. Duplex ultrasound can define reflux or obstruction and help plan vascular treatment. With adequate arterial supply, compression, movement, elevation when tolerated, and skin care address the mechanism; venous procedures may also be appropriate. Long-term prevention remains important after closure because the underlying venous problem may persist. [Society for Vascular Surgery: chronic venous insufficiency](https://vascular.org/your-vascular-health/vascular-conditions/chronic-venous-insufficiency)

**Do not begin strong compression solely because edema is present.** Examine pulses and assess arterial supply. For an active venous ulcer, the **WOCN compression algorithm** permits usual compression with ABI **0.8-1.3**, considers modified light compression **up to 30 mm Hg** for mixed disease with ABI **0.5 to <0.8**, and withholds compression for ABI **<0.5 or >1.3** pending further evaluation. A value at a boundary does not override ischemic symptoms, unreliable testing, or specialist judgment. These are this algorithm's compression thresholds, not the AHA/ACC diagnostic definition of noncompressible ABI below. Mixed disease needs vascular/wound supervision; new pain, numbness, or a cold foot requires reassessment. An atypical edge or persistent growth may need biopsy. [WOCN active-ulcer compression pathway](https://vlu.wocn.org/)

Select suitable compression the patient can apply, considering hand function, reach, footwear, work, help, and nursing access. Teach application, inspection, and response to new pain, numbness, color change, or coldness. NICE supports shared selection among appropriate systems; its NHS purchasing rules are not U.S. prescribing rules or proof of equal healing effectiveness. [Compression product selection and patient circumstances](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/HTG758/chapter/1-recommendations)

Arterial wounds reflect inadequate tissue perfusion. Distal ulcers, coolness, weak pulses, rest pain, and gangrene increase concern, but neuropathy can mask pain. In the AHA/ACC framework, resting **ABI <=0.90 is abnormal; >1.40 is noncompressible**. Diabetes and kidney disease can produce misleadingly high ankle measurements. Toe pressure/toe-brachial index with waveforms is indicated for suspected PAD with a noncompressible ABI; suspected chronic limb-threatening ischemia may require additional local perfusion testing even when ABI alone seems unremarkable. A wound is not proven adequately perfused by a single reassuring number. [AHA/ACC physiological testing recommendations](https://professional.heart.org/-/media/PHD-Files-2/Science-News/2/2024/2024-PAD-guideline-slide-set.pdf)

Tissue loss or ischemic rest pain needs prompt limb-preservation assessment for revascularization and coordinated wound care. Sudden severe pain, pallor, coolness, sensory loss, weakness, or loss of pulses suggests acute limb ischemia and an emergency pathway. Treat systemic atherosclerotic risk as well as the wound: smoking cessation, lipid lowering, indicated antithrombotic treatment, and diabetes/BP care are part of PAD management. Do not debride an otherwise stable dry ischemic wound simply to make its surface look cleaner before blood supply is addressed. [Current PAD management framework](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2024/05/09/15/00/2024-guideline-for-lower-extremity-pad)

**Clinical Case:** A delivery driver has a draining ankle ulcer, edema, and stasis pigment but a cool forefoot and weak pulses. Assess arterial supply before high compression and arrange mixed-disease wound/vascular care. Choose a plan compatible with standing, elevation, and ability to apply the system before work.

### The diabetic foot: offloading is treatment, not an accessory

Loss of protective sensation permits repeated unnoticed trauma. Deformity and callus concentrate loading, while vascular disease and infection can turn a small break into a limb-threatening problem. Examine the entire foot, interdigital spaces, footwear, sensation, pulses, and contralateral foot. Determine depth and whether infection or ischemia is present. A warm swollen foot with neuropathy may represent active Charcot neuroarthropathy, even without an ulcer; prompt immobilization/offloading and specialist assessment are important, as discussed in 04B. Do not label every swollen diabetic foot cellulitis. [Diabetic-foot assessment and multidisciplinary care](https://www.aafp.org/fpe/2025/557-foot-conditions-adults/diabetic-foot-ulcers)

For an appropriate **neuropathic plantar forefoot or midfoot ulcer**, IWGDF favors nonremovable knee-high offloading. Selection depends on infection, ischemia, site, tolerance, balance, and monitoring. If contraindicated or intolerable, use suitable removable knee-high/ankle-high devices consistently during weight bearing. Moderate/severe complications need their own pathway; address severe infection/ischemia first. Ordinary therapeutic footwear is not equivalent when a suitable offloading device is available. [IWGDF offloading recommendations](https://iwgdfguidelines.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/IWGDF-2023-06-Offloading-Guideline.pdf)

Diagnose diabetic-foot infection clinically rather than from a positive superficial swab alone. If an infected wound needs culture, an aseptically obtained tissue specimen after appropriate cleaning is preferred. Suspected osteomyelitis is assessed initially using the clinical examination, probe-to-bone when appropriate, plain radiographs, and inflammatory markers; MRI helps when uncertainty remains. Selected cases require bone sampling. **An uninfected ulcer does not need antibiotics to promote healing.** Deep abscess, necrotizing infection, gangrene, severe ischemia, or systemic illness needs urgent surgical/vascular coordination rather than simply a broader oral prescription. [IWGDF/IDSA diabetic-foot infection recommendations](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/diabetic-foot-infections/)

**Clinical Case:** A neuropathic patient notices sock blood after a long shift. A plantar ulcer beneath callus has no purulence, surrounding inflammation, or systemic illness. Assess depth/perfusion, arrange offloading and foot-team review, and accommodate work. Antibiotics do not relieve repeated loading.

### Local care and serial reassessment

Support viable tissue, manage exudate, protect surrounding skin, and relieve pain. Select debridement by perfusion, bleeding risk, tissue, infection, goals, and skill; avoid destroying viable tissue. Specify cleansing, contact layer, absorbency, change interval, and caregiver. Reassess during changes and more fully for deterioration. [Local treatment and monitoring of pressure injuries](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/wrr.13130)

Colonization is common in chronic ulcers. Increasing pain, warmth, spreading inflammation, or fever is more meaningful for infection than bacterial growth in an otherwise stable wound. NICE's leg-ulcer pathway does not recommend routine initial sampling; if infection worsens or fails to improve, clean the wound before sampling and reassess the treatment. This differs from tissue-culture selection in an infected diabetic foot. Do not apply one culture rule to every wound. Rapid deterioration, suspected bone involvement, sepsis, or disproportionate pain warrants escalation. [NICE leg-ulcer infection recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng152/chapter/recommendations)

**Example:** With the same length-times-width method, an ulcer changes from 4 x 3 cm to 3 x 2 cm: 12 to 6 cm2, or (12 - 6)/12 x 100 = **50% reduction**. This rectangular estimate is neither planimetry nor volume and does not prove infection eradication. Also document depth, tissue, drainage, pain, perfusion, and function; percentage alone does not set a healing deadline or adjunct indication.

### Burns: stabilize first, then assess depth and extent

Stop the exposure and assess airway, breathing, circulation, and associated trauma. Cool a recent thermal burn with **cool running water for a total of 20 minutes within three hours**, while preventing hypothermia, especially in young children. Remove constricting jewelry and nonadherent contaminated clothing; do not force off material stuck to skin. Do not use ice. Chemical and electrical mechanisms need their own urgent assessment; a small skin mark does not measure the full injury. Consider the history, exposure setting, inhalation symptoms, distal perfusion, pain control, and safeguarding concerns. [Acute burn assessment and first aid, updated April 2026](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/burns/)

| Burn depth | Typical findings | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epidermal | Dry, painful, blanching; no blisters | Symptom care; exclude from burn TBSA |
| Superficial partial thickness | Moist, blistered, blanching and painful | Protect viable dermis and reassess healing |
| Deep partial thickness | Drier, pale or mottled, reduced blanching or sensation | Early burn-specialist assessment |
| Full thickness | Leathery or waxy, variable white/brown/black color; reduced sensation | Urgent burn consultation; often operative care |

Depth can evolve; reduced pain can indicate destroyed nerve endings rather than a minor burn. Reassessment over **24-72 hours** can reveal a deeper injury than was apparent initially. A simple epidermal sunburn does not contribute to resuscitation TBSA. For scattered areas, specify the convention: the patient's **whole palmar hand including fingers is approximately 1% TBSA**, not the palm alone. Children require age-appropriate area assessment, such as a Lund-Browder chart. Do not substitute an adult rule of nines for every child. [Burn depth and outpatient reassessment](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463), [ABA extent assessment](https://www.ameriburn.org/burn-care-team/resources/guidelines-for-burn-patient-referral)

ABA recommends immediate consultation with consideration of transfer for **full-thickness burns, partial-thickness burns >=10% TBSA**, and deep partial/full-thickness injury of the face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, or over joints. Suspected inhalation injury, important comorbidity or trauma, poorly controlled pain, chemical injury, and high-voltage electrical or lightning injury also warrant urgent consultation. Potentially deep burns of any size and smaller partial-thickness injuries merit consultation. Pediatric burns may benefit from referral because of pain, dressing, rehabilitation, caregiver, or safeguarding needs. Consultation does not mean every listed patient automatically requires the same transfer destination. [Current ABA referral criteria](https://www.ameriburn.org/burn-care-team/resources/guidelines-for-burn-patient-referral)

For appropriate outpatient wounds, provide analgesia and a protective dressing suited to depth, exudate, and the planned review interval. Routine systemic antibiotic prophylaxis is not indicated. Do not make silver sulfadiazine the automatic choice for a superficial partial-thickness burn; it can delay healing, and product-specific restrictions matter. Blister treatment and advanced dressings depend on location and clinical assessment, not a single home instruction to open every blister. [Outpatient burn treatment](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463)

Provide analgesia before dressing care, preserve joint movement, manage edema, and arrange rehabilitation. Match change frequency to the dressing. Review new necrosis, drainage/pain, spreading warmth, or stalled epithelialization. Systemic illness may signal serious infection even after a small burn. After healing, address sun protection and scarring; outpatient care requires feasible dressings and reliable follow-up. [Burn dressings, follow-up, and rehabilitation](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Burns_post_acute_care_and_dressings/)

**Clinical Case:** A cook's small dominant-hand scald includes blanching painful skin and a pale, less-sensitive area across a finger joint. Small area does not establish minor injury. Give first aid/analgesia, document circulation/movement, and seek burn advice on depth and function. Consider work and dressing capability in disposition.

### Bites: a tiny opening may hide a deep inoculation

Ask what bit the patient, when, the circumstances, animal availability, and exposure location. Irrigate and assess tissue damage, depth, retained tooth fragments, neurovascular status, and tendon or joint involvement. Cat punctures and hand wounds deserve particular attention. A wound over a knuckle after striking teeth may communicate with the joint or extensor apparatus; its position changes with finger movement. Do not close a possible deep fight bite and discharge the patient with routine skin-infection treatment. Consider safeguarding and blood-borne exposure assessment where relevant. [NICE human and animal bite assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng184/chapter/Recommendations)

IDSA recommends **three to five days of early prophylaxis** for higher-risk animal bites, including immune suppression, asplenia, advanced liver disease, substantial edema, significant hand/face injury, or possible periosteal/joint penetration. Infected bites need aerobic and anaerobic coverage. One adult oral option is **amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg every twelve hours** when allergy, renal function, severity, and oral intake permit; infection treatment duration is not automatically the short prophylaxis course. Human bites may involve *Eikenella*: a first-generation cephalosporin or clindamycin alone is unreliable. Facial closure is a selected exception after meticulous care; routine closure of other bites is not the default. [IDSA recommendations XI-XIV and bite-treatment table 5](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/2/e10/2895845)

Do **not** use the 875-mg amoxicillin strength when **GFR <30 mL/min**; choose a renal-adjusted regimen. Serious beta-lactam allergy or prior amoxicillin-clavulanate-associated cholestatic jaundice/hepatic dysfunction also precludes routine use. Children need weight/formulation-specific dosing, and pregnancy with significant allergy warrants expert selection. Give explicit return precautions for fever, spreading inflammation, worsening pain, or impaired movement. Reassess infection that does not begin improving within **24-48 hours**, or immediately for rapid deterioration. [Amoxicillin-clavulanate renal and contraindication labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=704e99b1-c14a-40bf-adbd-c2794fa92c72), [NICE bite reassessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng184/chapter/Recommendations)

Asplenia and other vulnerability increase the risk of fulminant **Capnocytophaga** infection after dog/cat exposure. Fever, confusion, GI symptoms, purpura, or systemic decline requires urgent assessment despite a small wound; severe disease needs hospital/parenteral treatment. Assess tetanus and rabies/public-health needs separately because antibiotics prevent neither. [CDC Capnocytophaga clinical overview](https://www.cdc.gov/capnocytophaga/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

**Clinical Case:** An asplenic patient has a cat puncture to the hand without fever or much redness. Host and site risk support deep-structure assessment and prophylaxis before cellulitis develops. Explain urgent return for systemic symptoms or impaired finger movement, and assess tetanus/rabies separately.

### Lacerations, foreign bodies, and safe closure

Control bleeding and, before anesthesia when feasible, document distal perfusion, sensation, active movement, and tendon function. Explore with adequate exposure, lighting, analgesia, and anatomic knowledge. Refer suspected tendon, joint, major nerve/vessel, open fracture, or complex facial injury. Adhesive suits selected clean low-tension wounds, not deep disrupted structures. Choose closure by site, depth, contamination, cooperation, and functional/cosmetic needs. [Laceration assessment and repair](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2017/0515/p628)

There is no evidence-based universal six-hour cutoff after which all wounds must stay open. Time contributes to risk, but contamination, mechanism, tissue viability, location, and host factors also matter. An infected, dirty puncture and a clean facial cut are different closure decisions. Delayed closure or healing by secondary intention may be preferable when immediate closure would trap contamination. Routine oral antibiotic prophylaxis is not required for every uncomplicated clean laceration. Set a site-appropriate follow-up and suture-removal plan rather than using one interval for every body region. [Current AFP laceration-care review](https://www.aafp.org/afp/afp-community-blog/laceration-repair-best-practices)

For example, **RCH's pediatric laceration pathway** removes nonabsorbable scalp sutures at **seven days**, forehead sutures at **five to seven days**, limb sutures at **seven to ten days**, and trunk sutures at **ten to fourteen days**. These are site-specific pediatric examples, not universal deadlines for every adult wound or a reason to leave facial sutures until a limb-review visit. Absorbable sutures can avoid a second sedation when appropriate. Reassess perfusion, infection, wound tension, and healing before removal. [RCH laceration closure and removal guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Lacerations/)

Use normal saline or potable tap water for appropriate traumatic-wound irrigation; avoid routinely pouring tissue-toxic antiseptics into the wound. Persistent focal pain or a fragile-object mechanism raises concern for a retained foreign body even when nothing is visible. Radiographs help locate metal and glass; ultrasound is useful for radiolucent wood or plant material. A negative radiograph does not exclude a wooden splinter. Avoid MRI if metallic material may be present. Do not blindly dig near a tendon or neurovascular bundle; deep, complex, or unlocatable material warrants referral. Selected inert fragments can remain when removal would cause greater harm, after discussing risks and follow-up. [Foreign-body evaluation and management](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0615/p740)

### Tetanus prevention is a separate wound decision

CDC distinguishes clean minor wounds from dirty/major wounds such as punctures, contaminated bites, crush injuries, and burns with devitalized tissue. Clean the wound and remove foreign material as appropriate. Assess vaccination and **tetanus immune globulin (TIG)** separately.

| Vaccination history | Clean minor wound | Dirty or major wound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unknown, unvaccinated, or incomplete primary series | Vaccinate; no TIG | Vaccinate and give TIG |
| Complete series; last dose <5 years ago | No booster | No booster ordinarily |
| Complete series; last dose 5 to <10 years ago | No booster | Booster |
| Complete series; last dose >=10 years ago | Booster | Booster |
| HIV or severe immunodeficiency, with any vaccination history | Vaccine as above; no TIG | Vaccine as above; add TIG regardless of history |

TIG is never indicated for a clean minor wound. When indicated for prophylaxis, CDC specifies **250 international units intramuscularly**. It supplies passive protection and does not replace vaccination. Antibiotics are not prescribed to prevent tetanus. [CDC wound-management guidance, September 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Choose the age-appropriate tetanus-containing product and complete missing primary-series doses rather than treating one wound dose as an entire series. Tdap history, pregnancy, and the current vaccine notes affect product selection; see 01E. Wound prophylaxis is distinct from treatment of established tetanus, which requires emergency care. Document vaccine and TIG decisions explicitly so the next clinician can distinguish a completed series from a single dose given during an injury visit. [CDC tetanus vaccination recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/index.html)

### Sun protection and skin-cancer counseling

Use **AAD's protection framework**: shade, protective clothing, broad-brimmed hat, UV-protective eyewear, and sunscreen. Avoid tanning beds; cloud/reflected UV does not eliminate risk. Adapt counseling to work and recreation, with accessible shade breaks, clothing, and supplies rather than impractical avoidance advice. [AAD skin-cancer prevention](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/how-to-prevent)

Use **broad-spectrum, water-resistant SPF 30 or higher** generously before exposure; reapply at least every two hours outdoors and after swimming/sweating per product directions. Cover ears, neck, feet, and exposed scalp. Higher SPF does not permit longer exposure, nor water resistance all-day protection. Address irritation, cost, and formulation preference. Review photosensitizers without abruptly stopping necessary medicines. [AAD sunscreen application](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/how-to-apply-sunscreen)

Keep infants younger than six months out of direct sunlight with shade and protective clothing. If those measures are unavailable, AAP permits a small amount of sunscreen on limited exposed areas such as the face. This is not a reason to plan prolonged infant sun exposure. In older children, combine protection with safe outdoor activity and caregiver modeling; counsel about heat and hydration as separate issues. [AAP infant and child sun safety](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/pages/Sun-Safety.aspx?menu=181310)

USPSTF recommends behavioral counseling for people **six months through 24 years with fair skin types** and selective counseling for older adults with fair skin based on individual risk. The evidence population is not a claim that other skin tones cannot develop cancer or do not need protection. Explain personally relevant behaviors rather than delivering an indiscriminate checklist. [USPSTF behavioral counseling recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/RecommendationStatementFinal/skin-cancer-counseling)

Keep prevention counseling separate from screening and diagnostic evaluation. USPSTF's **I statement for routine clinician visual screening in asymptomatic people** does not mean a changing, bleeding, symptomatic, or nonhealing lesion should be ignored. A suspicious lesion requires evaluation; personal skin-cancer history and defined high-risk states need individualized care. Recognition and biopsy principles, including acral and nail lesions, are covered in 08A. [USPSTF screening population and limitations](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/skin-cancer-screening)

### Immunocompromise, atypical wounds, and referral

Immune-suppressed patients may have aggressive skin cancers, unusual infections, and delayed healing. Coordinate risk-based dermatology surveillance and prompt assessment of changing/nonhealing lesions with the prescribing team. Do not independently stop anti-rejection therapy. Photoprotection complements, not replaces, examination and biopsy. [AAD care of organ-transplant recipients](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/dermatologists-help-organ-transplant-patients-stay-healthy)

An atypical ulcer can be inflammatory, vasculopathic, malignant, infectious, or mixed. **Pyoderma gangrenosum** is a painful neutrophilic ulcerative disease that may worsen after trauma, including excessive debridement. An undermined violaceous edge and rapid deterioration after a procedure should prompt specialist reassessment, not automatic repeated excision. Infection and vascular disease must still be evaluated; appearance alone is not enough to start immune suppression. The immediate task is to recognize diagnostic uncertainty and obtain appropriately planned biopsy/culture and multidisciplinary advice. [Clinical guidance on pyoderma gangrenosum](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1346-8138.16845)

**Clinical Case:** A transplant recipient's forearm ulcer repeatedly crusts, bleeds, and grows despite topicals, without fever or cellulitis. Arrange prompt tissue diagnosis rather than another antibiotic cream; escalate the referral if progression outpaces access. Coordinate medicines with the transplant team without independently withdrawing them.

### Follow-up and recurrence prevention

Confirm that the patient or caregiver can demonstrate dressing care and has supplies and a review appointment. Spreading inflammation, fever, worsening pain, darkening tissue, loss of sensation or movement, or a cold limb requires earlier assessment. After closure, continue mechanism-specific prevention: pressure relief, appropriate footwear, venous care when safe, vascular-risk treatment, and skin protection. Closure does not cure neuropathy, venous hypertension, or impaired mobility.
