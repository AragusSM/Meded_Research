# Medivale Family Medicine

## 07C. Regional Injuries and Sports Care

Limb pain may reflect overload, tissue disruption, nerve compression, infection, or referred disease. Establish whether outpatient rehabilitation is safe, identify what needs protection, and relate imaging to function. Give an immediate plan, recovery goal, and earlier-return triggers.

Joint inflammation and osteoarthritis are in 07A; spinal pain and radiculopathy are in 07B. Bone health, falls, and developmental limp follow in 07D.

### Start with mechanism, function, and threats to the limb

Ask about mechanism, training changes, a pop, swelling timing, inability to continue, locking, instability, numbness, and weakness. Review injuries, operations, medicines, work, equipment, and usual mobility. Match loading goals to the patient's baseline and demands, not merely the tender structure.

Inspect alignment, bruising, wounds, swelling, skin, and gait; palpate bone and examine adjacent joints. Compare active/passive motion, resisted strength, sensation, and circulation. Document neurovascular status before and after splinting or reduction. Repeat pain-inhibited strength testing after analgesia or early review; retained movement does not prove tendon continuity. [AAOS soft-tissue injury principles](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/sprains-strains-and-other-soft-tissue-injuries?webid=24DCE756)

Open fractures, threatened skin, a cold or poorly perfused limb, new neurologic loss, unreduced major dislocation, and suspected compartment syndrome require emergency care. A wound near a fracture may communicate with bone even without visible bone protrusion. Cover and stabilize it, arrange urgent transfer, and address antibiotics and tetanus through the receiving trauma pathway; repeated probing or attempting routine office closure is inappropriate. A hot swollen joint, fever, or rapidly progressive hand infection requires the infection pathway, not a trial of a sports injection.

**Acute compartment syndrome** is pressure-related loss of perfusion within a fascial space. Disproportionate escalating pain, passive-stretch pain, tense swelling, or paresthesia after fracture, crush, reperfusion, or tight immobilization requires urgent surgical assessment, with pressure measurement when needed to guide fasciotomy. Pulses may persist; do not wait for paralysis or try home elevation. Exertional tightness resolving with rest is usually nonemergent, but persistent symptoms need reassessment. [AAOS compartment syndrome](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/compartment-syndrome/)

### Shoulder pain: distinguish painful loading from restricted passive motion

The cuff stabilizes the humeral head. Tendinopathy commonly causes lateral upper-arm pain during elevation/lifting or side-lying, painful resisted abduction/external rotation, and relatively preserved passive motion. Painful-arc, Hawkins, or Neer tests support localization without identifying a single tendon definitively. Radiation/paresthesia warrants neck and neurologic examination; exertional shoulder pain with chest/autonomic symptoms warrants cardiac assessment.

For uncomplicated atraumatic cuff-related pain, start education, temporary adjustment of provocative loads, and progressive resistance or motor-control exercise. Avoid complete disuse. The **2025 tendinopathy guideline** does not recommend imaging simply to confirm the initial working diagnosis; consider imaging and specialist assessment for severe persistent disability or failure to improve despite appropriate care, rather than indefinitely repeating analgesics. Ultrasound can answer many cuff questions; MRI is useful when the diagnostic or surgical question requires it. The guideline's maximum twelve-week conservative window does not apply to a newly disabling traumatic tear. [Rotator cuff tendinopathy guideline](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2025.13182)

An acute injury followed by marked weakness or inability to actively elevate an arm that can be moved passively raises concern for a substantial tear. Exclude fracture/dislocation and arrange timely cuff imaging and orthopedic assessment. An incidental chronic tear in an older adult does not mandate surgery: symptoms, tear features, function, goals, repairability, and response to therapy matter. A single appropriately placed corticosteroid injection may offer short-term pain relief, but it does not restore tendon integrity. Repeated injections and injections near planned repair deserve particular caution. Routine acromioplasty is not a universal solution to cuff symptoms. [AAOS 2025 cuff guideline](https://www.aaos.org/rccpg2025)

**Adhesive capsulitis** restricts active and passive motion, especially external rotation. Diabetes, thyroid disease, and immobilization increase risk, but injury need not precede it. Radiographs exclude arthritis or other structural causes; a classic presentation does not require MRI. Recovery may take months and remain incomplete. Use tolerable stretching, mobility work, and analgesia rather than forceful exercise or prolonged sling use. Glenohumeral steroid injection can facilitate short-term rehabilitation; assess glucose effects. Refer persistent major limitation. [AAOS adhesive capsulitis](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/frozen-shoulder/)

Pain at the acromioclavicular joint is often focal and reproduced by bringing the arm across the chest; glenohumeral arthritis restricts motion and has radiographic joint changes. Anterior pain with resisted elbow flexion or supination may involve the long head of biceps, but a provocative test is not definitive in isolation. After dislocation, assess axillary nerve sensation and deltoid function as well as distal circulation; older adults may also have a cuff tear. Reduction belongs in a setting with appropriate expertise, analgesia, and imaging access, followed by a specific rehabilitation and recurrence plan.

**Clinical Case:** A 61-year-old delivery worker loses active elevation and external-rotation strength after catching a falling cabinet despite nearly full passive elevation. Even with normal fracture films, arrange timely cuff-tear assessment rather than waiting three months on an atraumatic-tendinopathy pathway.

### Elbow and hand complaints: localize the tendon or nerve

Lateral elbow tendinopathy causes extensor-origin pain with gripping or resisted wrist extension. It is not simply acute inflammation. Adjust load, grip, and equipment and prescribe graduated exercise with short-term symptom relief. Atypical distribution, weakness, restricted motion, or course warrants assessment for radial neuropathy, cervical referral, or arthritis. Steroid-related relief does not establish durable tendon recovery. [AAOS lateral elbow pain](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/tennis-elbow-lateral-epicondylitis/)

Medial elbow pain reproduced by resisted wrist flexion/pronation suggests flexor-pronator loading, whereas ring/little-finger tingling points toward ulnar neuropathy. A swollen olecranon bursa may follow pressure or trauma, but erythema, tenderness, a skin break, or systemic illness raises concern for infection. Aspiration and antimicrobial decisions depend on that concern rather than treating every bursal swelling with steroid. Do not inject an undiagnosed infected region.

**Carpal tunnel syndrome** reflects median nerve compression at the wrist. Nocturnal paresthesia in the thumb, index, middle, and radial ring finger, shaking the hand for relief, and later thumb-abduction weakness fit the syndrome. Thenar wasting or persistent sensory loss suggests more advanced damage. Sensation over the thenar eminence may be spared because the palmar cutaneous branch travels outside the tunnel. A neck/root pattern, diffuse neuropathy, or prominent little-finger symptoms should prompt reconsideration. [AAOS median entrapment symptoms and assessment](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/carpal-tunnel-syndrome/)

The **2024 AAOS guideline** supports a validated clinical tool such as CTS-6 instead of routine ultrasound or electrodiagnostic testing in every typical adult presentation. Additional testing helps when localization, severity, another neuropathy, or procedural planning is uncertain. A neutral-position night splint and adjustment of aggravating wrist positions are practical initial measures in mild disease. Injection may help temporarily, but AAOS finds no long-term improvement from corticosteroid injection. Progressive weakness, wasting, persistent deficits, or unsuccessful conservative care warrants surgical assessment. Pregnancy-associated symptoms often improve after delivery; ongoing or severe impairment still needs evaluation. [AAOS CTS recommendations](https://www.aaos.org/globalassets/quality-and-practice-resources/carpal-tunnel/carpal-tunnel-2024/orthoguidelines_infographic_cts-6-5-24.pdf)

**Ulnar entrapment** at the elbow causes ring/little-finger symptoms with sustained flexion or medial pressure. Assess finger abduction/adduction, grip, and intrinsic bulk. Reduce elbow pressure/flexion, including during sleep; fitted night protection may help. Refer progressive weakness or atrophy promptly because recovery may be incomplete. Selective electrodiagnostic testing clarifies elbow, wrist, or proximal localization; percussion alone is not diagnostic. [AAOS cubital tunnel syndrome](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/ulnar-nerve-entrapment-at-the-elbow)

| Finding | Likely localization | Practical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radial wrist pain with thumb use | First extensor compartment | De Quervain pattern, not median sensory loss |
| Palmar catching at a digit's base | Flexor tendon/A1 pulley | Triggering differs from diffuse joint synovitis |
| Nocturnal thumb-to-middle-finger tingling | Median nerve at wrist | Examine thenar strength and other neuropathy sites |
| Ring/little-finger symptoms with elbow flexion | Ulnar nerve near elbow | Examine intrinsic hand strength, not just elbow tenderness |

**De Quervain tenosynovitis** involves the abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis in the first dorsal compartment. Grasping, lifting a baby, and thumb motion can provoke radial-styloid pain; pregnancy/postpartum status is relevant. Local tenderness and a carefully performed provocative maneuver support the diagnosis, but recent trauma with scaphoid tenderness needs a fracture assessment. Modify painful handling, use a thumb-spica splint when helpful, and consider a correctly placed sheath injection for persistent symptoms; avoid injecting into the tendon itself. [AAOS De Quervain care](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/de-quervains-tendinosis/)

**Trigger finger** results from impaired flexor tendon passage through a pulley, usually A1. A tender palmar nodule with catching or locking distinguishes it from a sensory entrapment syndrome. Diabetes increases its likelihood and can affect treatment response. Activity changes, splinting, or a sheath injection may help; persistent locking or impaired function merits hand referral. A new swollen, diffusely tender finger after a puncture is not routine triggering: fusiform swelling, a flexed resting position, sheath tenderness, and pain on passive extension suggest **flexor sheath infection**. Refer urgently for hand surgical care and antibiotics; absence of the full group of signs does not make delay safe. [AAOS trigger finger](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/trigger-finger/), [BSSH flexor sheath infection standard](https://www.bssh.ac.uk/_userfiles/pages/files/professionals/Trauma%20standards/10%20Flexor%20sheath%20infection.pdf)

**Clinical Case:** A musician develops little-finger numbness while rehearsing with a deeply flexed elbow. Examine the ulnar distribution and intrinsic muscles rather than assuming carpal tunnel syndrome. Modify positioning and escalate for weakness or persistent sensory loss.

### A jammed finger may have lost a tendon or ligament

After hand trauma, test active flexion and extension at each joint, inspect wounds/nails, and check sensation and perfusion. Obtain radiographs when fracture or avulsion is suspected; a normal film does not prove tendon continuity.

| Pattern | Structure at risk | Initial protection and follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drooping DIP joint that cannot actively extend after forced flexion | Terminal extensor injury: mallet finger | Keep the DIP continuously extended in a fitted splint, leaving other joints mobile; uncomplicated closed injuries commonly need 6-8 weeks of full-time splinting, including supported extension during hygiene |
| Loss of active DIP flexion after forceful pulling on a flexed finger | Flexor tendon avulsion: jersey finger | Splint and arrange prompt hand-surgical assessment; do not wait weeks for a sprain to improve |
| Ulnar thumb-MCP pain, weak pinch or instability after forced abduction | Thumb ulnar collateral ligament tear | Thumb-spica protection and radiographs; instability or a complete tear requires early hand assessment |

Mallet injuries with an open wound, nail-bed injury, subluxation, or a large avulsion need specialist assessment rather than a splint-only assumption. Flexor lacerations can also damage nerves/vessels, and partial injury may retain some movement. A cold or poorly perfused digit requires emergency care. [AAOS mallet finger](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/mallet-finger-baseball-finger/), [AAOS flexor tendon injuries](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/flexor-tendon-injuries/), [AAOS thumb ligament injury](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/sprained-thumb/)

### Hip and knee pain: distinguish location, instability, and true locking

Groin pain with reduced motion suggests intra-articular hip disease; side-lying pain with lateral tenderness suggests greater trochanteric pain involving tendons and/or bursae. Posterior pain may be spinal. Assess gait, strength, and passive rotation. Lateral symptoms often respond to load adjustment and strengthening; injection is selective. Fever, inability to bear weight, or severe passive-motion pain warrants urgent infection/fracture assessment. [AAOS lateral hip pain](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/hip-bursitis/)

Pain after repetitive loading may represent a **bone stress injury** even when initial radiographs are normal. New training volume, inadequate energy intake, low bone density, medicines, and previous stress injury matter. Focal pain progressing from exercise-only to walking or rest is concerning. Suspected femoral-neck stress injury warrants stopping impact, protected or nonweight-bearing according to clinical severity, urgent imaging, and specialist assessment because displacement has serious consequences. MRI can identify an occult stress injury. High-risk sites need a different plan from uncomplicated soft-tissue overload; do not prescribe more exercise while awaiting proof on a repeat film. [AAOS bone stress injuries](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/stress-fractures/)

At the knee, a twisting injury with a pop, early substantial effusion, and giving way raises concern for an ACL injury; Lachman testing assesses anterior translation and its endpoint. Pain and guarding can make an early examination inconclusive. Valgus and varus stress assess collateral structures, while a posterior drawer helps assess the PCL. Not every isolated ligament injury requires surgery. Instability, associated injuries, activity demands, and rehabilitation goals determine referral and treatment. If reconstruction is indicated for an acute isolated ACL tear, AAOS favors early reconstruction because additional meniscal/cartilage injury risk increases within three months; this is not an instruction to operate on every tear. [AAOS ACL guidance](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-updates-guideline-for-management-of-acl-injuries/)

Joint-line pain after twisting, delayed effusion, and catching can indicate a meniscal injury, but incidental degenerative tears are common. An acutely displaced tear that **physically prevents extension** deserves prompt orthopedic assessment. Pain-limited movement or momentary catching is not automatically a locked knee. AAOS's acute isolated-meniscus guideline favors MRI when needed and preservation of functional meniscal tissue when surgery is chosen; it does not cover degenerative tears, root tears, or combined ACL injury as though these were the same population. Nondisplaced tears may improve with rehabilitation. [AAOS acute meniscus recommendations](https://www.aaos.org/globalassets/quality-and-practice-resources/acute-meniscal-pathology/orthoguidelines_infographic_amp.v2.pdf)

Anterior pain with stairs, squats, running, or prolonged flexion without a large effusion suggests patellofemoral pain. Review training, strength, movement control, and footwear; emphasize progressive hip/quadriceps exercise. Effusion, lost active extension, focal bone pain, or instability warrants reassessment. Failed straight-leg raise after injury suggests patellar/quadriceps tendon disruption or patellar fracture. Examine the hip in adolescents with knee pain; see 07D. [AAOS patellofemoral pain](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/patellofemoral-pain-syndrome/)

### Ankle and foot pain: do not let the sprain label hide another injury

Inversion commonly injures the lateral ankle ligaments, particularly the anterior talofibular ligament. Examine the malleoli, fifth-metatarsal base, navicular, Achilles, and proximal fibula, not only the swollen lateral soft tissue. External-rotation injury or pain above the ankle may indicate syndesmotic disruption; proximal fibular tenderness can accompany a Maisonneuve injury. Plantar midfoot bruising, marked midfoot tenderness, or pain with loading raises concern for **Lisfranc injury**. Initial nonweight-bearing radiographs can miss instability. Protect from weight bearing and obtain appropriate specialist-directed weight-bearing views when feasible, CT, or MRI rather than clearing a suspicious foot on a normal first film. [AAOS Lisfranc injury](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/lisfranc-midfoot-injury), [ACR occult foot injury imaging](https://gravitas.acr.org/ACPortal/GetDataForOneScenario?senarioId=5276)

For an uncomplicated lateral sprain, use external support and progressive weight bearing matched to pain and stability. Begin protected movement and then strength, balance, and neuromuscular work; do not substitute weeks of rest for rehabilitation. Ice may provide symptom relief but does not replace loading progression. The lateral-ankle guideline allows **up to ten days of immobilization for more severe injuries**, not routine prolonged casting for every sprain. Reassess within the early recovery period if function is not improving; persistent pain or instability warrants reconsideration of occult fracture, osteochondral injury, tendon disease, or syndesmotic injury. [Lateral ankle sprain rehabilitation guideline](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/jospt.2021.0302.pdf)

An **Achilles rupture** may feel like a blow to the calf during push-off. Assess a palpable gap, resting foot position, push-off function, and plantar flexion with calf squeeze. Absent expected plantar flexion on the Thompson test supports rupture. Some active plantar flexion can persist through other muscles, so that movement alone cannot exclude it. Protect the tendon in plantar flexion and arrange urgent orthopedic assessment rather than prescribing calf stretching. Surgery and structured nonoperative functional rehabilitation are both possible; rerupture risk, wound/nerve risk, patient goals, and access to a reliable rehabilitation protocol influence the choice. [AAOS Achilles rupture](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/achilles-tendon-rupture-tear/)

Medial plantar heel pain worst on the first steps after rest suggests plantar fasciopathy. Local tenderness, calf/plantar fascia flexibility, footwear, and loading history guide care. Start activity adjustment, stretching, and supportive footwear; a heel spur does not itself identify the pain generator or mandate surgery. Atypical rest pain, neurologic symptoms, trauma, fever, or focal calcaneal bone pain warrants a different evaluation. Avoid routine repeated steroid injection because tissue injury and plantar fascia rupture are concerns. Posterior heel tendinopathy also needs a load-based plan, but a sudden loss of function changes the pathway to rupture assessment. [AAOS plantar heel pain](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/plantar-fasciitis-and-bone-spurs/)

### Use decision rules for the question they actually answer

Decision rules reduce unnecessary radiographs in eligible patients with acute trauma; they do not establish that a joint is healthy. Use an alert, cooperative examination with reliable sensation and the correct age, injury timing, and exclusions. Do not use a negative rule to overrule concern about an open injury, gross deformity, neurovascular compromise, neuropathic foot, or another dangerous presentation. The Ottawa group describes the knee rule for adult minor trauma; pediatric applications need their own validated pathway. ACR ankle criteria include appropriately assessed children aged five years or older. [Ottawa developer's current rule descriptions](https://ohri.ca/en/find-researcher/istiell), [ACR ankle imaging scope](https://gravitas.acr.org/ACPortal/GetDataForOneScenario?senarioId=5406)

| Rule and pain region | Findings that support radiographs | Important boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ottawa ankle: malleolar-zone pain | Posterior edge/tip tenderness at either malleolus, including distal 6 cm; or inability to take four steps both immediately and at assessment | Ankle rule is not a complete foot or tendon examination |
| Ottawa foot: midfoot-zone pain | Navicular or fifth-metatarsal-base bone tenderness; or inability to take four steps both immediately and at assessment | Does not exclude every midfoot injury, including subtle Lisfranc disruption |
| Ottawa knee: eligible acute adult injury | Any of: age at least 55; isolated patellar tenderness; fibular-head tenderness; inability to flex to 90 degrees; inability to take four steps both immediately and at assessment | Screens for fracture, not ACL or meniscal injury |

Use the specified pain zone and bone landmarks. For inability to bear weight, **both time points** matter; limping can still count as taking steps. Current walking ability does not negate bone tenderness. Keep full Ottawa rule cards and exclusions available at the point of care. [BC guideline's Ottawa application](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/health/practitioner-professional-resources/bc-guidelines/ankle-injury), [Adult knee-rule validation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7476189/)

Plain films are usually the first study for suspected acute fracture, but a normal film does not exclude all fractures. After a fall on the hand, anatomic snuffbox or scaphoid-tubercle tenderness merits protection and timely definitive assessment for a scaphoid fracture because nonunion and avascular injury can occur. **NICE NG38 recommends considering MRI as first-line imaging after a thorough examination for suspected scaphoid fracture.** Where immediate MRI is unavailable, immobilization and an explicit local imaging/follow-up plan are essential; do not discharge solely on a negative first radiograph. Stable injuries need a documented alignment, immobilization, skin/neurovascular, and follow-up plan; displaced, intra-articular, rotationally malaligned, or unstable injuries may need specialist treatment. [NICE noncomplex fracture recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng38/chapter/recommendations)

Match pain treatment to the person. Kidney disease, dehydration, anticoagulation, ulcer history, and cardiovascular disease can make systemic NSAIDs inappropriate; acetaminophen requires attention to liver disease and total combination-product exposure. In pregnancy, avoid NSAIDs from **20 weeks** unless necessary under clinician supervision, and at **30 weeks and later** because of additional ductal risk; see 07A for monitoring and the prescribed aspirin exception. Older adults need attention to falls and splint-related skin pressure; children need growth-plate assessment rather than automatic adult rules. Worsening pain, numbness, cold digits, or excessive swelling requires urgent reassessment. [FDA NSAID pregnancy safety](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic)

**Clinical Case:** A walker with lateral ankle swelling, midfoot pain, and fifth-metatarsal-base tenderness can take four steps. Bone tenderness still supports foot radiographs. Persistent plantar bruising or midfoot instability after normal films requires protection and further evaluation for Lisfranc injury.

### Concussion is a clinical brain injury, not a normal-scan diagnosis

A head impact or force transmitted through the body can produce headache, slowed thinking, dizziness, visual symptoms, imbalance, irritability, sleep disturbance, or amnesia. Loss of consciousness is not required. Remove the athlete from play when concussion is suspected; there is no same-day return after a diagnosed concussion. Assess airway, cervical injury, neurologic status, and the possibility of a more serious intracranial injury. Worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, increasing drowsiness, focal deficits, or abnormal behavior requires emergency evaluation. Symptoms may emerge after the initial sideline check. [CDC concussion recognition and danger signs](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/signs-symptoms/index.html)

Concussion is not ruled out by a normal CT. Use age-appropriate validated head-injury decision pathways when deciding whether to image for structural injury, rather than routinely imaging to diagnose uncomplicated concussion. Assess symptoms, cognition, balance, vestibular/ocular findings, sleep, mood, prior injuries, and learning needs. A checklist or computerized test does not independently determine recovery or clearance. Give written warning signs and a follow-up plan, with a responsible observer and individualized advice about driving, school, work, and activities with fall or collision risk. [CDC pediatric mild-TBI guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Use the **Amsterdam consensus** approach: relative rest for the initial **24-48 hours**, not dark-room isolation or complete physical/cognitive inactivity. Allow ordinary low-risk daily activity and then symptom-limited aerobic exercise. Early mild, brief symptom increase can be acceptable during treatment; do not generalize that allowance to collision exposure. Reinstate learning gradually with practical accommodations, such as rest breaks, reduced workload, and temporary test adjustment. Full return to learning should precede unrestricted sport. Clearance for later stages requires resolution of concussion-related symptoms and findings, including with exertion, and authorization by an appropriate clinician. [Amsterdam sport-concussion consensus](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/11/695)

CDC's supervised six-step progression is regular activities/school, light aerobic work, moderate activity, heavy noncontact training, appropriate controlled full-contact practice, and competition. Each step takes **at least 24 hours**, not a guaranteed six-day recovery. Stop for recurrent symptoms, contact the clinician, and resume the previous tolerated stage after reassessment/recovery. [CDC return-to-sport progression](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/guidelines/returning-to-sports.html)

Persistent dizziness, headache, neck symptoms, mood change, or school difficulty warrants targeted reassessment and appropriate vestibular, cervical, exercise, or psychological treatment. Coordinate confidentially with school and athletic staff under state clearance rules. Avoid attributing symptoms to permanent damage or poor effort; retirement decisions are individualized, not based on a fixed concussion count. [NATA 2024 concussion bridge statement](https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/bridge_statement_management_of_sport_related_concussion.pdf)

CDC recommends specialist referral for children/adolescents whose symptoms do not resolve as expected after **two to four weeks** of standard care; worsening symptoms require earlier assessment. Contact-sport clearance requires being symptom-free with exertion **without pain medicine masking symptoms**. [CDC pediatric mild-TBI safety checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/hcp/clinical-guidance/pediatric-mtbi.html)

**Clinical Case:** A 17-year-old's headache improves two days after a soccer collision, but reading still provokes symptoms. Use school accommodations and low-risk symptom-limited activity rather than isolation. Tolerating jogging does not clear heading drills; assess cognition, exertion, and supervised progression separately.

### Exertional collapse requires a cause, not automatic water

Collapse during activity can reflect cardiac arrest, heat stroke, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, anaphylaxis, or another emergency. Begin with responsiveness, airway, breathing, circulation, and emergency activation. An unresponsive person who is not breathing normally needs prompt arrest assessment and CPR/AED use according to the responder's training; brief jerking does not establish a primary seizure. Syncope during exertion is particularly concerning for cardiac disease. Post-finish-line venous pooling can be benign only after dangerous alternatives have been assessed; do not diagnose it solely because the patient is an endurance athlete. [AHA 2025 basic life support](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-basic-life-support), [AHA/ACC athlete cardiovascular considerations](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/clinical-considerations-for-competitive-sports-participation-for-athletes-with-cv-abnormalities)

| Presentation | Leading concern | Immediate priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unresponsive, absent normal breathing | Cardiac arrest | Activate EMS; CPR and AED |
| Hot exercise setting with confusion or ataxia | Exertional heat stroke | Stabilize and begin rapid whole-body cooling |
| Prolonged exercise, excess fluid intake, headache/confusion | Exercise-associated hyponatremia | Urgent sodium assessment; avoid indiscriminate water |
| Severe muscle pain/weakness, dark urine | Rhabdomyolysis or related exertional injury | Urgent renal, electrolyte, and muscle-injury assessment |
| Collapse after stopping, rapid improvement supine | Possible postural exercise-associated collapse | Exclude heat, cardiac, metabolic, and other dangerous causes |

**Exertional heat stroke** combines heat stress with CNS dysfunction; sweating may persist. Use rectal core temperature in the field because forehead, oral, and ear readings can mislead. Do not delay cooling, and a delayed or postcooling reading below 40 C does not exclude it. After airway/circulation assessment, use supervised whole-body cold-water immersion when safe, protecting the airway and coordinating EMS. WMS targets **38.3-38.8 C**. Antipyretics do not correct the mechanism. [WMS 2024 heat-illness guideline](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10806032241227924)

Heat exhaustion causes weakness, dizziness, nausea, and reduced performance without the persistent central neurologic dysfunction of heat stroke. Move to a cool setting, remove excess equipment, and use oral replacement if alert and able to swallow safely. Deterioration or failure to improve promptly requires reassessment and escalation. Heat acclimatization, environmental heat-load monitoring, rest/cooling opportunities, equipment adjustment, and attention to illness or thermoregulation-impairing medicines reduce risk. Hydration should avoid both dehydration and forced overdrinking; fluid access matters more than a rigid universal volume. [WMS heat prevention and field-care summary](https://wms.org/magazine/1540/Heat-Illness-CPG/default.aspx)

**Exercise-associated hyponatremia** can follow prolonged activity when water intake exceeds excretory capacity, often with sustained vasopressin effects. Headache, vomiting, confusion, and seizures may resemble heat illness. Weight gain can support overhydration but is not required. Obtain sodium promptly when available; do not reflexively give more hypotonic fluid. Encephalopathy requires emergency care, including monitored hypertonic saline. Treat coexisting emergencies, including heat stroke. See 06D for sodium correction principles. [WMS hyponatremia guidance](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1016/j.wem.2019.11.003)

Exertional rhabdomyolysis releases muscle contents, risking hyperkalemia and kidney injury. Marked muscle pain, weakness, swelling, or dark urine after unusually intense work deserves urgent CK, renal function, electrolyte, and urine assessment. A urine heme signal with few erythrocytes can reflect myoglobin, but no single urine finding determines severity. Review heat, medicines/supplements, infection, and sickling risk when relevant; do not use race as a substitute for history or documented status. Fluids and disposition depend on organ injury, electrolytes, volume, comorbidities, and follow-up reliability rather than a universal outpatient water prescription. [CDC rhabdomyolysis recognition/testing](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/rhabdo/signs-symptoms/), [CDC treatment principles](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/rhabdo/treatment/index.html)

**Clinical Case:** After a long charity walk, a participant who drank beyond thirst becomes nauseated and confused. A normal forehead temperature excludes neither heat stroke nor hyponatremic encephalopathy. Assess urgently, check core temperature and glucose, and obtain sodium rather than reflexively giving more water.

### Participation decisions combine prevention, recovery, and shared goals

The **AAP sixth-edition PPE framework** places assessment in the medical home, ideally during routine supervision and at least **six weeks before preseason practice**. Review personal/family history, injuries/concussion, medicines/supplements, disease control, nutrition, endocrine/menstrual symptoms, mental health, and accommodations, including private adolescent discussion. Tailor examination to history, including vitals, cardiovascular findings, vision, and musculoskeletal function. Investigate positive findings rather than relying on the form. [AAP 2026 PPE framework](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/preparticipation-evaluation)

Ask specifically about exertional chest discomfort, unexplained syncope, disproportionate breathlessness, palpitations, and relatives with premature sudden death or inherited cardiac disease. A concerning history or examination requires diagnostic evaluation before unrestricted clearance. AAP's current public PPE guidance does not recommend routine laboratory tests or ECG for everyone. The **2025 AHA/ACC statement** also considers ECG-inclusive screening reasonable when athlete-specific interpretation and equitable downstream evaluation are available. These are scoped screening policies, not conflicting instructions to withhold an ECG from a symptomatic athlete. Known cardiovascular disease needs condition-specific sports-cardiology assessment and shared decision-making; it is not an automatic lifetime ban from all exercise. [AHA/ACC screening and participation summary](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/latest-research-finds-people-with-certain-heart-conditions-may-play-competitive-sports)

**Relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs)** can affect athletes of any sex when intake does not support training and normal physiology. Recurrent bone stress injury, fatigue, impaired recovery, menstrual dysfunction, low libido, or performance decline warrants assessment even without a low BMI or a disclosed eating disorder. Evaluate other explanations, including pregnancy when relevant, anemia, thyroid disease, and systemic illness. Treatment restores adequate fueling and adjusts training, with sports medicine, dietetic, and mental-health support as needed. Severe physiologic instability or serious eating-disorder features requires urgent care; do not simply recommend supplements while permitting unsafe training. [IOC REDs consensus](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/17/1073)

Return after a limb injury depends on tissue healing, pain, motion, strength, balance, endurance, confidence, and sport-specific performance, not only elapsed days or a normal radiograph. The **PAASS ankle framework** organizes pain, ankle impairments, athlete perception, sensorimotor control, and sport/functional performance; it does not provide one universally validated passing cutoff. Check hopping, landing, direction change, and completion of practice when appropriate. A person who can walk pain-free may still be unready for repeated cutting. Continue preventive balance/strength work and consider bracing for recurrent ankle injury. [PAASS return-to-sport consensus](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2021/06/22/bjsports-2021-104087)

**Clinical Case:** A basketball player walks with little pain one week after a lateral sprain but remains unstable on single-leg landing. Continue support and rehabilitation, reassess sport-specific function, and progress to full practice before competition. Walking alone is insufficient for clearance.

Recovery after heat stroke, severe electrolyte disturbance, or rhabdomyolysis is not established by feeling better at the sideline. Arrange medical follow-up to confirm recovery of affected organs and laboratory abnormalities, review the cause, and plan a supervised, gradual return with appropriate heat preparation. Normal walking or a single lower CK result is not a universal clearance test. Similarly, a cardiac warning symptom needs its own completed evaluation before unrestricted activity; a reassuring general sports examination cannot override it. [CDC severity-based rhabdomyolysis care](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/rhabdo/treatment/index.html), [AHA/ACC condition-specific participation framework](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/clinical-considerations-for-competitive-sports-participation-for-athletes-with-cv-abnormalities)

Document participation restrictions, reassessment triggers, responsible clinician, and emergency plan. Share necessary eligibility information without disclosing confidential history. Asthma, diabetes, disability, and prior injury require individualized management and accommodations rather than reflex exclusion.
