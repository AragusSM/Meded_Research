# Medivale Family Medicine

## 07B. Back and Neck Pain

Most primary-care back and neck pain needs neither immediate imaging nor surgery. Identify emergency, inflammatory, neurologic, structural, and visceral causes while supporting activity. Pain severity and dramatic imaging do not alone establish a surgical disorder; mild pain does not justify observing progressive neurologic dysfunction.

Joint disease is discussed in 07A; regional injuries and bone health follow in 07C-07D. The VA/DoD low-back-pain pathway guides general treatment; infection, malignant compression, and inflammatory disease require their own cause-specific care.

### Define the clinical problem before choosing a test

Record location, onset, radiation, course, triggers, and effects on sleep, walking, hand use, work, and caregiving. Review prior episodes, treatment, injuries, cancer, infection/procedures, immune suppression, osteoporosis, medicines, and substance exposures. Distinguish local pain from limb radiation, objective weakness, sensory change, and incoordination. Several contributors may coexist; lifting does not prove strain or exclude fragility fracture.

**Nonspecific pain** means that the clinical assessment has not identified a particular disease requiring its own pathway; it does not mean that the pain is imaginary. Muscles, ligaments, joints, discs, and nervous-system responses may contribute without a reliable single tissue label. Using ACP's duration convention, acute pain lasts **less than four weeks**, subacute pain **four to twelve weeks**, and chronic pain **more than twelve weeks**. Duration guides reassessment, not permission to ignore new warning symptoms. [ACP nonradicular-pain framework](https://www.acponline.org/acp-newsroom/american-college-of-physicians-issues-guideline-for-treating-nonradicular-low-back-pain), [NIAMS back-pain mechanisms](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/back-pain)

Start with appearance and vital signs. Observe gait, posture, transfers, movement, midline/paraspinal tenderness, and relevant limb joints. Add abdominal, vascular, skin, or pelvic examination when indicated. Document strength, sensation, reflex asymmetry, and change from baseline. With neck and hand symptoms, examine the legs and gait too. Movement-related pain alone does not establish benign mechanical disease. [NIAMS back-pain assessment](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/back-pain/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

### Use warning features to direct the urgency and the investigation

Warning features modify probability and urgency; they do not all mandate the same scan. Prior cancer with progressive symptoms, new deficits, or systemic illness carries more weight than isolated age or disturbed sleep. Interpret combinations, trajectory, examination, and vulnerability. Reassess changing or unexpectedly persistent pain; one absent classic sign cannot exclude every emergency.

| Presentation | Main concern | Appropriate direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New bladder/perineal change with back or leg symptoms | Cauda equina or another compressive process | Emergency assessment and urgent MRI pathway |
| Hand clumsiness with gait change or upper-motor-neuron findings | Cervical spinal cord dysfunction | Urgent MRI and spinal assessment; emergency care for rapid decline |
| Focal spinal pain with bacteremia, fever, immune suppression, or recent procedure | Vertebral/disc infection or epidural abscess | Urgent infection and neurologic evaluation; MRI and cultures |
| Cancer history with progressive pain and new neurologic findings | Malignant cord or cauda equina compression | Emergency oncology/spinal pathway, not routine outpatient radiography |
| New focal pain after low-energy stress in a patient at fracture risk | Vertebral fragility fracture | Targeted imaging and stability/neurologic assessment |
| Pain with circulatory collapse, abdominal findings, or stroke symptoms | Vascular or other extraspinal emergency | Resuscitation and cause-specific emergency evaluation |

Abdominal symptoms, hypotension, syncope, or vascular findings may indicate an aortic emergency. Flank pain, fever, urinary symptoms, or hematuria suggests renal infection or stones; pelvic, pancreatic, and other abdominal disease can also refer pain. Exertional/autonomic chest or arm symptoms require ischemia assessment despite neck tenderness. Use cardiovascular or renal pathways rather than lumbar imaging for an extraspinal concern.

### Cauda equina syndrome can precede obvious retention

Lumbosacral root compression can impair bladder, bowel, sexual, sensory, and motor function. Ask about new hesitancy, reduced awareness of urine flow/bladder filling, altered wiping sensation, genital numbness, lost rectal fullness, sexual change, and leg weakness. Do not wait for painless retention, overflow, paralysis, or the full classic cluster. Record onset and progression; subtle reported dysfunction can precede dramatic examination findings.

GIRFT's pathway, revised through **March 2026**, calls for emergency referral when recent symptoms suggest cauda equina dysfunction. A normal or small postvoid residual, including one **below 200 mL**, does not exclude it. Bladder scanning is an adjunct, not a gatekeeper for MRI. Normal anal tone likewise cannot independently clear the patient. Arrange emergency imaging through the receiving service; the GIRFT operational target is as soon as possible and within **four hours of the MRI request**, not four hours of safe waiting in primary care. Deteriorating symptoms remain urgent even when onset was not recent. [GIRFT current suspected-cauda-equina pathway](https://gettingitrightfirsttime.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/National-Suspected-Cauda-Equina-Pathway-September-2025.pdf)

**Clinical Case:** A 46-year-old with unilateral leg pain can void but reports reduced stream sensation and numbness when wiping. Strength is preserved and postvoid residual is 85 mL. New sacral symptoms still require emergency assessment for incomplete cauda equina syndrome. Arrange the receiving destination directly; do not wait for incontinence.

When imaging confirms clinically relevant compression, prompt spinal surgical decision-making is required. Stabilize and treat the actual cause; disc herniation, hematoma, abscess, and tumor are not interchangeable. There is **no established routine steroid treatment for disc-related cauda equina syndrome**; WFNS identifies inadequate evidence for benefit. A steroid prescription must not replace imaging or decompression. The malignant-compression steroid pathway below addresses a different indication. If MRI is genuinely contraindicated or inaccessible, the receiving radiology/spinal team must arrange an appropriate alternative or transfer rather than accept an uninvestigated emergency. [WFNS cauda equina/conus recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10943478/)

### Recognize spinal infection without requiring the classic triad

Vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, and epidural abscess may present with focal persistent back or neck pain before fever or neurologic loss. Ask about bloodstream infection, endocarditis, skin infection, injection-drug exposure, dialysis, diabetes, immune-modifying drugs, and spinal procedures. Recent **Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia** followed by new spinal pain is particularly concerning. A normal blood leukocyte count or absence of fever does not eliminate the possibility. Neurologic findings, instability, sepsis, or rapid deterioration change the urgency immediately.

The IDSA native-vertebral-osteomyelitis pathway includes neurologic assessment, **two sets of blood cultures**, ESR/CRP, and spinal MRI. If the patient is stable, obtaining an organism before empiric treatment can improve directed care; selected cases need image-guided tissue sampling. Neurologic compromise, sepsis, or hemodynamic instability warrants immediate treatment and surgical assessment without waiting for a scheduled biopsy. CSF sampling is not a routine substitute for imaging an epidural process. Antimicrobial selection, route, duration, drainage, and stabilization depend on the organism, extent, and response. [IDSA adult vertebral infection guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/vertebral-osteomyelitis/)

**Clinical Case:** A 59-year-old receiving hemodialysis develops focal thoracic pain two weeks after a bloodstream infection. Despite being afebrile and attributing pain to sleeping in a chair, he needs urgent reassessment, cultures, and appropriate imaging. Neither preserved strength nor analgesic relief excludes spinal infection.

### Malignancy and fragility fractures need distinct pathways

Consider malignancy with a relevant cancer history, progressive focal pain, unexplained systemic features, or neurologic dysfunction. A normal plain film does not exclude epidural tumor or cord compression. The NICE MSCC pathway treats suspected compression with neurologic symptoms/signs as an oncologic emergency, with MRI as soon as possible and **within 24 hours**. Its adult regimen is **dexamethasone 16 mg orally, or an equivalent parenteral dose, as soon as possible**, then 16 mg daily while awaiting surgery or radiotherapy, with specialist-directed tapering afterward. Monitor glucose and offer proton-pump-inhibitor acid suppression. If imaging excludes spinal metastases and MSCC after empiric dexamethasone, discontinue it. Do not apply this regimen to ordinary disc prolapse. [NICE malignant spinal-compression recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng234/chapter/recommendations)

Without neurologic findings, suspected lymphoma or myeloma may require specialist advice before steroids because treatment can interfere with diagnostic tissue assessment. This is not a reason to delay emergency care when neurologic compression is present. Coordinate spinal stability, safe movement, analgesia, biopsy, surgery and/or radiotherapy through the receiving team. Avoid having a patient with possible instability repeatedly walk or perform provocative exercises merely to complete an office examination.

A vertebral compression fracture may occur after a fall, bending, coughing, or little recalled trauma in a person with osteoporosis, glucocorticoid exposure, or another bone disorder. Assess focal pain, height/kyphosis change, neurologic function, and the possibility of pathologic rather than osteoporotic fracture. Targeted radiographs can identify collapse; MRI or CT is selected for acuity, occult injury, posterior-wall/canal involvement, malignancy, or procedural planning. MRI marrow edema can support a recent symptomatic fracture but does not replace clinical correlation. [ACR vertebral compression-fracture imaging](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/70545/Narrative/)

Treat pain, support safe mobilization, assess stability, and address the bone disorder. Bracing or vertebral augmentation suits selected patients, not every radiographic deformity; an incidental healed wedge may not explain current pain. Neurologic impairment or instability requires urgent assessment. Arrange secondary-cause evaluation and fracture prevention through 07D.

### Lumbar radicular pain is not the same as every referred leg ache

Disc material and degenerative narrowing can mechanically compress and chemically irritate a nerve root. **Radicular pain** follows a nerve-root-related pattern; **radiculopathy** includes objective motor, sensory, or reflex dysfunction. Buttock or thigh pain can also arise from the hip, sacroiliac region, muscles, peripheral nerves, or vascular disease. Sciatica is a clinical symptom description, not proof of one particular scan lesion. Symptoms below the knee and neuropathic qualities support a root-related process but are not a complete diagnostic test. [AAOS lumbar disc mechanism and assessment](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/herniated-disk-in-the-lower-back/)

Compare resisted lower-limb power. Heel/toe walking, supported heel raises when safe, and great-toe extension can reveal functional weakness. A straight-leg raise should reproduce familiar radiating leg symptoms, not merely local back pain or hamstring tightness; a crossed maneuver may support the diagnosis. Femoral stretch assesses upper-lumbar roots. No maneuver alone proves herniation, and intolerance is not evidence of malingering. [Stanford bedside back examination](https://med.stanford.edu/stanfordmedicine25/the25/BackExam.html)

| Root emphasis | Useful motor comparison | Reflex clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L4 | Knee extension | Patellar response, shared with adjacent lumbar roots |
| L5 | Great-toe extension and ankle dorsiflexion | No consistently useful routine knee/ankle reflex; use the broader examination |
| S1 | Plantarflexion, toe walking or heel raises | Achilles response |

Root territories overlap and examination findings vary. Sensory testing should map the actual deficit rather than force every patient into a perfect dermatome. A common paracentral L4-L5 disc herniation affects the traversing **L5** root, while an L5-S1 lesion commonly affects **S1**; foraminal/far-lateral lesions can affect the exiting root instead. Correlate side, level, and objective findings before attributing symptoms to a report. Electrodiagnostic assessment is selective when root disease versus peripheral neuropathy/entrapment remains uncertain, not an obligatory first test for every acute episode.

Stable radicular symptoms without major deficit often permit conservative care and reassessment. Progressive weakness, foot drop, or sacral dysfunction requires urgent evaluation without completing a preset exercise course. Persistent disability can justify imaging and specialist discussion when results will change treatment. Surgery for concordant compression may improve limb symptoms without resolving every source of back pain.

### Distinguish neurogenic from vascular claudication

Lumbar stenosis narrows the space for nerve roots, often through combinations of disc, facet, ligament, and alignment changes. **Neurogenic claudication** commonly causes buttock/leg discomfort, heaviness, or weakness with standing and walking, improving with sitting or flexion. The patient may tolerate a flexed cycling posture better than upright walking. Back pain may be modest or absent, and a resting neurologic examination can be normal. Anatomic narrowing without a compatible clinical syndrome is not, by itself, symptomatic stenosis. [AAOS lumbar stenosis presentation](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/lumbar-spinal-stenosis/)

Vascular claudication may improve by stopping exertion without spinal flexion. Patterns overlap: examine pulses, skin, feet, and vascular risk and test when indicated. PAD can coexist with diabetic neuropathy, hip OA, or stenosis. Neither a shopping-cart history nor palpable pulses establishes the sole diagnosis.

**Clinical Case:** A 73-year-old has thigh aching with standing relieved by sitting, plus reproducible calf pain on level walking and reduced pedal pulses. Evaluate both spinal stenosis and PAD; targeting only an MRI abnormality may miss vascular disability and cardiovascular risk.

For uncomplicated stenosis, begin an individualized activity/rehabilitation plan and suitable symptom treatment, monitoring walking tolerance and neurologic function. Persistent important limitation can justify a surgical opinion after clinical-imaging correlation. Decompression addresses neural crowding; fusion is a separate decision involving instability/alignment and other factors. New cauda equina symptoms or progressive deficits are not an elective stenosis follow-up problem.

### Imaging should answer a decision, not mark the passage of time

ACR does not recommend routine imaging for uncomplicated initial low back pain, including many stable radicular presentations. When a surgery/intervention candidate has persistent or progressive symptoms despite about **six weeks of appropriate treatment**, lumbar MRI without contrast is usually appropriate. This is a specific indication, not a mandate to scan everyone on day 42 or wait six weeks with a deficit. Contrast is selected for the clinical question, particularly infection, tumor, or certain postoperative distinctions; it is not required for every disc assessment. [ACR low-back-pain imaging variants](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69483/Narrative/)

Explain that common age-related imaging changes may be incidental; interpret them against the clinical syndrome. Reconsider deferred imaging when symptoms or examination change. Document the indication or reason for deferral, review interval, and earlier-imaging triggers. Prior surgery, trauma, cancer, infection, or fragility may require a different imaging pathway.

**Clinical Case:** A 34-year-old with nine days of localized low back pain remains ambulatory without systemic or neurologic symptoms and has a normal focused examination. Despite fear prompted by a relative's spine surgery, immediate MRI is unnecessary. Begin activity/symptom care with reassessment and return precautions, not a guarantee against future serious disease.

### Neck pain requires a root-versus-cord distinction

Cervical radiculopathy may cause neck/scapular pain radiating into an arm, with sensory symptoms, focal weakness, or a reduced reflex. Examine shoulder movement and peripheral nerve distributions as well as the cervical region; carpal tunnel, ulnar entrapment, shoulder disease, and root disease can overlap. Reproduction or relief with selected neck/arm positions can support the assessment but does not independently establish the level. Many stable cases improve with nonsurgical treatment, while progressive weakness or persistent disability requires reassessment. [AAOS cervical radiculopathy](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/cervical-radiculopathy-pinched-nerve/)

For acute atraumatic neck pain without warning features, an MRI is not a routine first step. ACR's **2024** cervical pathway distinguishes this from chronic radiculopathy, for which noncontrast MRI is usually appropriate when imaging is needed. Infection, malignancy, prior surgery, and suspected vascular disease require separate study selection. Plain films can show alignment or degenerative changes but do not exclude cord disease. Avoid interpreting a common osteophyte as proof of the source of every hand symptom. [ACR cervical pain/radiculopathy imaging](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69426/Narrative/)

**Degenerative cervical myelopathy** affects the spinal cord, not just an exiting root. Ask about dropping objects, handwriting/button changes, hand numbness, imbalance, falls, leg stiffness, and urinary change. Examine gait, fine hand function, tone, reflexes, and plantar responses. Hyperreflexia, clonus, or pathologic reflexes support cord involvement, but the absence of one sign does not exclude it. Root-related reduced reflexes can coexist with cord-related findings elsewhere. Do not let minimal neck pain or a coexisting peripheral neuropathy obscure a progressive functional history. [AAOS cervical cord-compression recognition](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/cervical-spondylotic-myelopathy-spinal-cord-compression/)

Suspected clinical myelopathy requires MRI and timely spinal assessment; rapid decline or major deficits require emergency evaluation. AO Spine's **September 2026** recommendations emphasize that mild disease needs clinical and functional assessment, not prediction from MRI compression alone. Selected stable mild cases may undergo structured follow-up; progression, meaningful disability, and patient priorities can support surgery. A mild composite score does not mean that observation without a review plan is harmless. Discuss that decompression may prevent further injury yet leave some sensory deficits. [AO Spine mild-myelopathy recommendations, 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/21925682261492222)

**Clinical Case:** A 62-year-old cabinetmaker has worsening dexterity and trips on uneven ground despite little neck pain and prior carpal-tunnel splints. Brisk leg reflexes and gait change require prompt evaluation for cervical myelopathy, not more splinting or attribution to age and overuse.

Sudden unusual neck pain/headache with focal neurologic symptoms, partial Horner features, severe imbalance, diplopia, or other posterior-circulation symptoms raises concern for **cervical artery dissection or stroke**. Minor trauma may precede dissection, but it can also occur without major injury. Arrange emergency vascular/neurologic evaluation with the appropriate CTA/MRA pathway rather than a routine spine film or manipulation. Fever, meningism, severe systemic illness, or significant trauma likewise requires its own urgent evaluation. [AHA cervical artery-dissection statement](https://professional.heart.org/en/guidelines-statements/treatment-and-outcomes-of-cervical-artery-dissection-in-adults-a-scientificstr0000000000000457)

### Recognize inflammatory axial disease rather than repeatedly labeling strain

Consider axial spondyloarthritis with persistent back symptoms beginning in younger adulthood, night waking, improvement with movement, buttock pain, psoriasis, enthesitis, uveitis, inflammatory bowel disease, or a relevant family history. HLA-B27 or CRP alone cannot establish or exclude it. The NICE referral pathway considers onset **before age 45** and duration **over three months**, then counts additional features: onset before 35, second-half-night waking, buttock pain, movement benefit, prompt NSAID benefit, first-degree family history, arthritis, enthesitis, and psoriasis. Four or more supports referral; with exactly three, a positive HLA-B27 supports referral. Persistent clinical concern still warrants reassessment even outside the rule. Sacroiliac radiographs and an appropriate inflammatory-protocol MRI have distinct roles, and normal plain films do not exclude nonradiographic disease. [NICE axial recognition and diagnosis](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng65/chapter/recommendations)

The **ACR 2026 summary** favors an AP pelvis radiograph for initial adult axial assessment, followed by **sacroiliac MRI without contrast** when radiographs are nondiagnostic; lumbar MRI is not a substitute for that question. In children/adolescents, sacroiliac MRI without contrast is preferred initially. After diagnosis, physical therapy and appropriate NSAIDs are initial options. Persistent active disease may need targeted therapy: TNF or IL-17 inhibitors are preferred initial biologics over JAK inhibitors; monoclonal TNF inhibitors are favored with uveitis or IBD, whereas psoriasis can favor IL-17 inhibition. Conventional RA medicines do not treat purely axial disease interchangeably. These are rheumatology-directed choices, not reasons to repeat empirical steroids while delaying referral. [ACR 2026 approved axial-spondyloarthritis summary](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltee37abb6b278ab2c/blt424f1e71604af245/axspa-guideline-summary-2026.pdf)

### Build an active treatment plan that fits the diagnosis

For uncomplicated pain, explain the working diagnosis without promising a fixed recovery date. Encourage tolerable movement, position changes, and gradual return to tasks rather than bed rest. Tailor exercise to capability and symptoms; new deficits or fracture concern require reassessment before progression.

Superficial heat can briefly relieve acute nonradicular pain when sensation and burn risk permit. Massage/manual treatment may be adjuncts; avoid manipulation with suspected fracture, infection, instability, vascular disease, or cord compromise. Discuss modest expected benefits, cost, access, and preferences. Temporary relief neither proves malalignment nor justifies indefinite passive treatment. [ACP initial nonpharmacologic treatment](https://www.acponline.org/acp-newsroom/american-college-of-physicians-issues-guideline-for-treating-nonradicular-low-back-pain)

For **chronic primary low back pain**, WHO supports education, structured exercise, and appropriate psychological strategies such as CBT to improve participation and coping. Address sleep, mood, fear, and access barriers. Routine traction, lumbar belts, and opioids are not foundational treatment; fracture support or pregnancy adaptations are separate indications. [WHO chronic primary low-back-pain guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240081789)

### Medicines offer limited benefits and patient-specific harms

Under the VA/DoD pathway, NSAIDs can be considered when safe, and **duloxetine** is an option for chronic low back pain. The pathway suggests against acetaminophen as a low-back-pain treatment, chronic muscle relaxants, chronic opioids, and systemic steroids for low back pain with or without radicular symptoms; it recommends against benzodiazepines. Evidence is insufficient for a routine short acute course of a nonbenzodiazepine muscle relaxant. This is not an instruction to substitute one sedating drug for another whenever an NSAID is unsuitable. Set a treatment goal and stop or reconsider drugs that do not improve meaningful function. [VA/DoD treatment recommendations](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/Pain/lbp/VADoDLBPCPGFinal508.pdf)

For **sciatica**, NICE advises against gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids and benzodiazepines because overall benefit has not been demonstrated and harms occur. Neuropathic-sounding pain is not sufficient reason to transfer a drug's benefit in diabetic neuropathy to compressed lumbar roots. NSAID benefit is limited and must be weighed against GI, hepatic and cardiorenal risks. Patients already taking dependence-forming medicines need individualized review and a withdrawal plan rather than abrupt discontinuation. [NICE sciatica medication decisions](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendations)

| Treatment consideration | What to check before use | Follow-through |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral NSAID | Kidney function, volume/BP/HF, ulcer/bleeding history, anticoagulants and pregnancy | Shortest effective exposure; review adverse effects and avoid duplicate OTC NSAIDs |
| Duloxetine for chronic pain | Renal/hepatic function, serotonergic drugs, BP, falls and mood risk | Assess tolerance/function; daily treatment is not an as-needed rescue; plan tapering |
| A selected muscle-relaxant preparation | Sedation, driving, falls, anticholinergic and interaction risks | A time-limited trial is not a justification for chronic renewal |
| Existing opioid/benzodiazepine regimen | Benefit, sedation, combinations, dependence and overdose risk | Coordinate review and a patient-specific change plan; avoid abrupt forced withdrawal |

NSAIDs can worsen renal perfusion, edema, BP, heart failure, and bleeding. Reconcile ibuprofen, naproxen, combination products, aspirin, anticoagulants, and renal/volume-active drugs. Gastroprotection does not remove renal or cardiovascular harm. Assess CKD, dehydration, and congestion before prescribing. [NIDDK medicine-related kidney safety](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/keeping-kidneys-safe)

Duloxetine is not an immediate rescue drug. For adult chronic musculoskeletal pain, the delayed-release capsule product label starts **30 mg orally daily for one week, then 60 mg daily**; higher doses do not add established benefit for this indication and cause more adverse effects. Avoid use with **GFR below 30 mL/minute** or chronic liver disease/cirrhosis. Review serotonergic combinations, BP, orthostasis/falls, discontinuation symptoms, and the antidepressant warning for younger patients. Reassess tolerance and function and taper rather than abruptly stopping. This adult pain regimen is not a pediatric sciatica prescription. [Duloxetine prescribing information](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5fa0eda4-0886-415c-a30c-ae9729f16974)

Muscle relaxants differ in their pharmacology and risks. Cyclobenzaprine has sedating, anticholinergic and serotonergic effects; its extended-release label limits use to **two to three weeks** and does not recommend that preparation in older adults. This is a product limitation, not evidence that every patient should receive a three-week course. Nighttime dosing does not guarantee safe driving the following morning. Combining sedatives with alcohol or an opioid can worsen impairment; a medicine that prevents safe work may undermine the rehabilitation goal. [Cyclobenzaprine ER product precautions](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=8a8c9479-b3ab-453a-bd8a-deaf38000742&type=display)

### Procedures require a specific target and realistic expectations

The AAN **2025 systematic review** finds that epidural steroids probably provide limited short-term pain/disability benefit in radiculopathy, with insufficient evidence for long-term pain relief. Lumbar stenosis findings differ: disability may improve in some patients, but short-term pain benefit was not demonstrated. Cervical evidence is more limited. These are evidence estimates, not a mandate to inject every patient with an abnormal scan. NICE distinguishes acute severe sciatica, where an epidural may be considered, from neurogenic claudication due to central stenosis, where it advises against epidurals. Discuss the specific indication rather than promising a general spine-pain cure. [AAN clinician evidence summary](https://aanfiles.blob.core.windows.net/guidelines/e5b9aa53-c4e9-48a5-b43a-3a33d66a6b03/25_EpiduralInjectnReview-Clinician_v04.pdf)

Injection planning requires infection, bleeding/anticoagulant, diabetes, technical and neurologic risk assessment. FDA warns of rare serious neurologic injuries after epidural corticosteroid administration, including stroke, paralysis and visual loss. Image guidance does not eliminate every risk. A procedure must not delay evaluation of a progressive deficit or emergency compression. Radiofrequency procedures, surgery, and implants address selected clinical problems; they are not interchangeable next steps for all persistent pain. [FDA epidural corticosteroid safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/files/drugs/published/FDA-Drug-Safety-Communication-for-Corticosteroid.pdf)

### Persistent pain and work limitations need a planned review

For persistent symptoms, reassess diagnosis, trajectory, neurologic findings, treatment exposure, and function. Distinguish new disease from load intolerance, poor sleep, mood symptoms, and fear of reinjury. Check access before labeling therapy unsuccessful. Track a meaningful functional goal, supported when useful by a validated disability measure.

NIOSH supports return-to-work programs with modified tasks, restricted duties or temporary transfers for musculoskeletal disorders. Specify current capabilities and temporary restrictions, their duration, and the reassessment date. Repeated lifting, awkward loads, vibration, prolonged fixed postures, shift schedules, and lack of recovery time may need adjustment. Return to activity need not await zero pain, but it must respect neurologic safety, healing restrictions, and medication impairment. Occupational coordination requires the patient's consent and should disclose only the information needed for safe accommodation. [NIOSH worker recovery and modified duty](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ergonomics/ergo-programs/promote-recovery.html)

**Clinical Case:** A 51-year-old bus driver improves walking from 10 to 18 minutes but becomes sedated after a new night medicine. The **eight-minute gain**, **80% relative to baseline**, is neither an 80% cure nor driving clearance. Review medication and job demands while preserving activity gains and arranging graded duties or rehabilitation.

### Adapt the plan across ages and pregnancy

Back pain in a child should not automatically receive an adult nonspecific-pain label. Night pain, systemic symptoms, an abnormal gait, neurologic deficit, persistent focal pain, or bowel/bladder change requires investigation. Consider infection, malignancy, inflammatory disease, and stress injury such as spondylolysis in an adolescent with repetitive extension loading. Imaging is indication-specific, with attention to radiation and the question being asked. Growth-related and sports presentations are expanded in 07C-07D. [ACR pediatric back-pain criteria, revised 2024](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/3099011/Narrative/)

Pregnancy-related back or pelvic pain may respond to activity adaptation, appropriate exercise and obstetric/physical-therapy support. Severe pain, fever, urinary symptoms, contractions, bleeding, or neurologic symptoms requires a broader obstetric or medical assessment. Do not explain every new pain as the enlarging uterus. Avoid NSAIDs from **20 weeks** unless necessary under clinician supervision, and at **30 weeks or later** because of additional ductal risk. The 20-30-week duration/monitoring precautions and prescribed low-dose aspirin exception are summarized in 07A. [ACOG pregnancy back-pain care](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/back-pain-during-pregnancy), [FDA pregnancy NSAID safety](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic)

In older adults, assess frailty, falls, bone health, baseline neurologic function, polypharmacy, and home support. Immune suppression can mute infection signs. New severe spinal pain with deficits after a procedure or during anticoagulation may indicate bleeding. Address practical barriers and specify urgent return for urinary/perineal change, weakness, gait/hand deterioration, systemic illness, or vascular symptoms.
