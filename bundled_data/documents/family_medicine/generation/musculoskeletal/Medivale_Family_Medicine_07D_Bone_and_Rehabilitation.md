# Medivale Family Medicine

## 07D. Bone and Rehabilitation

Fracture prevention combines bone treatment with safer movement. Better BMD does not correct falls, orthostasis, or weakness, and a reassuring density report does not negate a fragility fracture. Primary care coordinates secondary-cause assessment, medication sequencing, and rehabilitation.

Screening is introduced in 01C; calcium/PTH physiology and kidney mineral-bone disease are in 04E and 06C-06D. Regional injuries and sport-specific recovery are in 07C. Adult fracture prevention below follows BHOF treatment thresholds and ISCD densitometry positions; pediatric bone disease requires separate interpretation.

### Recognize fragility rather than waiting for the first dramatic fracture

**Osteoporosis** reduces bone strength through loss of quantity and structure. Aging, estrogen deficiency, glucocorticoids, and other disorders disrupt coupled osteoclast resorption and osteoblast formation. Disease is often painless until fracture; height loss, kyphosis, reduced rib-to-pelvis space, or focal back pain may reveal vertebral injury. Men and women are affected regardless of appearance or ancestry. [NIAMS osteoporosis mechanisms and clinical presentation](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/osteoporosis)

A **fragility fracture** follows force unlikely to break healthy bone, commonly a standing-height fall or less. Establish mechanism, site, prior fractures, focal lesions, and metabolic disease. Hip fracture, unexplained vertebral collapse, and a lytic lesion need different immediate evaluations despite possible low BMD. Higher-energy trauma does not exclude underlying fragility.

Treat the acute injury promptly. Inability to bear weight after a fall warrants occult hip-fracture assessment despite normal initial films. Vertebral pain with weakness, bladder change, cancer, or systemic illness follows 07B's urgent pathways. Assign responsibility for bone assessment, treatment, and recovery; fracture liaison services help coordinate hospital-to-clinic care. [BHOF current clinical decision-support resources](https://www.bonesource.org/bhofmobileapps)

### Screening, diagnostic evaluation, and treatment are different decisions

The **2025 USPSTF screening recommendation** applies to adults without known osteoporosis or a fragility-fracture history and excludes important secondary-osteoporosis populations. Screen women aged **65 years or older** with DXA. In younger postmenopausal women, first identify risk factors and then use clinical risk assessment to decide whether DXA is appropriate. The USPSTF finds evidence insufficient for routine screening in men; that is not a recommendation against evaluating a symptomatic man, a fracture, or medication-related bone loss. [USPSTF osteoporosis screening recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/osteoporosis-screening)

ISCD testing indications beyond routine screening include men aged **70 years or older**, younger at-risk adults, fragility fractures, and bone-harming medicines. Keep screening, country-specific probability models, and treatment thresholds distinct. Test when results will affect care, recognizing treatment may already be indicated by fracture history. [ISCD adult testing and interpretation positions](https://iscd.org/official-positions-2023/)

**Central DXA** measures areal BMD, usually at lumbar spine and proximal femur. T-scores compare with young adults; Z-scores use age-appropriate references. Use valid sites and assess artifacts from degeneration, hardware, compression, and positioning, especially in the spine. The one-third radius has defined uses, including uninterpretable usual sites and hyperparathyroidism.

For adult diagnosis, accepted central sites are the PA lumbar spine, femoral neck and total hip; use the lower valid femoral-neck or total-hip T-score, not Ward's area. Exclude artifactual vertebrae rather than averaging them into a reassuring result; one evaluable vertebra alone is insufficient for spine-based diagnosis. Consider lateral spine imaging or DXA vertebral fracture assessment when the T-score is below -1.0 plus an ISCD indication: women at least 70, men at least 80, historical height loss over 4 cm, undocumented prior vertebral fracture, or prednisone-equivalent exposure at least 5 mg/day for at least three months. Symptomatic suspected fracture still needs diagnostic imaging regardless of those screening indications. [ISCD diagnostic sites and vertebral assessment](https://iscd.org/official-positions-2023/)

| Result or setting | Interpretation | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T-score at least -1.0 | Normal densitometric category | No possibility of future fracture |
| T-score below -1.0 but above -2.5 | Low bone mass, often called osteopenia | Automatic need for medication or absence of high clinical risk |
| T-score -2.5 or lower at an accepted site | Densitometric osteoporosis in an applicable adult population | The cause, medication choice, or absence of other bone disease |
| Premenopausal adult or man younger than 50 | Prefer Z-score; -2.0 or lower is below expected for age | An adult T-score-only osteoporosis diagnosis |

These categories primarily apply to postmenopausal women and men aged at least 50. Interpret BMD with fracture history, endocrine status, medicines, and scan quality. Improvement above a diagnostic T-score threshold during treatment does not erase established osteoporosis. [NIAMS diagnosis and treatment assessment](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/osteoporosis/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

In children, densitometry alone does not establish osteoporosis. One or more vertebral compression fractures without high-energy trauma or local disease indicates osteoporosis regardless of BMD. Without vertebral fractures, the ISCD definition requires **Z-score -2.0 or lower** plus a clinically significant history: **at least two long-bone fractures by age 10, or at least three by age 19**. A higher Z-score does not exclude skeletal fragility. Bone size and growth affect interpretation. Recurrent low-trauma fractures, unusual deformity, delayed growth, or chronic disease warrants pediatric bone expertise rather than automatic adult bisphosphonate treatment. [ISCD pediatric skeletal assessment](https://iscd.org/learn/official-positions/pediatric-positions/)

### Combine fracture history with probability, not probability instead of history

For **postmenopausal women and men aged 50 or older**, BHOF's principal pharmacologic treatment indications include a hip or vertebral fracture regardless of BMD, a qualifying T-score of **-2.5 or lower**, or low hip bone mass with U.S.-adapted FRAX probability of **at least 3% for hip fracture or at least 20% for major osteoporotic fracture over ten years**. These are alternative qualifying pathways, not requirements that all must be present. Other fractures and intermediate situations require individual assessment. Explain likely benefit, harms, burden, and preferences rather than treating a cutoff as a mandate. [BHOF adult treatment thresholds](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9546973/)

FRAX estimates hip and major osteoporotic fracture probability from clinical factors, with optional femoral-neck BMD. Major osteoporotic fracture includes hip, clinical vertebral, proximal humeral, and forearm fractures, not every possible fracture. Use the appropriate country model and actual femoral-neck data; lumbar-spine density is not a substitute input. Recurrent or recent fractures, glucocorticoid dose, falls, diabetes, and spine/hip discordance can make the basic estimate incomplete. FRAXplus supplies additional adjustments, but they should be used deliberately, not added together ad hoc. A low estimate does not overrule a qualifying hip or vertebral fracture, and changes in FRAX are not a treatment-response target. [FRAX developer's interpretation and limitations](https://www.fraxplus.org/faq)

**Clinical Case:** A 74-year-old man with height loss has an unrecognized low-trauma vertebral fracture and hip T-score -1.8. The fracture supports treatment without waiting for -2.5, another fracture, or a higher FRAX estimate. Evaluate cause and medicine safety, then offer fracture prevention.

### Look for secondary causes before calling bone loss inevitable aging

Assess weight/dietary change, diarrhea or bariatric surgery, menstrual/hypogonadal symptoms, thyroid and kidney/liver disease, inflammation, immobility, smoking, alcohol, and family history. Review glucocorticoids, thyroid replacement, antiseizure medicines, aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation, and other contributors. Skeletal risk varies with dose, duration, indication, and vulnerability; inhaled and systemic exposures are not equivalent.

Before antifracture treatment, evaluate secondary causes. BHOF's baseline studies include CBC, albumin-adjusted calcium, renal function, phosphate, magnesium, liver tests, **25-hydroxyvitamin D**, PTH and 24-hour urine calcium/creatinine; total testosterone and gonadotropins are included for men aged 50-69. Alkaline phosphatase helps evaluate mineralization and turnover. Select thyroid tests, celiac serology with IgA assessment, or monoclonal-protein studies from the presentation. Interpret urine calcium with intake, renal function and collection adequacy. Normal serum calcium does not establish adequate intake, and low BMD does not identify the cause. [BHOF secondary-cause assessment, Table 3](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00198-021-05900-y.pdf)

**Osteomalacia** is defective mineralization, not merely another word for low bone mass. Diffuse bone pain, proximal weakness, low phosphate, elevated alkaline phosphatase, and relevant deficiency or phosphate-wasting history should prompt investigation. Calcium may be preserved by compensatory PTH activity. Treat the cause and mineral deficit; simply suppressing resorption can worsen an untreated mineral disorder. Conversely, persistently low alkaline phosphatase with suggestive fractures raises a different concern, including hypophosphatasia. Anemia, hypercalcemia, renal dysfunction, or focal destructive lesions should raise concern for myeloma or another nonroutine explanation. [NIH vitamin D and mineralization](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/)

**Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis** can develop early and at a higher BMD than ordinary postmenopausal disease. ACR recommends fracture-risk assessment for adults starting or continuing prednisone-equivalent treatment of **at least 2.5 mg/day for more than three months**. Its risk categories and glucocorticoid-adjusted FRAX pathway are distinct from the general BHOF thresholds; FRAX is not validated below age 40. Assess fractures, BMD and vertebral imaging as indicated, and use risk-appropriate treatment rather than waiting for a screening birthday. Minimize steroid exposure when the underlying disease permits, but do not abruptly stop necessary treatment. [ACR glucocorticoid guideline summary](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltee37abb6b278ab2c/blt8c68fa62e5f70069/giop-guideline-summary-2022.pdf)

In advanced CKD, low BMD can coexist with high- or low-turnover renal bone disease. Calcium, phosphate, PTH trends, vitamin D metabolism, renal function, and specialist assessment guide treatment; lack of renal clearance does not establish drug safety. Pregnancy/lactation-associated fractures and premenopausal disease also need cause-specific, reproductive-aware care. [NIDDK kidney mineral-bone disorder](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/mineral-bone-disorder)

**Clinical Case:** A 48-year-old after gastric bypass develops proximal weakness, foot pain, low phosphate, and elevated alkaline phosphatase. Assess mineralization, malabsorption, and possible stress fracture before prescribing an antiresorptive; low BMD alone does not identify the mechanism.

### Use nutrition and activity as foundations, not replacement treatment

Count calcium from food and supplements. NIH allowances are **1000 mg/day for men aged 51-70, 1200 mg/day for women aged 51-70, and 1200 mg/day for adults older than 70**; younger requirements differ. Prefer food and supplement only the shortfall. Assess elemental calcium, absorption, constipation, stones, and interactions such as levothyroxine. Large supplemental doses absorb less efficiently; formulation and meals matter. [NIH calcium intake and supplement principles](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/)

For diagnosed osteoporosis, ensure vitamin D adequacy, particularly before potent antiresorptives. In its adult bone-disease pathway, BHOF uses **800-1000 IU/day as needed** in adults 50 or older; deficiency, malabsorption and advanced CKD may require different treatment. This is not a universal high-dose or serum-target prescription for healthy adults. Calcium and vitamin D support treatment but do not replace indicated antifracture medication. Adequate protein/energy intake, smoking cessation, moderation of alcohol, and progressive weight-bearing/resistance activity also matter. A patient unable to tolerate dairy can meet needs through appropriate alternatives; dietary preference is not itself a diagnosis of deficiency. [BHOF nutrition in bone-disease care](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00198-021-05900-y.pdf)

Tailor strength, balance, posture, and weight-bearing exercise to capacity and fracture history. After vertebral fracture, physiotherapy can teach safer bending, lifting, and spinal loading. Avoid high-impact or forceful loaded flexion in unassessed high-risk patients without imposing permanent inactivity. Walking does not replace balance/resistance work. Reassess new pain, deficits, or suspected fracture before progression. [IOF exercise and fracture-specific precautions](https://www.osteoporosis.foundation/patients/prevention/exercise)

### Select a medicine with fracture-site evidence and a feasible administration plan

Bisphosphonates are a common initial choice. Recent/multiple vertebral fractures, very low BMD, or other very-high-risk features can justify early bone-forming therapy discussion. Select by approved population, fracture-site evidence, renal/calcium status, swallowing, cardiovascular and reproductive risks, cost, and ability to maintain dosing.

| Treatment approach | Main role | A decision-changing caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral alendronate or risedronate | Common initial antiresorptive options | Esophageal/administration limitations and product-specific renal restrictions |
| IV zoledronic acid | Infrequent dosing when oral treatment is unsuitable | Acute renal impairment, low calcium, hydration and infusion safeguards |
| Denosumab | RANKL inhibition for selected high-risk patients | Advanced-CKD hypocalcemia; no unplanned delay or discontinuation |
| Teriparatide or abaloparatide | Bone-forming therapy in selected very-high-risk patients | Skeletal malignancy/radiation and calcium-related precautions; subsequent antiresorptive plan |
| Romosozumab | Time-limited sclerostin inhibition in eligible postmenopausal patients | Cardiovascular warning and need for follow-on treatment |
| Raloxifene | Selected postmenopausal vertebral-risk situations | VTE risk; not a general hip-fracture-prevention substitute |

**Alendronate** binds bone mineral and reduces osteoclast activity. One labeled adult osteoporosis treatment regimen is **70 mg orally once weekly**. Swallow the tablet with **6-8 fluid ounces of plain water** on arising, at least **30 minutes before** food, other drinks, or medicines; stay upright for at least 30 minutes and until after eating. Correct hypocalcemia first. Esophageal emptying disorders and inability to remain upright are contraindications; it is not recommended at creatinine clearance **below 35 mL/min**. A prevention regimen is not automatically the same as a treatment regimen. Stop and assess new dysphagia, painful swallowing, or new/worsening heartburn rather than continuing unchanged. [FDA alendronate prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/020560s073lbl.pdf)

**Zoledronic acid** offers an IV alternative, but avoiding the esophagus does not remove systemic safety checks. The osteoporosis treatment regimen is **5 mg IV once yearly**, infused over **at least 15 minutes**. Before each dose, check creatinine and calculate **Cockcroft-Gault CrCl using actual body weight**, correct hypocalcemia, and assess hydration. Creatinine clearance **below 35 mL/min** or acute renal impairment is a contraindication. Counsel about an acute postinfusion flu-like reaction and distinguish osteoporosis dosing from oncology regimens. Do not infuse during dehydration or assume a smaller dose removes the contraindication. [Current zoledronic acid label](https://www.novartis.com/us-en/sites/novartis_us/files/reclast.pdf)

Bisphosphonate fracture-site evidence differs: **ibandronate is not recommended to reduce hip or nonvertebral fracture risk** in the Endocrine Society pathway. Review dental health/procedures and discuss uncommon jaw osteonecrosis and atypical femoral fractures in proportion to untreated risk. New thigh/groin pain warrants prompt assessment. [Endocrine Society drug-selection guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/osteoporosis-in-postmenopausal-women)

**Clinical Case:** A 79-year-old with osteoporosis and achalasia cannot use an oral bisphosphonate safely. CrCl 28 mL/min also excludes routine zoledronic acid. Assess renal bone disease and obtain an individualized plan rather than switching automatically to denosumab without calcium/CKD evaluation.

### Denosumab requires continuity; bone-forming therapy requires sequencing

Denosumab inhibits RANKL and reduces osteoclast formation and activity. The osteoporosis-dose product is **60 mg subcutaneously every six months**; it is not interchangeable with oncology schedules. Correct hypocalcemia and ensure appropriate calcium/vitamin D support. The current label warns of severe hypocalcemia in advanced CKD, especially with CKD-mineral bone disorder; expert supervision, pretreatment investigation, and additional monitoring are necessary. Pregnancy is a contraindication. Reconcile other denosumab products so the patient does not receive duplicate active therapy. [Current denosumab prescribing information](https://www.pi.amgen.com/united_states/prolia/prolia_pi.pdf), [FDA advanced-CKD boxed warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-adds-boxed-warning-increased-risk-severe-hypocalcemia-patients-advanced-chronic-kidney-disease)

In **eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2**, including dialysis, the Prolia label requires CKD-MBD assessment using intact PTH, calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D before treatment decisions. Under CKD-MBD expertise, monitor calcium **weekly for the first month and monthly thereafter**, with an individualized calcium/activated-vitamin-D plan. Muscle spasms, tingling, confusion, seizures, or rhythm symptoms warrant urgent assessment. If a dose is missed, give it as soon as feasible and schedule subsequent doses six months from the actual injection date.

Denosumab lacks the persistent skeletal reservoir of bisphosphonates: **do not give it a routine drug holiday**. Delays or cessation can cause rapid bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures. Arrange subsequent antiresorptive treatment before stopping, using an individualized agent/timing protocol, especially with renal impairment. Anticipate travel, coverage gaps, and missed visits; improved BMD does not make interruption safe. [Endocrine Society denosumab continuity recommendation](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/osteoporosis-in-postmenopausal-women)

Teriparatide and abaloparatide stimulate bone formation through intermittent PTH-receptor signaling and are given as daily subcutaneous injections. They are options for selected patients at very high risk, not routine treatment for every low-density scan. Assess calcium disorders, stone history, orthostatic symptoms, skeletal malignancy, previous skeletal radiation, Paget disease, and reproductive considerations. Follow treatment with an antiresorptive to preserve gains. For **teriparatide specifically**, use beyond two lifetime years is considered only when the patient remains or again becomes high risk; do not turn this into an absolute prohibition or extrapolate it to every related product. [Current teriparatide safety and duration labeling](https://pi.lilly.com/us/forteo-pi.pdf)

For abaloparatide, use beyond two lifetime years is not recommended. Assess hypersensitivity, hypercalcemia/hypercalciuria, orthostasis, and osteosarcoma risk. Follow its own product-specific plan rather than extrapolating teriparatide instructions. [August 2026 abaloparatide labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=712143d9-e21e-4013-bb3b-3426a21060a8)

Romosozumab inhibits sclerostin, increasing formation and reducing resorption. Its labeled course is limited to **12 monthly doses**, with follow-on antiresorptive treatment if osteoporosis therapy remains warranted. Correct hypocalcemia and assess cardiovascular risk. The U.S. label says **not to initiate after myocardial infarction or stroke within the preceding year** and to discontinue if one occurs during treatment; additional cardiovascular risk still matters outside that year. This drug is not a workaround for an unexplained mineral disorder or an unreviewed cardiovascular history. [Current romosozumab boxed warning and course limit](https://www.pi.amgen.com/united_states/evenity/evenity_pi_hcp_english.pdf)

Raloxifene has a selective role in postmenopausal vertebral-fracture prevention, with potential breast-cancer-risk considerations. Avoid it with active or previous venous thromboembolism; consider stroke risk and hot flashes. Stop it **at least 72 hours before and during prolonged immobilization**, restarting only when fully ambulatory. It is not equivalent to broad hip-fracture protection. Menopausal hormone therapy requires its own symptom, uterine, thrombotic, and cancer assessment; it is not the default osteoporosis prescription for an older adult. Calcitonin is not a preferred substitute for more effective long-term therapy. [Current raloxifene safety labeling](https://pi.lilly.com/us/evista-pi.pdf)

**Clinical Case:** A patient receiving denosumab wants to skip treatment during ten months abroad because BMD improved. Arrange continued access or a planned transition before departure, with injection dates and prescriber coordination. She should contact the treating team promptly if a dose will be delayed.

### Monitor for fractures, meaningful change, and treatment burden

Review new fractures, height loss, pain, falls, adverse effects, actual dosing, supplements, and access. Fracture prevention, not BMD alone, is the goal. A fracture during treatment warrants reassessment of adherence, exposure duration, secondary causes, and risk rather than automatic declaration of failure. Ask about swallowing, cost, cognition, and schedule feasibility.

Repeat DXA when the result is likely to change care, using comparable technique and preferably the same calibrated system. BHOF suggests a first treatment-monitoring scan **one to two years after initiation or a treatment change**, then intervals adapted to risk and treatment. Compare absolute BMD with the facility's **least significant change**, not just decimal differences in T-score. Smaller changes may be measurement noise. New fractures or risks justify reassessment, but another scan must not delay secondary fracture prevention. [BHOF monitoring](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00198-021-05900-y.pdf), [ISCD serial-DXA and precision positions](https://iscd.org/official-positions-2023/)

**Example:** Total-hip BMD changes from 0.740 to 0.726 g/cm2 on comparable scans. The decrease is 0.014/0.740 x 100 = **1.9%**, rounded. If that facility's valid least significant change is 3.0%, the difference is not established as a statistically significant loss. This does not prove treatment success or remove the need to investigate a new fracture; it prevents mistaking measurement variation for certain deterioration.

After several years of bisphosphonate treatment, reassess whether risk remains high. The Endocrine Society advises reassessment after **three to five years**, with a possible monitored holiday for lower-risk patients and continued or alternative therapy for those remaining high risk. The decision depends on the agent, treatment exposure, fractures, BMD, age, and ongoing risks; it is not automatic cessation at year five. New fracture or meaningful bone loss can change a holiday plan. Do not transfer this strategy to denosumab or to the end of an anabolic course.

### Investigate falls and restore the function the patient values

Ask directly about falls in the past year, unsteadiness, and fear of falling. Reconstruct the event: activity, footwear, lighting, surface, prodrome, loss of consciousness, head impact, injury, and ability to get up. An apparent trip may coexist with poor balance or orthostasis; unexplained recurrent falls can reflect syncope, arrhythmia, neurologic disease, visual impairment, hypoglycemia, or medication effects. Acute illness, focal deficits, concerning head injury, and cardiac symptoms need their own urgent pathways, not a routine exercise referral.

Use **CDC STEADI** to assess gait, strength, balance, vision, feet, aids, orthostasis, and environmental risk. Include hearing, nocturia, cognition, nutrition, and available help. The 2025 AGS recommendations emphasize team-based action: refer clear mobility deficits without waiting to complete every test, and stage assessment across visits when useful. [CDC current STEADI resources](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/hcp/clinical-resources/), [AGS primary-care implementation recommendations](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/262129)

The **Timed Up and Go** assesses rising, walking three meters, turning, returning, and sitting. Use regular footwear and the usual walking aid, guarding for safety; record both time and observed movement. CDC identifies **12 seconds or longer** as a fall-risk signal, not a diagnosis or a guarantee that a faster patient is safe. A chair-stand test and balance assessment provide complementary information. For orthostatic assessment, CDC uses five minutes supine, then standing measurements at one and three minutes; a systolic drop of **at least 20 mm Hg**, a diastolic drop of **at least 10 mm Hg**, or symptoms is abnormal. Interpret the cause and the whole clinical picture. [CDC mobility assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/media/pdfs/STEADI-Assessment-TUG-508.pdf), [CDC orthostatic assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/media/pdfs/STEADI-Assessment-MeasuringBP-508.pdf)

Review sedative-hypnotics, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, opioids, psychotropics, glucose-lowering agents, and blood-pressure medicines in context. The goal is to reduce harmful exposure while treating the underlying condition, not abruptly discontinue every medicine on a list. Assess timing, dose, interactions, hydration, and recent changes; use a gradual supervised taper when appropriate. Review aids, footwear, nighttime routes to the toilet, lighting, stairs, and reachable support. Occupational therapy can link home modifications to the actual transfers and tasks causing difficulty. [CDC medicines linked to falls](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/media/pdfs/STEADI-FactSheet-MedsLinkedtoFalls-508.pdf)

For community-dwelling adults aged at least 65 at increased fall risk, **USPSTF recommends exercise interventions** and selective individualized multifactorial care. Adapt balance, functional, and strength training with supervision, progression, and review. Walking advice, vitamins, and osteoporosis drugs are not substitutes; treat deficiency separately. These recommendations do not automatically extend to nursing-facility or acutely hospitalized populations. [USPSTF falls-prevention recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/falls-prevention-community-dwelling-older-adults-interventions)

**Clinical Case:** An 82-year-old after hip fracture becomes dizzy during transfer practice. BP falls from 148/78 supine to 122/70 standing, a **26 mm Hg systolic drop**. Address orthostasis, hydration, and medicines while arranging supervised transfers, an aid, and the surgeon's weight-bearing instructions. Avoid both bed rest and pushing through presyncope; see 15A for multifactorial falls care.

Set task-based goals such as toilet transfers, a bus-stop walk, or a step with an aid. Review progress, near falls, confidence, and home safety. Physical therapy builds movement capacity; occupational therapy adapts tasks/environment. Coordinate caregiver training, pain control, weight-bearing limits, nutrition, equipment, and follow-up so temporary protection does not become indefinite immobility.

### A child's limp requires localization before a reassuring label

Observe spontaneous gait, crawling, and play when possible. Ask about onset, trauma, fever, recent illness, night pain, morning stiffness, rash, weight loss, fatigue, developmental change, and ability to bear weight. Examine the spine, abdomen, skin, joints above/below the complaint, and both hips; hip disease can present as thigh or knee pain. Check growth, joint motion, focal bone tenderness, neurologic findings, and perfusion. Absence of pain does not make a new progressive weakness, ataxia, or loss of milestones nonurgent.

An acutely non-weight-bearing child, marked motion restriction, systemic illness, or severe focal pain needs urgent assessment. Bruising, an implausible mechanism, an injury inconsistent with development, or other safeguarding concerns requires an appropriate child-protection evaluation; fracture shape alone neither proves nor excludes inflicted injury. Persistent night/rest pain, pallor, weight loss, a mass, or unexplained blood abnormalities should prompt assessment for malignancy or systemic disease. Do not label a daytime limp as growing pains. [RCH October 2025 limping-child guideline](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Child_with_limp/)

| Pattern | Important possibilities | Next decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute painful refusal to bear weight | Infection, occult fracture, severe hip disorder | Urgent clinical assessment; do not require fever |
| Young child improving after viral illness | Transient synovitis, with serious alternatives excluded | Analgesia, short-term observation and explicit review |
| Persistent school-age hip/thigh/knee symptoms | Perthes disease, inflammatory or other pathology | Hip examination and appropriate imaging/referral |
| Adolescent knee pain with hip-motion loss | SCFE, among other causes | Stop weight bearing and seek urgent orthopedic assessment |
| Local activity-related apophyseal pain | Osgood-Schlatter or Sever disease | Confirm a fitting examination and modify load |

Testing follows the suspected cause. Targeted radiographs can identify fracture or structural disease; a normal early film does not exclude osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, stress injury, or early Perthes disease. Ultrasound detects a hip effusion but cannot reliably tell sterile inflammation from infection. CBC, inflammatory markers, cultures, MRI, or joint sampling are used when infection, inflammation, marrow disease, or an occult process is suspected. A comfortable ambulatory child without warning features may need no initial tests with a sound working diagnosis and a plan for review **within seven days of limp onset if symptoms persist**. Ongoing symptoms at seven days, inability to bear weight after analgesia, or deterioration warrants pediatric reassessment; dangerous findings require earlier care. [RCH limp investigation and review](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Child_with_limp/), [RCH bone and joint infection assessment](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/Bone_and_joint_infection/)

### Do not use a normal inflammatory marker to exclude a septic joint

Pediatric bacterial arthritis can damage a joint and cause sepsis. Fever, inability to bear weight, and painful restriction increase concern, but young children with **Kingella kingae** may have less dramatic systemic or laboratory findings. Kocher-type criteria can organize concern in a compatible hip presentation, but their original probability estimates are not universal and a low score does not safely exclude infection. Joint aspiration and source-control decisions belong to the urgent pediatric/orthopedic service, not a delayed routine office appointment.

PIDS/IDSA recommends prompt empiric antibiotics for an ill-appearing child or rapidly progressive infection, obtaining blood cultures first if feasible without delay. Do not withhold life-saving treatment while waiting for aspiration or MRI. A clinically stable child may have sampling before antibiotics under close observation within an organized receiving-service plan. Assess for adjacent osteomyelitis and tailor coverage to age, organism risk, local resistance, and cultures; neonatal disease has a separate scope. [PIDS/IDSA pediatric bacterial arthritis guideline](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/acute-bacterial-arthritis-in-pediatrics2/)

Empiric therapy must cover **S. aureus**, with MRSA activity chosen for local susceptibility and illness severity. In children **6-48 months**, include activity against **K. kingae** rather than assuming every antistaphylococcal regimen covers it. The receiving service directs drainage, drug dosing, and response-based treatment duration.

**Transient synovitis** is a short-lived hip limp, often after viral illness in a well child, diagnosed after considering serious alternatives. Use analgesia, relative rest, and gradual return as comfortable. Improvement should begin within days. Fever, worsening pain, reduced motion, or inability to bear weight requires urgent reassessment; persistent/recurrent symptoms need re-examination. [RCH transient synovitis guidance](https://www.rch.org.au/kidsinfo/fact_sheets/Transient_synovitis/)

### Distinguish Perthes disease from a slipping growth plate

**Legg-Calve-Perthes disease** is osteonecrosis of the developing femoral head, usually in younger school-age children. A gradual limp, reduced hip abduction/internal rotation, and thigh or knee pain can precede clear radiographic changes. The femoral head can collapse and remodel; outcome depends on age, extent, shape, and containment, not simply whether pain settles. Refer for pediatric orthopedic assessment, activity protection, and preservation of motion. Selected children need operative containment. Persistent clinical concern after normal radiographs needs further evaluation rather than exclusion on that film alone. [AAOS Perthes disease](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/perthes-disease/)

**Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)** is failure through the proximal femoral growth plate. It often occurs during adolescence and can present with only knee pain. Reduced internal rotation and obligatory external rotation with hip flexion are important clues. Higher body weight increases risk, but a lean child can have SCFE; unusually young age, short stature, or other atypical features raises concern for endocrine/renal contributors. A child unable to walk even with aids has an unstable presentation with greater osteonecrosis risk. Do not repeatedly test walking to prove instability. [AAOS SCFE presentation and risks](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/slipped-capital-femoral-epiphysis-scfe)

Once suspected, stop weight bearing and arrange urgent orthopedic assessment; use a bed or wheelchair for safe transfer. Do not attempt forceful hip manipulation or reduction. Imaging must fit the presentation: AP pelvis and an appropriate lateral view evaluate both hips, but **avoid frog-leg positioning in an acute or suspected unstable slip**; a cross-table lateral is the safer specified alternative. A chronic stable presentation may be imaged differently under the receiving protocol. SCFE requires surgical stabilization, and the opposite hip needs surveillance and sometimes prophylactic fixation according to specialist risk assessment. [RCH SCFE emergency handling and imaging pathway](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/fractures/Slipped_upper_femoral_epiphysis_SUFE_-_Emergency_Department/)

**Clinical Case:** A 15-year-old girl with progressive knee pain suddenly cannot bear weight after stepping off a curb. The knee has no effusion, but hip motion is markedly restricted. Normal weight and no direct hip trauma do not exclude SCFE. Keep her non-weight-bearing and arrange urgent assessment without forcing frog-leg positioning.

### Growth-related pain should fit the site, examination, and recovery course

**Osgood-Schlatter disease** is traction-related pain at the tibial tubercle during growth, often aggravated by running, jumping, or kneeling. A localized tender prominence with otherwise compatible knee function supports the diagnosis; a large effusion, fever, loss of active extension, acute avulsion mechanism, or hip-motion loss requires another assessment. Manage symptoms with load adjustment, quadriceps/hamstring flexibility and strength, and practical support. Activity may continue as tolerated in an established uncomplicated case; substantial pain or limping should prompt temporary reduction rather than instruction to push through. [AAOS Osgood-Schlatter disease](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/osgood-schlatter-disease-knee-pain/)

**Sever disease** is calcaneal apophyseal pain in a growing child, often during running/jumping sports. Local heel tenderness and a fitting activity history support it. Adjust activity, heel support, footwear, and calf flexibility. A fragmented-looking apophysis alone is not diagnostic because normal development varies. Unilateral severe rest pain, systemic symptoms, a wound, or persistent inability to walk needs evaluation for fracture, infection, or another cause. Reassess either apophyseal condition if recovery is atypical. [AAOS Sever disease](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/severs-disease/)

In toddlers, an occult tibial fracture can cause refusal to walk after an unwitnessed twist despite normal early films. Persistent painless asymmetry, limited hip abduction, leg-length difference, or abnormal development instead suggests dysplasia or neuromuscular disease; arrange pediatric assessment, not repeated sprain treatment. See 13B and 13D for developmental and safeguarding evaluation.
