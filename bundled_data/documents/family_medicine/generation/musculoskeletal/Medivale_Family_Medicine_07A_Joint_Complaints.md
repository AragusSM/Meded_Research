# Medivale Family Medicine

## 07A. Joint Complaints

Joint pain may reflect mechanical loading, crystals, infection, or systemic disease. Localize the pain, assess inflammation and functional change, and identify urgent threats before ordering tests. Osteoarthritis or gout does not exclude concurrent infection.

Back/neck assessment, regional injuries, and bone rehabilitation follow in 07B-07D. The condition-specific pathways below distinguish urgent infection assessment, symptom relief, and prevention of structural damage.

### Localize the complaint before naming the disease

**Arthralgia** is joint pain; **arthritis** requires inflammation. Look for synovial swelling, warmth, effusion, and restriction. Distinguish these from bony enlargement, bursitis, tendon tenderness, and myalgia. Compare sides, nearby joints, gait, strength, sensation, and circulation, separating pain-limited effort from true weakness. Greater pain with active than passive movement suggests a periarticular source; painful restricted passive motion suggests joint disease. Neither excludes infection.

Record onset, duration, number and distribution of joints, symmetry, migration, recurrence, stiffness, and effects of rest and activity. Monoarthritis affects one joint, oligoarthritis usually two to four, and polyarthritis five or more. Assess lost function in dressing, work, sleep, mobility, and caregiving. Pain intensity alone does not establish damage or urgency. Ask about injury, procedures, injections, skin wounds, travel, sexual exposure, diarrhea, and preceding infection.

Inflammatory pain typically causes prolonged morning stiffness, rest/night symptoms, soft swelling, and improvement with movement. Mechanical pain usually worsens with loading and causes brief stiffness after inactivity. Patterns overlap: severe OA can hurt at rest and inflammatory arthritis with use. Reassess every newly hot swollen joint regardless of prior radiographs. [NIAMS arthritis overview](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/arthritis), [Rheumatoid presentation](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/rheumatoid-arthritis)

| Clinical pattern | Helpful observations | Important next distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abrupt hot swollen joint | Rapid loss of movement; infection or crystals plausible | Decide urgently whether aspiration and hospital assessment are needed |
| Gradual load-related pain | Brief stiffness, bony enlargement, crepitus | Assess function and atypical features before assuming uncomplicated OA |
| Persistent small-joint swelling | Soft swelling at wrists, MCP/PIP or forefoot joints | Refer early for inflammatory arthritis even if blood tests are negative |
| Tendon insertion or whole-digit swelling | Enthesitis or dactylitis, sometimes psoriasis/eye/bowel symptoms | Consider spondyloarthritis rather than repeated isolated injury labels |
| Widespread pain without synovitis | Fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, diffuse tenderness | Assess pain processing and coexisting disease; do not invent joint inflammation |

**Fibromyalgia** can cause widespread pain, disturbed sleep, cognitive symptoms, and fatigue without destructive synovitis; it can coexist with RA or OA. Inflammatory markers alone neither establish fibromyalgia nor explain every painful site. Use examination and targeted testing to identify coexisting disease. Avoid empirical steroid courses as a diagnostic test. [NIAMS fibromyalgia assessment and treatment](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/fibromyalgia/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

### The hot joint: decide about infection first

Septic arthritis can follow bloodstream spread, direct inoculation, or nearby infection. Risk rises with immune suppression, diabetes, damaged joints, recent procedures, skin infection, and injection-drug exposure, but absence of a recognized risk does not exclude it. Fever may be absent. Normal blood leukocytes, ESR, or CRP cannot clear the joint. Arrange urgent aspiration and hospital/orthopedic assessment when infection is plausible; a routine outpatient urate result or elective MRI must not postpone source control.

The SANJO approach obtains synovial fluid promptly, prioritizing bacterial testing, then cell count/differential and crystals if fluid is scarce. A synovial leukocyte count **above 50,000 cells/microliter** increases suspicion but is not diagnostic; a lower count, negative Gram stain, or crystals does not exclude infection. Thresholds are less reliable with immune suppression. Obtain at least two blood-culture sets when fever, bacteremia, or sepsis is suspected. Collect diagnostic samples before antibiotics if the patient is stable and this is promptly feasible; **sepsis requires immediate treatment without waiting for aspiration**. Urgent drainage and empiric antibiotics must cover likely staphylococci, with MRSA and gram-negative coverage determined by patient risks and local resistance; narrow treatment when microbiology permits. [EBJIS SANJO recommendations](https://jbji.copernicus.org/articles/8/29/2023/index.html)

**Clinical Case:** A 71-year-old receiving immune-modifying treatment develops a swollen wrist after a skin wound. Temperature is 37.2 C, blood leukocytes 8,600/microliter, and aspirate contains CPP crystals with 29,000 leukocytes/microliter. Neither crystals nor reassuring blood results exclude infection. Send microbiology and arrange urgent specialist assessment for possible concurrent septic arthritis.

In children, acute refusal to use a limb or bear weight, painful restricted motion, or a suspected hip effusion warrants urgent assessment. Infection can be subtle, including with **Kingella kingae** in young children. PIDS/IDSA recommends immediate empiric treatment for an ill-appearing child or rapidly progressive illness, after blood cultures if feasible; aspiration should still follow promptly. In a well-appearing child, sampling before treatment requires careful observation and a realistic transport/procedure plan, not an unmonitored wait at home. Pediatric pathogen coverage, dosing, and drainage decisions are age-specific. Neonates need a separate pathway. [PIDS/IDSA pediatric bacterial arthritis](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/acute-bacterial-arthritis-in-pediatrics2/)

### Distinguish disseminated, reactive, and exposure-related disease

**Disseminated gonococcal infection** can produce asymmetric joint symptoms, tenosynovitis, and sparse pustular/acral lesions, or a purulent arthritis. Genital symptoms may be minimal or absent. Obtain specimens from exposed mucosal sites and relevant disseminated sites, with cultures for susceptibility when possible; a joint-only testing strategy can miss the source. Initial hospitalization and infectious-disease consultation are recommended, especially with uncertain diagnosis or purulent effusions. The CDC arthritis/arthritis-dermatitis regimen is **ceftriaxone 1 g IM or IV every 24 hours**, not the single-dose uncomplicated-genital regimen. Treat possible chlamydia if not excluded using the appropriate pregnancy-specific adaptation, and arrange partner care and other STI testing. [CDC disseminated gonococcal pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-adults.htm)

Examine for meningitis and endocarditis, which change treatment. For the arthritis-dermatitis syndrome, a susceptibility-guided oral switch may be considered **24-48 hours after substantial improvement**, with **at least seven days total treatment**. Purulent arthritis requires source-control assessment; CNS or cardiac infection needs a longer specialist-directed regimen. Chlamydia treatment and partner follow-up are detailed in 09B.

Reactive arthritis is an inflammatory response after certain gastrointestinal or genitourinary infections, rather than ordinary bacterial growth inside the affected joint. Lower-limb asymmetric arthritis, heel enthesitis, dactylitis, and eye or urinary inflammation are useful clues; the entire classic triad need not be present. A preceding diarrheal illness does not exempt a hot joint from sampling. Identify and treat a current infection when present, but antibiotics are not automatically indicated merely because postinfectious arthritis persists. Ongoing inflammatory symptoms need reassessment and sometimes rheumatology treatment. [NIAMS reactive arthritis presentation](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/reactive-arthritis), [Cause-directed treatment](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/reactive-arthritis/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

Consider Lyme arthritis with compatible exposure and substantial swelling of a large joint, often a knee. A recalled tick bite is not required. The AAN/ACR/IDSA pathway begins with **serum antibody testing** rather than joint-fluid antibody tests; selected seropositive uncertain cases may benefit from synovial PCR. An isolated IgM result is not persuasive evidence of late Lyme arthritis. Initial confirmed Lyme arthritis generally receives **28 days of an appropriate oral antibiotic**. Persistent swelling needs a response-based reassessment, not indefinite antibiotics without considering postinfectious inflammation or another diagnosis. Acute pyogenic infection remains a competing concern when the presentation is severe. [IDSA Lyme arthritis diagnosis and treatment](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/lyme-disease/)

### Aspirate with a clinical question and a result plan

Aspiration diagnoses infection or crystals and can relieve an effusion; injection is a separate treatment decision. Explain benefits, alternatives, bleeding/infection risks, and result follow-up. Review anticoagulants, bleeding history, skin integrity, allergies, and the joint involved; coordinate rather than routinely stopping anticoagulation. Deep or difficult joints may need image guidance. Prosthetic joints require a dedicated infection pathway.

Use sterile technique and an appropriate route; do not puncture an abscess and avoid overlying cellulitis when possible. Ensure the laboratory can handle bacterial studies and polarized microscopy. Gross cloudiness does not distinguish crystals from bacteria. A dry tap is not proof that the joint is sterile. **Do not inject a glucocorticoid into a joint with possible infection.** Routine bacterial cultures generally require **five to seven days**; persistent suspicion with negative cultures may require prolonged incubation or molecular testing. No growth at 48 hours is not an antibiotic-stop rule. Record who reviews delayed results and reassess worsening symptoms. [SANJO microbiologic methods](https://jbji.copernicus.org/articles/8/29/2023/index.html), [ACR joint aspiration and injection information](https://rheumatology.org/patients/joint-injections-joint-aspirations)

| Finding | What it supports | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent crystals | Monosodium urate deposition | Absence of concurrent infection |
| Rhomboid/rod-like, weakly positively birefringent crystals | Calcium pyrophosphate deposition | The cause of every symptom or freedom from infection |
| Neutrophil-rich inflammatory fluid | An inflammatory response | A clean separation of infection, crystals, and immune disease |
| Positive bacterial culture | Organism-directed infection management when clinically consistent | That a separate crystal or structural problem cannot coexist |
| Negative Gram stain or early culture | Incomplete microbiologic information | A safe automatic discharge or antibiotic-stop rule |

### Gout is a crystal-burden disease, not a dietary judgment

Urate from purine metabolism can accumulate as monosodium urate crystals and trigger inflammatory flares. Renal excretion, genetics, medicines, metabolic factors, food, and alcohol contribute. Age, kidney impairment, and thiazide/loop exposure increase risk, but gout also occurs in younger and premenopausal patients. Recurrent inflammation, tophi, erosions, and stones reflect urate burden; dietary blame neither diagnoses nor treats it. [NIAMS gout mechanisms and risks](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/gout)

Podagra is recognizable, but ankles, knees, wrists, and other sites can be involved; established gout can become polyarticular or chronically inflammatory. Examine for tophi and document the frequency and effect of prior episodes. **Serum urate alone does not diagnose gout**, and a value within the laboratory interval during a flare does not exclude it. NICE advises repeating a low value **at least two weeks after the flare settles** when suspicion remains. Aspirate when diagnosis is uncertain or infection must be assessed. [NICE gout diagnostic assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng219/chapter/Recommendations)

Characteristic ultrasound deposits or dual-energy CT can establish gout without fluid confirmation under EULAR guidance when sampling is difficult. Suspected infection still requires aspiration. Deposits may be incidental, so correlate with symptoms and examination. Conventional radiographs may remain normal early and show structural damage later. [EULAR crystal-arthropathy imaging](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/38320811/)

### Treat the flare safely while planning prevention separately

Choose an NSAID, low-dose colchicine, or glucocorticoid according to renal function, GI/bleeding and cardiovascular risk, diabetes, infection concern, medicines, and preferences. Oral or selected local steroids can help but carry hyperglycemia and infection risks. Add ice, temporary load reduction, and reassessment; symptom relief does not confirm gout. [NICE acute gout treatment selection](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng219/chapter/Recommendations)

For an eligible adult using this **0.6-mg colchicine tablet** preparation, the labeled acute regimen is **1.2 mg orally at the first sign of a flare, then 0.6 mg one hour later**. The total loading course is **1.8 mg**, not repeated hourly doses until diarrhea. Renal/hepatic impairment, dialysis, recent courses, and existing prophylaxis change allowable use. Colchicine with a P-glycoprotein or strong CYP3A4 inhibitor is contraindicated in patients with renal or hepatic impairment. Clarithromycin and certain antiviral or transplant medicines are particularly important exposures. Toxicity can include muscle injury and marrow suppression; diarrhea should not be treated as a target of dosing. [Colchicine tablet prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=2cd5298e-43c6-a8dc-4467-9a3e1ade9307)

For that preparation, severe renal impairment (**CrCl below 30 mL/min**) limits repeat flare courses to **no more than once every two weeks**; dialysis reduces the acute course to **0.6 mg once**, with the same repeat limit. The label does not recommend flare dosing on top of prophylactic colchicine in renal impairment. These are not permissions to use colchicine despite a contraindicated interaction; choose another flare treatment when necessary.

**Clinical Case:** A 64-year-old with gout, stage G4 CKD, apixaban use, and newly prescribed clarithromycin requests his old colchicine course. Renal impairment, the interaction, and bleeding risk make neither that course nor an OTC NSAID automatically safe. Reassess infection and coordinate an alternative flare treatment and, for recurrent attacks, prevention.

Under the ACR pathway, offer urate-lowering therapy for tophi, gout-related radiographic damage, or **at least two flares per year**. More than one previous flare, even with fewer than two per year, supports a conditional shared decision. After a first flare, CKD stage 3 or worse, urate **above 9 mg/dL**, or urolithiasis supports conditional initiation. Allopurinol is preferred first-line, including in CKD, using a low start and serial urate-guided titration toward **below 6 mg/dL**. Prevention is usually ongoing rather than a three-month cure. Give concurrent anti-inflammatory prophylaxis for **at least three to six months**, extending when activity warrants. Continue established urate-lowering treatment during flares; starting it during a flare is permissible with appropriate acute management. Do not routinely treat asymptomatic hyperuricemia as gout. [ACR long-term treatment recommendations](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltee37abb6b278ab2c/blt04d52e3b6ff5112f/632cab5b258fb55f6b2186af/gout-guideline-2020.pdf)

The allopurinol product label starts uncomplicated adult gout treatment at **100 mg daily**. Its renal starting table specifies **50 mg daily at eGFR above 30 through 60 mL/min**, **50 mg every other day above 15 through 30**, and still less frequent starts at lower function. Titrate cautiously with serial urate and renal monitoring; a starting dose is not a permanent efficacy ceiling. Check baseline blood count, kidney/liver status, interactions, and hypersensitivity risk. Consider **HLA-B*58:01** testing before initiation in higher-prevalence groups, including the ACR's Han Chinese, Korean, Thai and African American groups. A negative result does not eliminate severe reactions. Stop and urgently assess a new rash or systemic hypersensitivity symptoms. Xanthine oxidase inhibition interacts importantly with azathioprine/mercaptopurine; these are not ordinary unreviewed combinations. [Allopurinol prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=5a1bfae2-116f-4ef9-bb7b-3b69ac74df03)

Febuxostat is another xanthine oxidase inhibitor, but U.S. labeling reserves it for inadequate response to maximally titrated allopurinol, intolerance, or circumstances where allopurinol is inadvisable. Its cardiovascular-death boxed warning requires an explicit risk discussion, especially with established cardiovascular disease. **Concomitant azathioprine or mercaptopurine is contraindicated.** Uricosuric treatment is not interchangeable with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor in significant CKD or stone risk. Severe refractory tophaceous disease may need specialist infusion therapy, not indefinite renewal of an ineffective low dose. [Febuxostat U.S. labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=164feabd-f722-445b-b81a-d975b3db5f53)

Explain that urate lowering can initially increase flares. Review adherence, serial urate, prophylaxis tolerance, and refill access before declaring failure. Discuss alcohol, sugary drinks, weight, and medication contributors without promising diet alone will dissolve established deposits. Preserve the indication when changing a diuretic or cardiovascular medicine, and provide an accessible early-flare plan. [NIAMS gout longitudinal care](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/gout/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

### CPPD resembles gout but has a different long-term target

Calcium pyrophosphate deposition can cause an acute inflammatory attack, OA with deposits, or persistent inflammatory arthritis. Knees and wrists are common sites, particularly in older adults. Chondrocalcinosis supports deposition but is neither required on every film nor proof that an incidental deposit caused today's symptoms. CPP crystals may be small and weakly birefringent, so a negative microscopy report is not an infallible exclusion. The 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria were developed for **classification**, not as a requirement to delay clinical care until a score is met. [ACR/EULAR CPPD classification and limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10529191/)

Treat acute inflammation with appropriately selected anti-inflammatory therapy or aspiration/local steroid after infection assessment. Unlike gout, there is currently no established treatment that dissolves CPP deposits; **allopurinol does not remove CPP crystals**. Recurrent attacks or persistent inflammation may need rheumatology-directed prophylaxis. Earlier-than-expected or unusual CPPD should prompt a targeted search for associated disorders, including iron overload, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, and hypophosphatasia. Choose calcium/PTH, magnesium, iron studies, and other tests from the presentation rather than ordering every endocrine test for every older patient's incidental calcification. Treating an associated disorder does not guarantee disappearance of established deposits. [ACR CPPD education, updated 2025](https://rheumatology.org/patients/calcium-pyrophosphate-deposition-cppd)

**Clinical Case:** A 52-year-old with recurrent wrist swelling, CPP crystals, and persistent hypomagnesemia takes several GI medicines. Review magnesium loss, renal handling, medicines, and other indicated metabolic causes while treating flares safely. Allopurinol will not remove CPP deposits. New infection features in a later episode still warrant sampling.

### Osteoarthritis: treat the person and the whole joint

OA affects cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium, and surrounding structures. Age, injury, mechanics, and excess load contribute. Common sites are knees, hips, DIP/PIP joints, and thumb bases; bony DIP and PIP enlargements are Heberden and Bouchard nodes. Hip disease may cause groin or referred knee pain. Disability, sleep, work, and mood may correlate poorly with radiographic severity. [NIAMS osteoarthritis presentation and mechanisms](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/osteoarthritis)

NICE permits a clinical OA diagnosis without routine imaging in a person **45 or older** with activity-related pain and no morning stiffness or stiffness lasting **30 minutes or less**. Younger patients and atypical presentations need a broader assessment, not an automatic exclusion of all OA. Rapid deterioration, marked heat/swelling, systemic symptoms, or substantial trauma should reopen the differential. When structural imaging is needed, radiographs generally precede advanced scans; an MRI abnormality does not necessarily identify the painful structure. Use the patient's functional goals rather than requiring a scan to authorize movement. [NICE OA diagnosis and reassessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG226/chapter/recommendations)

The **2026 VA/DoD hip/knee pathway** supports structured physical therapy, tailored activity, and weight management where appropriate. Topical NSAIDs are an option for knee OA; oral NSAIDs or acetaminophen are selected according to risk, and duloxetine can be considered for knee symptoms. Avoid starting opioids, including tramadol, as routine hip/knee OA treatment. Persistent pain may justify an intra-articular corticosteroid; hip injections should be image-guided. The pathway finds insufficient evidence for knee viscosupplementation and advises against it for hip OA. Do not translate uncertain evidence for orthobiologics into a promise of cartilage regeneration. Review benefit, burden, adverse effects, and alternatives. [VA/DoD 2026 OA recommendations](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/CD/OA/Osteoarthritis-CPG_2026-Guideline_final_20260618.pdf)

For hand OA, the **2026 ACR summary** conditionally supports hand exercise and first-CMC/finger orthoses and strongly supports topical NSAIDs; it conditionally favors topical over oral NSAIDs for knee/hand disease. Fit a splint to the painful task rather than immobilizing every joint indefinitely. Canes, selected knee braces, occupational adaptations, and strengthening preserve participation. A distinct exception is **erosive hand OA**, for which ACR conditionally supports rheumatology-directed methotrexate; do not extend this to routine hand, hip, or knee OA. Topical drugs are unlikely to help a deep hip joint. Oral NSAIDs still require renal, BP/volume, cardiovascular, ulcer/bleeding, and interacting-drug assessment; gastric protection does not remove renal or cardiovascular harm. [ACR 2026 approved OA summary](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltee37abb6b278ab2c/bltb3d12c34020da842/oa-guideline-summary-2026.pdf)

Begin tolerable activity and adjust load, frequency, and recovery. Distinguish expected exercise soreness from a new hot joint, injury, or major decline, and track a functional goal. Address pain amplification, poor sleep, depression, and fear of movement alongside joint treatment.

**Clinical Case:** A 68-year-old with knee OA and heart failure avoids community meetings because of stairs despite only moderate radiographic disease. Prioritize strengthening, a walking aid, accessible activity, and safer localized treatment over high-dose oral NSAIDs. Review walking/stair function and consider surgical assessment if nonsurgical care is ineffective or unsuitable.

NICE bases joint-replacement referral on substantial quality-of-life impact and ineffective or unsuitable nonsurgical treatment. Age, BMI, smoking, or comorbidity alone should not automatically bar referral; individual operative risk still needs discussion and optimization. Arthroscopic lavage/debridement is not OA treatment. A new true locked joint, acute injury, or suspected infection is a different problem from uncomplicated degenerative pain. Before an injection in someone approaching arthroplasty, coordinate timing and infection-risk precautions with the surgeon. [NICE OA referral and procedures](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG226/chapter/recommendations)

### Persistent synovitis needs early disease-modifying care

RA is immune-mediated synovial inflammation capable of damaging cartilage and bone and affecting tissues beyond joints. Symmetric wrist, MCP, PIP, or MTP swelling with prolonged morning stiffness is characteristic, but early disease may be incomplete or asymmetric. DIP-predominant bony change suggests other diagnoses; it does not exclude coexisting RA. Look for fatigue, nodules, dry eyes/mouth, breathlessness, and neurologic or cervical symptoms. Do not wait for deformity to make referral worthwhile. [NIAMS rheumatoid arthritis overview](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/rheumatoid-arthritis)

For suspected persistent synovitis, the NICE referral pathway is urgent when small hand/foot joints or multiple joints are affected, **even with normal inflammatory markers and negative RF/anti-CCP**. Tests should not delay referral. RF is not specific to RA; anti-CCP is more specific but a negative result does not exclude seronegative disease. CBC, renal/liver tests and targeted investigations establish context and a treatment baseline. Hands/feet radiographs can document erosions, but normal early films do not make persistent clinical swelling harmless. Classification criteria support structured research/assessment; neither a duration score nor waiting six weeks is a prerequisite for referral. [NICE persistent-synovitis assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng100/chapter/Recommendations)

**Clinical Case:** A 39-year-old hair stylist has four weeks of MCP swelling and morning difficulty making a fist despite negative RF/anti-CCP, normal CRP, and no radiographic erosions. Refer early to rheumatology; negative tests do not erase synovitis. Begin work adaptations and symptom care while the diagnosis is established.

The EULAR 2025 update recommends prompt DMARD treatment after RA diagnosis, with **methotrexate in the initial strategy** unless contraindicated or intolerable, and a target of sustained remission or low disease activity. Monitor active disease frequently; no improvement by about **three months** or failure to reach target by **six months** prompts treatment adjustment. Biologic treatment is added when the conventional strategy fails; JAK inhibitors require particular cardiovascular, cancer, and thromboembolic risk consideration. Short-term glucocorticoids may bridge a conventional DMARD's onset but should be tapered off rapidly. These are specialist-directed decisions, not a primary-care instruction to start a biologic empirically. [EULAR RA 2025 update, published 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ard.2026.01.023)

**ACR 2021 conditionally favors a conventional DMARD without routine short-term glucocorticoids**, whereas EULAR more readily considers bridging. Both prioritize disease modification over chronic steroids; NSAIDs and analgesics do not prevent erosive damage. Disease activity, pregnancy plans, infection history, lung/liver/kidney disease, and access affect selection. Primary care supports prompt referral, toxicity recognition, and completion of monitoring. [ACR RA treatment recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9273041/)

### Methotrexate safety and shared longitudinal care

For inflammatory arthritis, methotrexate is taken **once weekly, not daily**; daily medication errors can be fatal. Specify the weekday and actual tablet strength, use teach-back, and provide folate as prescribed. Check pregnancy status and reproductive plans; methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy for nonmalignant disease. CBC, liver tests, and kidney function require baseline and ongoing review, with dose/interval decisions tailored to clearance and toxicity. Review alcohol, interacting prescriptions, and OTC products; avoid trimethoprim or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole combinations because severe marrow suppression can result. Mouth ulcers, fever, bruising, new cough, or breathlessness require prompt assessment rather than simply waiting for the next routine blood draw. [Methotrexate tablet safety, 2026 label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=09931d44-a3f8-49dc-a3f2-b527ee4214bf), [SPS low-dose methotrexate interactions](https://www.sps.nhs.uk/articles/managing-interactions-with-methotrexate/)

Coordinate infection screening, vaccination, pregnancy counseling, and monitoring during immune-modifying therapy. Address cardiovascular risk, smoking, bone health, mood, activity, and everyday function. Persistent pain may reflect damage or another pain disorder rather than active inflammation; transient pain relief may also mask ongoing synovitis. Continue rehabilitation and occupational support. [NIAMS RA team care and monitoring](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/rheumatoid-arthritis/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

### Recognize spondyloarthritis and systemic clues

Spondyloarthritis can involve the spine, peripheral joints, entheses, and whole digits. Ask about psoriasis in hidden sites and nail pitting/onycholysis, inflammatory bowel disease, prior uveitis, family history, and preceding infection. Psoriatic arthritis may precede recognized skin disease and can involve DIP joints or an asymmetric distribution. A normal CRP, negative HLA-B27, or normal plain film does not independently exclude the group. Refer persistent unexplained enthesitis, dactylitis, or inflammatory arthritis appropriately. A painful red eye with photophobia or blurred vision needs **same-day ophthalmologic assessment** for possible acute anterior uveitis. [NICE spondyloarthritis recognition and referral](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng65/chapter/recommendations)

Axial symptoms beginning in a younger adult, night pain, alternating buttock symptoms, and improvement with activity warrant the structured back-pain assessment in 07B. Do not use HLA-B27 as a population arthritis screen. Peripheral and axial disease need different treatment choices; a medicine useful for peripheral synovitis is not automatically effective for axial inflammation. Coordinate rheumatology with dermatology, gastroenterology, or ophthalmology when those organs are involved rather than treating each recurrence as an unrelated complaint.

In an adult over 50, new bilateral shoulder/hip-girdle aching and marked stiffness raises **polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)**; true objective weakness suggests alternatives such as myopathy. Check ESR/CRP in context, recognizing that they are nonspecific, and assess mimics before committing to prolonged steroids. Ask directly about new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, and visual disturbance. Suspected associated **giant cell arteritis (GCA)** requires urgent assessment and treatment; visual symptoms are an emergency. Steroid responsiveness alone is not a diagnosis. [NIAMS PMR/GCA recognition](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/polymyalgia-rheumatica-giant-cell-arteritis), [NIAMS evaluation](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/polymyalgia-rheumatica-giant-cell-arteritis/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take)

The **2025 EULAR recommendations, published in 2026**, advise specialist assessment for suspected PMR or GCA. In uncomplicated suspected PMR, glucocorticoids can usually await diagnostic confirmation; confirmed PMR remission induction uses **15-25 mg/day prednisone-equivalent**, followed by an individualized taper and response/toxicity monitoring. This low-dose pathway is **not adequate for suspected GCA**: start indicated higher-dose glucocorticoids without waiting for confirmation and arrange urgent specialist care. Review glucose, BP, infection and bone risk during sustained treatment; relapse or toxicity may require steroid-sparing therapy. Headache/visual evaluation is developed in 11A. [EULAR PMR/large-vessel vasculitis recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ard.2026.06.009)

Arthritis with photosensitivity, oral/nasal ulcers, Raynaud symptoms, cytopenias, serositis, or urinary abnormalities may indicate lupus or another systemic disease. Choose ANA and additional serology according to the phenotype rather than screening every ache; a positive antibody is not the disease itself. Check blood pressure, CBC, kidney function and urine when organ involvement is plausible. Proteinuria, active urine sediment, neurologic symptoms, or cardiopulmonary compromise can make referral urgent even when joint symptoms seem modest. **Joint improvement does not establish that organ-threatening disease is controlled.** [NIAMS systemic lupus manifestations](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/lupus), [NIDDK lupus nephritis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/lupus-nephritis)

### Adapt evaluation and close the loop

Children with persistent swelling, morning stiffness, or an unexplained limp need pediatric assessment for inflammatory disease and its mimics. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis is not simply adult RA at a smaller dose. Some associated eye inflammation is silent, requiring the specialist's scheduled ophthalmic surveillance rather than waiting for a red eye. Fever, weight loss, bruising, focal bone pain, or inability to bear weight should broaden the assessment for infection, malignancy, or another urgent cause. [NIAMS juvenile arthritis](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/juvenile-arthritis)

In pregnancy, verify anti-inflammatory and disease-modifying safety for the gestational stage and product. FDA advises avoiding NSAIDs from **20 weeks onward** unless necessary under clinician supervision: at 20-30 weeks use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration and consider amniotic-fluid ultrasound if treatment exceeds **48 hours**; avoid at **30 weeks and later** because of additional ductal-closure risk. This warning does not apply to prescribed **81-mg aspirin** for an obstetric indication. In older adults and CKD, review falls, fluid retention, accumulation, anticoagulants, and regimen complexity. Sustained glucocorticoids add glucose, infection, bone-loss, and withdrawal risks.
