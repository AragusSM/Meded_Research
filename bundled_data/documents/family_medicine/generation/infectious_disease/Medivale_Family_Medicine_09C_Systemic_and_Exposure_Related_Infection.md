# Medivale Family Medicine

## 09C. Systemic and Exposure-Related Infection

After a tick bite, needlestick, or animal bite, assess whether prevention can still reduce risk. Fever, expanding rash, anemia, or organ dysfunction requires disease assessment rather than prophylaxis alone. Establish exposure/timing, immunity, and follow-up; treat emergencies without waiting for reassuring early serology.

CDC exposure pathways guide prevention; AAN/ACR/IDSA and IDSA guide Lyme disease and babesiosis. Account for age, pregnancy, organ function, interactions, and formulation. Related care: liver 05D, wounds 08D, systemic triage 09A, HIV/STIs 09B, respiratory antivirals/renal regimens 03D.

### A tick bite: decide whether this is prevention or disease

Remove an attached tick promptly with fine-tipped tweezers near the skin, using steady traction rather than heat or petroleum. Record the location, likely species, attachment duration, engorgement, and removal time. Species identification can help, but testing the tick for a pathogen does not establish whether transmission occurred. An asymptomatic person's immediate Lyme antibody test is not useful for diagnosing infection acquired that day. [CDC tick removal and follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/after-a-tick-bite/index.html)

For Lyme prophylaxis, the AAN/ACR/IDSA pathway defines a high-risk bite by three features: an identified *Ixodes* vector, exposure in a highly endemic area, and attachment for **at least 36 hours**. When these are met, give single-dose oral doxycycline within **72 hours after removal**: **200 mg once in adults**, or **4.4 mg/kg once in children, maximum 200 mg**. If risk cannot be classified confidently, observation with instructions is appropriate. Pregnancy requires an individualized benefit-risk discussion; do not invent an equivalent single-dose amoxicillin substitute. Prophylaxis is not guaranteed protection and does not prevent every tick-borne infection. [AAN/ACR/IDSA prevention recommendations](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/lyme-disease/)

**Clinical Case.** An asymptomatic 24-kg child has an engorged *Ixodes* bite in a highly endemic area, attached for 48 hours and removed 20 hours ago. If doxycycline is suitable, 24 x 4.4 = **105.6 mg once**, below the 200-mg maximum; measure the actual formulation accurately. Later fever/headache requires disease reassessment, not repeat prophylaxis.

Give return precautions for expanding rash, fever, severe headache, weakness, facial asymmetry, palpitations, or joint swelling. Missing bite recall does not exclude infection. Discuss age/product-appropriate repellents, clothing, checks, and prompt removal. Immediate small itchy reactions differ from lesions expanding over days.

### Recognize Lyme disease without requiring a target-shaped rash

Early Lyme disease can cause **erythema migrans**, a progressively enlarging lesion that need not have central clearing. In a compatible exposure setting, a typical lesion is diagnosed clinically; early antibodies may be absent. Multiple lesions, cranial neuropathy, radicular pain, meningitis, conduction disturbance, or later large-joint swelling suggest dissemination. Consider cellulitis, tinea, local bite reactions, other tick infections, and noninfectious disease when the appearance or course differs.

Use an FDA-cleared standard or modified two-tier serologic pathway. Antibodies can persist for years and cannot prove cure; isolated IgM is unhelpful after more than 30 days. Low pretest probability and cross-reactions mislead. An atypical early lesion with negative testing may need reassessment/repeat serology, not dismissal or an unsupported chronic-infection diagnosis. [CDC Lyme testing and limitations](https://www.cdc.gov/lyme/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)

For uncomplicated erythema migrans in a suitable adult, the named IDSA pathway uses **doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 10 days**, or **amoxicillin 500 mg orally three times daily for 14 days**, or **cefuroxime axetil 500 mg orally twice daily for 14 days**. Pediatric amoxicillin is 50 mg/kg/day divided into three doses, maximum 500 mg per dose; cefuroxime is 30 mg/kg/day divided into two doses, maximum 500 mg per dose, for 14 days. These beta-lactams are options when doxycycline is unsuitable, including selected pregnant patients. Verify allergy and kidney-related adjustments. Do not extend the early-rash regimen to meningitis, arthritis, or carditis. [CDC early-Lyme dosing](https://www.cdc.gov/lyme/hcp/clinical-care/erythema-migrans-rash.html)

Ask about syncope, presyncope, breathlessness, chest discomfort, and palpitations. Obtain an ECG for possible cardiac involvement. **Symptomatic disease, second- or third-degree block, or PR at least 300 milliseconds** warrants urgent hospital assessment, monitoring, and initially intravenous treatment under CDC's severity pathway; do not await serology. Mild carditis can receive selected oral treatment, whereas severe disease commonly starts ceftriaxone, with transition when improved and a total 14-21-day course. Temporary pacing may be needed. Facial palsy or meningitic symptoms also require a manifestation-specific assessment, not automatic use of an early-rash prescription. [CDC Lyme carditis](https://www.cdc.gov/lyme/hcp/clinical-care/lyme-carditis.html)

### Fever after outdoor exposure: do not wait for a classic triad

Rickettsial infection can initially resemble a nonspecific viral illness. Geography, season, outdoor work, travel, and tick exposure modify suspicion, but a missing bite history or absent rash does not exclude it. Endothelial injury in RMSF can progress to vascular leak and multiorgan disease. Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and aminotransferase elevation help suggest ehrlichiosis or anaplasmosis; early laboratory values can nevertheless be unrevealing. PCR and acute/convalescent serology have different timing limitations. Obtain appropriate samples without delaying treatment in a compatible illness. [CDC tick-borne rickettsial diagnostic framework](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/rr/rr6502a1.htm)

| Syndrome | Useful clues, not required criteria | Immediate clinical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RMSF | Fever, headache, gastrointestinal symptoms; rash may later begin at wrists/ankles and spread toward the trunk, sometimes involving palms/soles | Treat suspected illness before the rash or confirmatory antibodies appear |
| Ehrlichiosis | Fever with leukopenia, low platelets, and elevated aminotransferases; rash is variable | A routine beta-lactam does not substitute for doxycycline |
| Anaplasmosis | Similar febrile cytopenic illness; rash is uncommon | Consider overlapping *Ixodes*-transmitted infection when recovery is incomplete |
| Babesiosis | Fever with hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, jaundice, or dark urine | An intraerythrocytic parasite requires a different regimen, not doxycycline alone |

For suspected RMSF, use doxycycline in **all ages**, including children younger than eight; pregnancy is not a reason to leave a potentially fatal illness untreated. Selected adult dosing is **100 mg every 12 hours**; children below 45 kg receive **2.2 mg/kg per dose every 12 hours, maximum 100 mg per dose**. Use oral or intravenous delivery according to severity and absorption. Continue at least three days after fever resolves and clinical improvement occurs, with a minimum course of five to seven days. Sulfonamides are not substitutes. Severe allergy needs urgent specialist management. No routine antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended for an asymptomatic bite to prevent RMSF. [CDC RMSF care](https://www.cdc.gov/rocky-mountain-spotted-fever/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

Ehrlichiosis uses the same adult/weight-based doxycycline framework, generally at least five to seven days and until 72 hours after fever resolves with improvement. Anaplasmosis generally requires **10-14 days**, accounting for possible concurrent *Borrelia* infection. Neither disease has a routine post-bite prophylactic antibiotic pathway. In mild anaplasmosis during pregnancy, an expert may select an alternative, but rifampin does not reliably cover RMSF or Lyme coinfection. Failure to improve should prompt reconsideration of severity, diagnosis, absorption, and coinfection. [CDC ehrlichiosis treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/ehrlichiosis/hcp/clinical-care/index.html), [CDC anaplasmosis treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/anaplasmosis/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

Babesiosis is particularly concerning in asplenia, advanced age, and substantial immune suppression. Confirm acute infection with **blood smear or PCR**, not a single positive antibody result. Fever that persists despite doxycycline, especially with falling hemoglobin and biochemical hemolysis, should trigger this possibility. Selected severe cases require transfusion expertise, including consideration of exchange transfusion for high parasitemia or major hemolytic/organ complications; the percentage alone is not the whole decision. [IDSA babesiosis diagnosis and escalation](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/babesiosis/)

For an appropriate adult with mild-to-moderate babesiosis managed as an outpatient, the usual regimen is **atovaquone 750 mg orally twice daily with food, preferably a fatty meal, plus azithromycin 500 mg orally on day 1 then 250 mg daily**, completing seven to ten days. This is combination therapy, not either drug alone. Severe disease, pregnancy, immune suppression, persistent parasitemia, and inability to eat require a different supervised plan. [CDC adult babesiosis regimen](https://www.cdc.gov/babesiosis/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

Highly immunocompromised patients can need substantially longer treatment and smear-directed follow-up. Pregnancy changes drug selection, often favoring clindamycin plus quinine under expert care. Review interactions, tolerability, and organ function rather than borrowing a malaria prescription. Most asymptomatic patients do not need treatment.

**Clinical Case.** A 61-year-old on B-cell-depleting therapy remains febrile after two days of doxycycline for a woodland-associated cytopenic illness. Hemoglobin falls from 12.4 to 9.3 g/dL with rising bilirubin/LDH. Assess hemolysis and urgently obtain Babesia smear/PCR rather than simply extending doxycycline; investigate alternatives and admit if unstable.

### Animal bites: assess injury, bacterial infection, and rabies separately

Assess bite injury, bacterial infection, tetanus, and rabies separately. Irrigate and check depth, movement, neurovascular status, and foreign material; hand punctures/fight bites may conceal deep injury. IDSA recommends **three to five days of antibiotic prophylaxis** for selected high-risk bites: significant hand/face injury, deep penetration, edema, asplenia, immune suppression, or advanced liver disease. Infected wounds need treatment. Amoxicillin-clavulanate often covers oral aerobes/anaerobes; adjust for host/renal/pregnancy/age/allergy/severity. See 08D for closure/tetanus. [IDSA bite management](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/skin-and-soft-tissue-infections/)

Rabies prevention must precede symptoms. Assess saliva/neural-tissue contact with broken skin or mucosa, species, geography, behavior, animal testing, and prior vaccination. Proximity/contaminated clothing alone is not equivalent. Direct bat contact needs assessment when a bite/scratch cannot be excluded. Consult public health promptly, including uncertain exposures in patients unable to give reliable histories. [CDC exposure assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/rabies/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)

In coordination with public health, a healthy **dog, cat, or ferret** can undergo a **10-day observation** after exposing a person, even if vaccinated. This is not a universal observation rule for wildlife. An ill animal or other high-risk exposure needs a species- and circumstance-specific testing/PEP decision. Do not have the patient attempt unsafe capture. International exposure and unavailable animals can substantially change the decision. [CDC animal observation and testing](https://www.cdc.gov/rabies/hcp/veterinarians/index.html)

When PEP is indicated, clean wounds immediately. For an immunocompetent person who has not previously received an appropriate rabies vaccine series, use HRIG plus **1-mL intramuscular rabies vaccine on days 0, 3, 7, and 14**. Day 0 is the first vaccine day, not necessarily the exposure day. Give vaccine in the deltoid; anterolateral thigh is acceptable in children, but not the buttock. Previously appropriately vaccinated immunocompetent patients receive vaccine on **days 0 and 3 without HRIG**. Immunocompromised patients need an expert-directed plan, generally a fifth vaccine dose on day 28 and post-series antibody assessment; uncertain prior vaccination or foreign regimens require verification. Pregnancy is not a contraindication to indicated PEP. [CDC PEP guidance, September 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/rabies/hcp/clinical-care/post-exposure-prophylaxis.html)

HRIG is **20 IU/kg once** for all ages when indicated. Infiltrate around/into wounds as anatomically feasible, using judgment for fingers and other constrained sites; give remaining volume intramuscularly distant from vaccine. Never mix HRIG and vaccine or place the first vaccine at the same site. Check concentration before calculating volume: 150-IU/mL and 300-IU/mL products are not volume-equivalent, and dilution instructions differ. Do not increase the total dose merely to fill multiple wounds. [CDC rabies biologics](https://www.cdc.gov/rabies/hcp/clinical-care/biologics.html)

If indicated HRIG was omitted initially, it can still be given **through day 7 after the first vaccine**, not seven days after the bite. Do not delay starting vaccine while locating HRIG; coordinate completion promptly with public health. A lapse of a few days in a later vaccine dose generally calls for resuming with appropriate spacing, not automatically restarting the entire series; major deviations need expert review. [CDC rabies timing and error management](https://www.cdc.gov/rabies/hcp/clinical-care/post-exposure-prophylaxis.html)

**Example: HRIG volume.** A 70-kg unvaccinated adult needing PEP receives 70 x 20 = **1,400 IU**: 9.33 mL at 150 IU/mL or 4.67 mL at 300 IU/mL before product-appropriate dilution. These are total volumes, not amounts to force into a finger. Document infiltration, distant remainder, vaccine site, and dates; assess tetanus separately.

### TB: an immune-response test does not distinguish latent from active disease

Screen asymptomatic adults for latent TB by exposure/progression risk: high-prevalence-country residence, higher-risk congregate settings, close contact, immune suppression, or planned biologic therapy. USPSTF screening does not replace symptomatic evaluation/contact investigation; a positive test requires assessment before treatment. [USPSTF targeted screening](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/latent-tuberculosis-infection-screening)

IGRA avoids BCG-related false positivity and a return visit for reading. Borderline, indeterminate, or invalid results are not negative results; repeat or alternative testing may help. A negative result can occur during the early immune-response window or advanced immune suppression. After a recent infectious contact, repeat a negative infection test **eight to ten weeks after the last exposure**. Test choice in young children follows pediatric guidance and local expertise; CDC generally favors TST below age five, while acknowledging use of IGRA by some experts. [CDC IGRA guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/testing-diagnosis/interferon-gamma-release-assay.html)

TST measures **induration, not redness**, at 48-72 hours. Interpret the result by risk, not a single universal threshold or an assumption that BCG explains any positive reaction.

| TST induration considered positive | Selected qualifying settings |
| --- | --- |
| At least 5 mm | HIV, recent infectious contact, transplant, significant immune suppression, or fibrotic radiographic changes consistent with prior TB |
| At least 10 mm | Higher-prevalence-country origin, higher-risk congregate setting, mycobacteriology laboratory work, certain progression-risk illnesses, or young children/relevant pediatric exposure |
| At least 15 mm | No known TB risk factors |

These categories are not an exhaustive replacement for the CDC risk list. A negative skin test cannot rule out active disease in a symptomatic patient. [CDC TST interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/testing-diagnosis/tuberculin-skin-test.html)

Persistent cough, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis, unexplained fever, or possible extrapulmonary disease calls for clinical evaluation, chest imaging, and site-appropriate specimens. Suspected pulmonary TB requires airborne precautions and public-health coordination. Obtain sputum for smear, molecular testing, culture, and susceptibility testing; CDC describes at least three specimens separated by 8-24 hours, including an early-morning sample. Smear negativity does not exclude TB, and acid-fast organisms are not necessarily *M. tuberculosis*. Molecular results accelerate decisions but do not replace culture and susceptibility evaluation. **Exclude active disease before latent-infection therapy.** [CDC clinical and laboratory evaluation](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/testing-diagnosis/clinical-and-laboratory-diagnosis.html)

### Choose a TB regimen with the TB program, not from duration alone

For latent infection, CDC favors shorter rifamycin-based courses when suitable. The selection depends on presumed susceptibility, age, pregnancy, interactions, and feasibility. Rifamycins can substantially alter ART, anticoagulants, hormonal contraception, and other therapies. An isoniazid alternative may be appropriate when interactions prevent a short course. Assess adherence and toxicity at least monthly; liver-risk history, HIV, pregnancy/postpartum status, and abnormal baseline results determine laboratory monitoring.

| Latent-infection option | Schedule and important boundary |
| --- | --- |
| 3HP | Once-weekly isoniazid plus rifapentine for three months; age at least two, compatible ART if HIV; not pregnancy/planned pregnancy during treatment |
| 4R | Daily rifampin for four months; useful in suitable HIV-negative patients across ages, but major interactions can preclude it |
| 3HR | Daily isoniazid plus rifampin for three months; selected ages/HIV regimens with interaction review |
| 6H or 9H | Daily isoniazid for six or nine months when short rifamycin regimens are unsuitable; completion and hepatotoxicity require attention |

NTCA/CDC favors suitable short rifamycin regimens. [NTCA/CDC latent-infection guideline](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/rr/rr6901a1.htm)

Symptoms of hepatitis require stopping medication and prompt assessment. CDC generally withholds therapy at aminotransferases over three times the upper limit with symptoms or over five times without symptoms. These are toxicity-management thresholds, not reasons to ignore earlier symptoms. [CDC latent-TB regimens and monitoring](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/treatment/latent-tuberculosis-infection.html)

Active drug-susceptible pulmonary TB requires multidrug therapy. Current options include a selected **four-month rifapentine-moxifloxacin regimen** for eligible patients, not an improvised substitution into an older course. Eligibility includes age at least 12, weight at least 40 kg, appropriate susceptibility and interactions, and, if HIV is present, CD4 at least 100 with compatible ART. A **six-month 2HRZE/4HR** regimen remains an option: two months of isoniazid/rifampin/pyrazinamide/ethambutol followed by four months of isoniazid/rifampin. Site, resistance, cavitation, culture response, and special populations can change duration. Public-health case management and observed treatment support completion. [CDC current drug-susceptible TB care](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/treatment/tuberculosis-disease.html)

Children with a recent infectious contact can need **window prophylaxis despite a negative initial test**, after symptoms and imaging have excluded disease. Repeat testing at eight to ten weeks guides the next decision; very young age or immune compromise can alter stopping criteria. Selected non-severe pediatric disease has a distinct four-month regimen, which is not the adolescent/adult moxifloxacin course. [CDC pediatric TB](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/clinical-care/children.html)

Pregnancy does not justify delaying treatment of active TB. For latent infection, many pregnant patients can defer until two to three months postpartum, but recent close contact or substantial progression risk argues against delay. Avoid 3HP during pregnancy or anticipated pregnancy during the course. Selected alternatives include 4R, 3HR, or isoniazid with pyridoxine; choose with TB/obstetric expertise. Active-disease regimens need separate pregnancy review, including the individualized role of pyrazinamide. [CDC TB in pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/clinical-care/pregnancy.html)

**Clinical Case.** Before TNF-inhibitor therapy, a BCG-vaccinated adult has positive IGRA and six weeks of cough/weight loss. Investigate active TB with precautions and public-health coordination before four months of presumed-latent rifampin. BCG does not explain IGRA positivity; negative IGRA would not dismiss these symptoms.

### Mononucleosis: interpret the timing and protect the spleen

EBV causes fever, pharyngitis, fatigue, posterior cervical nodes, splenomegaly, atypical lymphocytes, and aminotransferase elevation. CMV, acute HIV, toxoplasmosis, hepatitis, and GAS can overlap; use exposure, medicines, and systemic findings. Reassess persistent/atypical illness rather than attributing it to remote EBV antibodies. [CDC mononucleosis overview](https://www.cdc.gov/epstein-barr/about/mononucleosis.html)

Heterophile testing has false negatives, particularly in children, and does not specifically prove EBV. CDC does not recommend Monospot for general use. When clarification matters, EBV-specific antibodies are more informative: **VCA IgM with absent EBNA** supports primary infection; VCA IgG plus EBNA generally reflects past infection. A high IgG result alone does not date the illness. Interpret the panel with symptom timing and the laboratory method. If acute HIV remains plausible, use the acute-infection pathway in 09B rather than allowing a negative heterophile result to end the workup. [CDC EBV antibody interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/epstein-barr/php/laboratories/index.html)

Uncomplicated EBV care is supportive; routine antivirals or corticosteroids are not indicated. Airway compromise, severe cytopenias, dehydration, or suspected splenic rupture changes disposition. Splenic enlargement creates a rupture risk that may persist after the throat improves. Return to activity must consider symptom onset, clinical recovery, and the risk of impact or straining, not simply a negative test or the date antibiotics ended. [AMSSM 2023 position statement abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37186809/)

As a practical initial restriction, avoid athletic exercise during the first three weeks of symptoms; once afebrile and clinically well, consider gradual light, noncontact activity with clinician review. This is **not automatic clearance for collision sport at three weeks**. Contact activity and heavy exertion require individualized discussion. Routine ultrasound is not a universal clearance test. New left upper-quadrant pain, shoulder pain, syncope, or circulatory symptoms requires urgent assessment. An aminopenicillin-associated eruption during mono should be documented and evaluated rather than automatically recorded as lifelong anaphylactic penicillin allergy. [AMSSM practical activity guidance](https://www.sportsmedtoday.com/mononucleosis-va-94.htm)

### Fever with rash: assess contagion and complications before naming the eruption

Assess morphology, distribution, mucosa, palms/soles, and rash-fever timing, vaccination, medicines, pregnancy, contacts, travel, and immunity. Toxic petechiae, meningism, shock, skin pain, blistering, or mucosal detachment requires urgent assessment. Isolate suspected airborne infection before entry to a crowded waiting room.

| Pattern or concern | Primary-care action |
| --- | --- |
| Fever, cough/coryza/conjunctivitis, descending rash, possible measles exposure | Isolate immediately with airborne precautions, notify public health, and arrange coordinated respiratory PCR/serum testing |
| Vesicles in successive crops, possible breakthrough varicella | Use appropriate precautions and lesion PCR when confirmation is needed; immune status and complications guide treatment |
| Parvovirus-compatible rash or adult arthralgia | Assess pregnancy and chronic hemolytic anemia; mild appearance does not exclude fetal or marrow risk |
| Fever with purpura or circulatory/neurologic deterioration | Emergency sepsis/meningitis evaluation; do not wait for a complete rash pattern |

Measles can transmit before the rash. Report suspected cases without waiting for confirmation; coordinate specimen collection and exposed-contact assessment. Routine care is supportive, with supervised age-appropriate vitamin A for children according to the clinical pathway, not unsupervised high-dose supplements or routine antibiotics. Immune suppression can alter presentation and isolation duration. [CDC measles care and diagnosis](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

For susceptible measles contacts, **MMR within 72 hours of initial exposure** can prevent or modify disease when age and immune/pregnancy status permit. **Immune globulin within six days** is the alternative exposure pathway, prioritized for infants, susceptible pregnant patients, and severe immune suppression. Do not give live MMR during pregnancy or severe immune suppression, or combine vaccine and IG without checking their interference and spacing. Public health determines contact eligibility, exclusions, and follow-up; routine vaccine scheduling in 01E does not replace this urgent decision. [CDC measles exposure prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/measles-rubeola.html)

VZV **lesion PCR** is preferred for laboratory confirmation; Tzanck smears lack adequate sensitivity and specificity. Sample an appropriate lesion rather than assume blood antibody proves the cause of a new rash. [CDC VZV laboratory guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/chickenpox/php/laboratories/index.html)

Healthy children with uncomplicated typical varicella do not routinely require antivirals. Selected higher-risk patients may benefit from early oral therapy, ideally within 24 hours of rash onset; severe or disseminated disease needs intravenous treatment. Susceptible pregnant or substantially immunocompromised contacts need prompt exposure-specific assessment, potentially VariZIG within ten days when indicated; live vaccine is not an interchangeable substitute. See 08C and the vaccination chapter for related care. [CDC varicella special-population guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/chickenpox/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Parvovirus suppresses erythroid production. A patient with chronic hemolysis can develop a marked hemoglobin fall with a low reticulocyte response; immunodeficiency can permit persistent infection. A child with a late facial rash may already be past peak infectiousness, whereas aplastic crisis or chronic infection changes that assessment. Pregnant patients with a credible exposure or compatible symptoms need obstetric evaluation, serology, and appropriate fetal follow-up rather than reassurance from mild maternal symptoms. [CDC parvovirus clinical and transmission considerations](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/healthcare-personnel-epidemiology-control/parvovirus.html)

**Meningococcal disease changes both disposition and contact care.** Suspected meningitis or rapidly progressive fever with purpura/shock needs emergency treatment and public-health notification; do not wait for a lumbar puncture or a complete rash. Household members, childcare contacts, and people directly exposed to oral secretions need prompt chemoprophylaxis regardless of vaccination, not a screening throat swab first. Selected adult options are **rifampin 600 mg orally every 12 hours for two days**, **ceftriaxone 250 mg IM once**, or **ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally once only when local resistance permits**. Ceftriaxone is an option in pregnancy; rifampin and ciprofloxacin are not recommended for pregnant contacts in this CDC pathway. Rifampin has important contraceptive, anticoagulant, and antiseizure-drug interactions. Casual contact alone is not the same exposure, and an ill contact needs disease evaluation rather than prophylaxis alone. [CDC meningococcal treatment and contact regimens](https://www.cdc.gov/surv-manual/php/table-of-contents/chapter-8-meningococcal-disease.html)

### A febrile returned traveler needs a dated itinerary

Ask countries and regions visited, arrival/departure dates, rural and urban stays, mosquito/tick exposure, food/water, freshwater contact, animals, sex, medical care, and prophylaxis adherence. Include visits to friends and relatives. Incubation periods help prioritize testing, but common pneumonia, urinary infection, and noninfectious illness remain possible. Obtain targeted baseline studies and cultures when indicated. Severe illness, uncertainty, or unreliable follow-up lowers the admission threshold. Infection-control and reporting decisions accompany the diagnostic workup. [CDC Yellow Book returned-traveler framework](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/post-travel-evaluation/post-travel-evaluation-of-the-ill-traveler.html)

**Exclude malaria urgently** after fever and plausible endemic-area travel, even if prophylaxis was taken or the patient is afebrile at the visit. Arrange immediate laboratory evaluation; thick/thin smears identify parasites, species, and parasitemia, with rapid testing as an adjunct when available. If initial microscopy is negative but suspicion persists, repeat **every 12-24 hours for a total of three sets**. Do not defer testing to a routine appointment. Severe features need hospital treatment; species, acquisition region, pregnancy, weight, and resistance determine therapy. Relapsing-species treatment also requires G6PD and reproductive-safety assessment before selected liver-stage drugs. [CDC malaria diagnosis](https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)

Dengue can cause fever, pain, rash, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia. The dangerous phase can begin **as fever subsides**, when plasma leakage and shock develop. Warning signs such as severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, fluid accumulation, or altered behavior require hospital assessment. Use acetaminophen rather than aspirin/NSAIDs, encourage suitable oral hydration when stable, and arrange close reassessment. Do not give prophylactic platelets solely for a low count or assume every patient needs IV fluids; fluid management changes with perfusion, leakage, and recovery. Pregnancy, comorbidity, and access barriers can change disposition. [CDC dengue care, April 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/dengue/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

**Clinical Case.** After five days of fever/myalgia, a traveler's temperature normalizes on day six while vomiting, abdominal pain, tachycardia, and oliguria develop. Arrange hospital assessment for dengue warning signs and evaluate malaria/other itinerary-based causes. Defervescence does not establish recovery.

### Viral hepatitis: separate infection testing from exposure prevention

For jaundice, anorexia, nausea, dark urine, or unexplained aminotransferase elevation, consider drug injury, ischemia, obstruction, autoimmune disease, and infection. Assess bilirubin, synthesis, mentation, hydration, and severity. Encephalopathy/impaired synthesis suggests acute liver failure requiring urgent care; aminotransferase height alone does not measure liver reserve (05D).

HAV generally causes acute rather than chronic disease. Test IgM anti-HAV in the appropriate symptomatic or exposure context; total antibody alone does not establish acute infection. Support hydration and nutrition, review potentially hepatotoxic substances, report as required, and identify contacts. Severe illness or preexisting liver disease changes the level of care. [CDC hepatitis A testing](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html), [CDC hepatitis A clinical care](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)

For a susceptible contact exposed within **two weeks**, give a single-antigen hepatitis A vaccine promptly when age at least 12 months. Add **immune globulin 0.1 mL/kg intramuscularly** for immunocompromise or chronic liver disease; in adults older than 40, addition is based on risk assessment. Infants below 12 months or people with a vaccine contraindication receive IG instead. Give vaccine and IG at different sites and complete the vaccine series for durable protection. Do not use Twinrix as HAV PEP or confuse an infant pretravel vaccine exception with the postexposure pathway. IG affects timing of certain live vaccines. [CDC Yellow Book HAV exposure pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/hepatitis-a.html)

HBV screening uses **HBsAg, anti-HBs, and total anti-HBc**. Vaccine-induced immunity does not produce anti-HBc; natural infection can leave a core-antibody footprint even after surface antigen disappears. Isolated anti-HBc requires interpretation, not an automatic immune/susceptible label. HBsAg-positive patients need HBV DNA and liver-disease assessment with linkage to care. Prior resolved infection can reactivate during immune therapy. See 05D for current AASLD/IDSA treatment and reactivation decisions. [CDC HBV serologic interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)

After a blood exposure, cleanse the site, document timing and route, and assess **HBV, HCV, and HIV independently**. For HBV, obtain reliable vaccine/response records and source HBsAg as indicated. A documented immunocompetent vaccine responder after a complete series does not need HBV PEP merely because a later titer was not recently measured. A completed series without response documentation is a different situation. Nonoccupational and perinatal algorithms have distinct rules and must not be replaced by a health-care-worker table. [CDC HBV exposure framework](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/infection-control/index.html)

| Occupational HBV situation | Source positive or unknown | Source negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Documented responder after complete series | No HBV PEP | No HBV PEP |
| Complete series, response undocumented; current anti-HBs below 10 mIU/mL | HBIG once plus revaccination pathway | Additional vaccine/retesting pathway; no HBIG |
| Documented nonresponse after two complete series | HBIG twice, one month apart; no further vaccine series | No HBV PEP |
| Unvaccinated or incomplete series | HBIG once plus start/complete vaccine | Start/complete vaccine; no HBIG |

For response-undocumented clinicians with current anti-HBs at least 10 mIU/mL after a complete series, no PEP is needed regardless of source status. Give indicated protection promptly and coordinate infection follow-up and postvaccination testing; HBIG can interfere with the timing of anti-HBs measurement. [CDC occupational HBV decision table](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/infection-control/table-1.html)

When occupational HBIG is indicated, the standard adult dose is **0.06 mL/kg IM as soon as possible**, at a site separate from vaccine; effectiveness after seven days is unknown. At-risk exposed clinicians also need baseline total anti-HBc and approximately six-month HBsAg/total anti-HBc follow-up. Document vaccine response one to two months after the final vaccine dose, but defer anti-HBs testing until passive HBIG antibody is no longer detectable, generally six months after HBIG. Do not confuse the HBIG volume dose with rabies HRIG's IU/kg dose. [CDC occupational HBIG dose and timing](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr6210a1.htm) [Current CDC follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/infection-control/index.html)

HCV antibody with reflex RNA distinguishes exposure from current viremia; recent exposure may require RNA even before antibody develops. There is **no recommended HCV PEP**. For occupational exposure requiring follow-up, obtain baseline antibody with reflex RNA, preferably within 48 hours, and test the source. Perform exposed-person RNA at **three to six weeks** and, if negative, final antibody with reflex RNA at **four to six months**. Use RNA instead of repeat antibody when antibody was already positive at baseline. Symptoms prompt earlier reassessment. Detectable RNA warrants treatment linkage, not waiting indefinitely for chronic disease. HIV PEP timing and follow-up are covered separately in 09B. [CDC HCV occupational follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-c/hcp/infection-control/index.html)

### Recurrent infection: document the pattern before naming an immune defect

Recurrent infections may reflect exposure, a persistent focus, structural disease, failed source control, or acquired/inborn immune dysfunction. Document organisms, sites, severity, response, and well intervals. Assess family history, growth, diarrhea, thrush, unusual pathogens, abscesses, autoimmunity, lymphoproliferation, medicines, HIV, malignancy, nutrition, and protein loss. Adult onset does not exclude inherited disease. Evaluate the pattern and secondary causes rather than infection count alone. [AAAAI/ACAAI current inborn-errors-of-immunity parameter](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/2025-Inborn-errors-of-immunity-pp.pdf)

Start with a CBC/differential and targeted assessment of the clinical problem. Quantitative IgG/IgA/IgM, antigen-specific antibody responses, and lymphocyte phenotyping evaluate different aspects of defense; normal total immunoglobulins do not establish normal antibody function. Interpret age-adjusted values and effects of illness or treatment. Specialist-directed complement or phagocyte-function testing follows the pattern rather than an indiscriminate panel. Recurrent infection in one location also deserves structural assessment. Persistent concern despite screening results merits immunology referral, not repeated empiric antibiotics without an explanation.

**Recognizable patterns guide the next test.** Recurrent bacterial sinopulmonary infections with giardiasis, autoimmune cytopenias, or low immunoglobulins warrant assessment for antibody deficiency, including common variable immunodeficiency; one low IgG result alone is not diagnostic. Repeated invasive **Neisseria** infection or a family history of meningococcal disease suggests a terminal or alternative complement defect. Initial functional testing uses **CH50 and AH50**, with careful specimen handling and specialist interpretation rather than a C3/C4 level alone. [AAAAI/ACAAI antibody and complement evaluation](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/2025-Inborn-errors-of-immunity-pp.pdf)

Cellular/humoral defects can coexist; treatment, dose, timing, and disease determine risk. Non-live vaccines are generally safe but may be less effective. Live-vaccine eligibility depends on defect and suppression, not a blanket ban for patients/household contacts. Coordinate timing, prophylaxis, and replacement therapy with specialists using vaccine-specific schedules (01E). [CDC immune assessment and vaccine principles](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-best-practices/altered-immunocompetence.html)

**Clinical Case.** A 38-year-old has recurrent confirmed pneumonia, diarrhea, and immune thrombocytopenia despite a normal leukocyte count. Review immunosuppression/protein loss, test HIV, obtain differential/immunoglobulins, assess structural lung disease, and refer for functional testing. One mildly low IgG does not justify replacement; treat unstable infection first.

Confirm recovery, results, contact prevention, and recurrence risks. Asplenia, severe immune suppression, and neutropenia require urgent-fever plans (09A); appearance or wound size may underestimate danger.
