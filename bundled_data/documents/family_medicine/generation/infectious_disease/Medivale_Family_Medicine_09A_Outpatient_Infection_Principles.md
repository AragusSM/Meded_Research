# Medivale Family Medicine

## 09A. Outpatient Infection Principles

First establish severity, likelihood of infection, site, and whether antimicrobials will help. Select coverage that reaches the site and fits the host. Even an appropriate drug can fail without abscess drainage, relief of obstruction, or access and absorption.

The **2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC)** guides adult sepsis; pediatric and obstetric pathways are separate. **CDC outpatient stewardship** and AHRQ guide routine prescribing, while syndrome-specific recommendations determine regimens. See 09B for STI/HIV, 09C for systemic/exposure infections, 10A-10C for ENT/eyes/allergy, and chapters 3, 6, and 8 for respiratory, urinary, skin, and wound sources. [Current outpatient stewardship framework](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/core-elements/outpatient-antibiotic-stewardship.html)

### Assess severity before naming the organism

Fever is a regulated temperature rise with infectious and noninfectious causes. Establish measurement method, maximum, duration, antipyretics, and trajectory. Ask about focal symptoms, travel, contacts, immunization, animal/tick and sexual exposures, procedures, devices, new medicines, and antibiotics. Compare baseline function and immunity; weakness may signal serious infection, while fever alone does not establish a bacterial cause.

Record vital signs, saturation, mental state, hydration, and perfusion. Seek focal sources and organ dysfunction: confusion, distress, hypotension, oliguria, mottling, or rapid functional decline. Compare baseline and obtain collateral history. Do not attribute altered behavior to age, disability, anxiety, or language barriers without assessment. [Recognizing possible sepsis in the community](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG253/chapter/could-this-be-sepsis)

**Sepsis is infection-associated, life-threatening organ dysfunction**, not simply fever plus a fast pulse. Bacterial, viral, fungal, or other infections can trigger it. The host response can impair vascular tone, endothelial integrity, coagulation, and cellular function; organ injury may continue even when the superficial source appears small. Septic shock is the particularly dangerous circulatory/metabolic subset. Formal definitions help communication, but outpatient clinicians must act on evolving danger rather than wait for every research criterion or a completed organ-failure score. [Sepsis definitions and organ dysfunction](https://www.sccm.org/clinical-resources/sepsis-definitions)

The 2026 SSC advises against **qSOFA alone** as the preferred hospital screening approach. No single biomarker excludes sepsis. Repeated assessment and the clinical course matter; antimicrobial urgency depends on likelihood and shock. [SSC adult recognition and timing recommendations](https://www.sccm.org/survivingsepsiscampaign/guidelines-and-resources/surviving-sepsis-campaign-adult-guidelines)

| Adult clinical situation | Antimicrobial timing in the SSC framework | Meaning in primary care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possible, probable, or definite septic shock | Immediately, ideally within 1 hour of recognition | Emergency response and transfer; do not await routine results |
| Probable or definite sepsis without shock | Immediately, ideally within 1 hour | Urgent treatment and hospital assessment |
| Possible sepsis without shock | Rapid, time-limited investigation; within 3 hours if concern persists | Active monitored assessment, not unattended delay |
| Low likelihood of infection, without shock | May defer while closely monitoring | Reassess the diagnosis and trajectory |

These are urgency targets, not permission to wait until a clock expires. Arrange emergency transport when indicated and communicate the suspected source, organ dysfunction, allergies, recent antimicrobials, and interventions. Obtain cultures promptly when feasible without compromising timely care. Initial stabilization, appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and repeated assessment proceed together. A clinic without monitoring or resuscitation capability should not keep an unstable patient for a complete outpatient workup. [CDC immediate evaluation and treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/sepsis/hcp/clinical-care/)

Adult resuscitation generally favors balanced intravenous crystalloid, with frequent reassessment of perfusion and overload. Persistent shock needs monitored vasopressor care. These principles support emergency recognition and disposition, not an office substitute for an ICU protocol. [SSC initial resuscitation recommendations](https://www.sccm.org/survivingsepsiscampaign/guidelines-and-resources/surviving-sepsis-campaign-adult-guidelines)

**Clinical Case.** After a procedure, an older adult develops weakness and abdominal pain. Temperature is 37.5 C after acetaminophen, respiratory rate 30/min, pressure 86/52 mm Hg, with confusion and scant urine. Activate emergency transfer for possible sepsis with organ dysfunction and begin available stabilization; absence of high fever is not reassuring.

### Age, pregnancy, and trajectory change the fever decision

Children require age-appropriate vital-sign interpretation and examination of interaction, breathing, hydration, and perfusion. An infant **younger than three months with a temperature >=38.0 C** belongs to a high-risk group and needs prompt assessment through a young-infant pathway. Do not apply a reassuring older-child viral diagnosis or a generic delayed prescription. Clinical improvement after an antipyretic is not a reliable test excluding serious illness. A distressed child with poor perfusion or reduced responsiveness needs emergency evaluation regardless of the measured temperature. [NICE young-child fever assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG143/chapter/recommendations)

The **2026 pediatric SSC pathway is distinct from the adult pathway**: suspected septic shock calls for antimicrobials as soon as possible, ideally within one hour; probable sepsis without shock allows rapid evaluation with treatment ideally within three hours when substantiated. Avoid transferring adult fluid targets into pediatric care. Pediatric fluid decisions depend on shock, response to each bolus, overload signs, and the availability of intensive care. These distinctions are reasons to use an age-specific resuscitation system, not to delay treatment while debating terminology. [Current pediatric sepsis guidance](https://www.sccm.org/clinical-resources/guidelines/guidelines/surviving-sepsis-campaign-international-guidelines-for-the-management-of-sepsis-and-septic-shock-in)

Pregnancy and the postpartum period also need a separate approach. Suspected infection with otherwise unexplained organ dysfunction can represent **maternal sepsis even without fever**. Consider urinary, respiratory, wound, and uterine sources, but do not assume every postpartum illness is obstetric. Escalate urgently and coordinate obstetric and acute-care teams; source control may be critical. Physiologic pregnancy changes and a single screening tool should not replace judgment. [SMFM maternal sepsis recommendations](https://s3.amazonaws.com/cdn.smfm.org/attachments/1171/dece53eb9e95e50c7658a8a137ae1a66.pdf)

In a stable patient, use symptoms and examination to select testing rather than ordering every available panel. Persistent or recurrent fever deserves a fresh history, medication review, repeated examination, and targeted investigation for infection, inflammatory disease, malignancy, and other causes. Several days of undifferentiated fever is not automatically formal fever of unknown origin. Indiscriminate repeated antibiotic courses can obscure the diagnosis; unstable, neutropenic, or significantly immunocompromised patients are important exceptions to a watchful diagnostic approach. [Prolonged fever and diagnostic reassessment](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2022/0200/p137)

### Make the infection diagnosis worth treating

Order tests to answer a clinical question. Interpret organism detection against the syndrome, sampling site/quality, prior treatment, contamination, and colonization. A positive result does not necessarily establish causation; multiplex detection of several organisms does not automatically require several drugs.

**Asymptomatic bacteriuria** illustrates this distinction. In most nonpregnant adults, bacteria in urine without attributable urinary symptoms do not require treatment. An older adult's delirium or fall alone does not prove UTI when there are no local urinary symptoms or systemic signs suggesting infection; evaluate other causes. Pregnancy and selected endourologic procedures involving mucosal trauma are important exceptions. Conversely, hemodynamic instability with an unclear source needs urgent evaluation and treatment, not rigid application of an asymptomatic-bacteriuria rule. [IDSA asymptomatic bacteriuria recommendations](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/asymptomatic-bacteriuria/)

Catheters commonly develop colonization/biofilm. Cloudy urine or catheter presence alone does not justify culture. Use appropriate indications and collection technique, interpreting results with symptoms and trajectory to avoid treating colonization while missing the illness's cause. [CDC urinary catheter culture stewardship](https://www.cdc.gov/uti/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Cultures do not remove the need for **source control**. Drainage of an abscess, relief of infected obstruction, removal or management of infected material, and appropriate surgery may determine recovery. The intervention depends on the anatomy and patient stability; an office incision is not suitable for every deep collection. Infected diabetic-foot tissue, a chronically colonized leg ulcer, and a closed-space infection need different sampling and referral decisions. See 06A-06B and 08C-08D for those pathways. [Source control and tissue-directed skin infection care](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/skin-and-soft-tissue-infections/)

### Choose an antimicrobial for organism, site, and host

Empiric selection considers syndrome, severity, host, and exposure; subsequent results guide refinement. The **2026 IDSA AMR guidance** highlights microbiology from the previous twelve months, antibiotics in the previous three months, and local resistance. These inputs do not automatically require maximum-spectrum therapy. [Current IDSA resistance guidance](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/amr-guidance/)

An **antibiogram** summarizes susceptibility among local sampled isolates. Check population, specimens, period, and setting; ICU data may not represent community infection. Prior resistant isolates may outweigh population averages. Use organism-specific breakpoints and laboratory interpretation, not raw MIC comparisons across drugs. [Antimicrobial selection and local susceptibility patterns](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/bacteria-and-antibacterial-medications/overview-of-antibacterial-medications)

Resistance belongs to organisms, not the patient's body. Antibiotics select resistant pathogens and flora; mechanisms spread between microbes and include inactivating enzymes, altered targets, reduced entry, and efflux. Stewardship combines indicated effective therapy, reduced unnecessary exposure, and transmission prevention; it must not delay treatment of severe illness. [CDC antimicrobial resistance overview](https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/about/)

**Urinary concentration does not establish tissue or bloodstream exposure.** Nitrofurantoin is inappropriate for upper-tract or bloodstream infection. Oral fosfomycin is not initial cUTI therapy or bloodstream treatment; specialist-directed transition after IV improvement in selected ESBL-producing E. coli cUTI, when other oral options are unavailable, is a limited exception. Do not generalize it to an unstable untreated patient. [Site-specific treatment limitations and exceptions](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/amr-guidance/)

Fever, flank pain, or vomiting during treatment of presumed cystitis requires site/severity reassessment despite reported susceptibility. See **06A** for tissue-directed regimens and the obstruction pathway.

Beta-lactams inhibit cell-wall synthesis; other classes target protein synthesis, nucleic-acid processes, or folate metabolism. Efficacy depends on susceptibility and exposure, absorption, intervals, distribution, host defenses, and source control, not simply bactericidal/bacteriostatic classification. Oral therapy can suit stable patients; vomiting, malabsorption, or critical illness may require IV treatment. [Antibacterial pharmacology and route selection](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/bacteria-and-antibacterial-medications/overview-of-antibacterial-medications)

Kidney impairment may require a different dose or interval, while evolving acute kidney injury makes a single static estimate unreliable. Check the method and units relevant to the product and clinical context: laboratory eGFR indexed to body surface area is not automatically identical to an absolute clearance used in a dosing instruction. Very small or large body size and low muscle mass can complicate interpretation. Involve a pharmacist when the estimate is near a dosing boundary or the drug has a narrow safety margin. [NIDDK drug dosing and kidney-function estimates](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/research-funding/research-programs/kidney-clinical-research-epidemiology/laboratory/ckd-drug-dosing-providers)

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, age, weight, and hepatic disease also affect selection. Use the current indication-specific source and exact formulation; a recommendation for an otherwise healthy adult is not automatically a pediatric or obstetric prescription. Check the entire medication list, including nonprescription products, and establish how the patient will take the drug. If a liquid is needed, specify both concentration and administered volume. Confirm the prescribed quantity and duration, and explain relevant food or separation instructions rather than assuming a patient can infer them from a drug name.

### Useful drug distinctions without a universal prescription table

Drug roles depend on syndrome, susceptibility, formulation, and host; this table highlights distinctions rather than complete spectra or regimens.

| Drug or group | Useful distinction | Prescribing consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional oral beta-lactams | Activity varies across agents and organisms | Do not assume every agent covers MRSA or every gram-negative pathogen |
| TMP-SMX | Can be useful for selected susceptible infections | Review kidney function, potassium, interactions, and severe-reaction history |
| Nitrofurantoin | Bladder-focused role | Do not select it for suspected upper-tract or systemic infection |
| Azithromycin | Resistance and QT risk can limit a convenient regimen | A short pack is not a generic treatment for cough |
| Fluoroquinolones | Selected indications with important class toxicities | Use only when benefit and alternatives justify the risk |

### Allergy assessment starts with the actual reaction

Establish the drug, indication, timing, symptoms, severity, treatment, and subsequent tolerance. Obtain records when possible. Diarrhea, family history, a vague childhood label, and anaphylaxis imply different risks. Document the reaction, not just the word allergy. [CDC penicillin-allergy history and risk assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-signs/)

| Reported history | Main concern | Appropriate direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nausea or diarrhea without allergic features | Intolerance rather than immune allergy | Clarify the record and discuss tolerability |
| Remote mild isolated skin eruption | May be low risk after assessment | Consider a validated evaluation pathway |
| Rapid hives with airway, circulation, or multisystem symptoms | Possible immediate hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis | Urgent reaction care; risk-based specialist evaluation |
| Blistering, mucosal injury, fever with organ injury, or cytopenia | Severe delayed hypersensitivity | Avoid routine challenge; specialist assessment |

**Delabeling is an evaluated clinical process**, not an instruction to retry leftover amoxicillin at home. Selected low-risk patients may undergo a supervised oral challenge under a standard protocol with observation and rescue treatment available. Anaphylaxis or a recent IgE-compatible reaction generally changes the testing pathway. Severe delayed reactions such as SJS/TEN, DRESS, or drug-associated organ injury are not candidates for a routine direct challenge or ordinary penicillin skin-testing pathway. See 08C for severe drug eruptions. [CDC testing pathways and severe-reaction exclusions](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-signs/)

The AAAAI/ACAAI practice parameter supports proactive evaluation and distinguishes histories inconsistent with allergy from low-risk cutaneous histories and anaphylaxis. Beta-lactam cross-reactivity depends substantially on **structural side chains and the reaction phenotype**, not a blanket prohibition of all cephalosporins. A structurally dissimilar agent may be usable through the appropriate pathway, but severe delayed cutaneous or organ reactions require separate specialist decisions. Allergy testing should not delay emergency treatment for sepsis; select an appropriate urgent regimen while clarifying the history. [Drug allergy practice parameter](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Drug-Allergy-2022.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** Penicillins have been avoided since age six, when the only recalled reaction was nausea without rash, swelling, dyspnea, or emergency treatment. Clarify the record through the practice's evaluation process. Persistent uncertainty warrants supervised assessment, not an inaccurate anaphylaxis label or an unsupervised home trial.

Record the tested drug/dose, date, response, and delayed-symptom advice; communicate results to the patient, chart, pharmacy, and treating teams. Distinguish allergy, intolerance, and unresolved history. Tolerance of another drug does not erase a genuine severe reaction.

### Adverse effects and interactions are part of the treatment decision

New airway swelling, collapse, or rapidly progressive multisystem symptoms requires emergency anaphylaxis care and intramuscular epinephrine when indicated. Painful rash, mucosal injury, blistering, or systemic organ injury may indicate severe delayed hypersensitivity. Do not dismiss these as ordinary adjustment to treatment or re-prescribe the culprit. [Serious immediate and delayed medication reactions](https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Drug-Allergy-2022.pdf)

**TMP-SMX** can cause hyperkalemia, especially with kidney dysfunction or other potassium-raising treatment. It can enhance warfarin's anticoagulant effect, and concurrent methotrexate is a major toxicity concern. Check whether a safer active alternative exists and arrange appropriate renal, potassium, blood-count, or INR monitoring according to risk and treatment. A normal pretreatment value does not eliminate an interaction that develops after several doses. Product-specific age, pregnancy, breastfeeding, renal, and allergy restrictions still apply. [TMP-SMX prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=f59d0c04-9c66-4d53-a0e1-cb55570deb62)

**Clinical Case.** A patient with CKD taking an ACE inhibitor and spironolactone is prescribed TMP-SMX. Reassess the indication and active alternatives because of additive potassium risk. If needed, coordinate medication changes and monitoring. New weakness, palpitations, or systemic symptoms requires prompt assessment.

Fluoroquinolones can cause disabling tendon, peripheral nerve, and central nervous system adverse effects and can worsen myasthenia gravis. Selected uncomplicated infections should receive them only when appropriate alternatives are unavailable. Ciprofloxacin also has important interactions, including a contraindication with tizanidine and reduced absorption with multivalent cations; check the actual product's separation instructions. Steroid exposure, age, kidney function, and other comorbidities affect the risk-benefit assessment. A previous successful course is not proof that a new course is the best choice. [Ciprofloxacin prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=888dc7f9-ad9c-4c00-8d50-8ddfd9bd27c0)

Azithromycin can prolong QT and rarely cause torsades or fatal arrhythmia. Check QT, bradycardia, potassium/magnesium depletion, and interacting drugs. Another macrolide or fluoroquinolone may share this risk; choose an indicated active agent by the patient's overall risk-benefit balance. [FDA azithromycin cardiac safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/files/drugs/published/FDA-Drug-Safety-Communication--Azithromycin-%28Zithromax-or-Zmax%29-and-the-risk-of-potentially-fatal-heart-rhythms-%28view-and-print-version%29.pdf)

New significant diarrhea during or after antibiotic exposure raises concern for **C. difficile**, but not every loose stool is CDI. In an adult with **at least three unformed stools in 24 hours** without another explanation such as laxatives, send an appropriate diarrheal-stool test and institute indicated infection precautions. Assess hydration, abdominal pain, fever, and severity; colonization can yield a positive result without disease, and routine testing to prove cure is not useful after recovery. Review whether the inciting antibiotic is still needed. Severe abdominal pain, distension, hypotension, or systemic decline requires urgent care rather than antidiarrheal treatment alone. See **05B** for CDI treatment and the fulminant-disease branch. [CDC CDI clinical assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/c-diff/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

### Stewardship is active treatment planning

AHRQ's ambulatory framework asks whether antibiotics are needed, which tests help, which narrow effective regimen is safest, and whether the patient understands recovery/follow-up. Record indication, duration, and review instructions. When withholding antibiotics, provide symptom relief and a route to reassessment. [AHRQ ambulatory antibiotic decisions](https://www.ahrq.gov/antibiotic-use/ambulatory-care/four-moments/index.html)

For a compatible uncomplicated viral respiratory illness, routine antibiotics do not improve recovery. Colored sputum alone does not establish bacterial bronchitis, and the assessment should consider pneumonia and other alternatives when findings warrant it. Discuss the expected course and symptomatic options appropriate to the person's age and comorbidities. A second visit for persistent cough is an opportunity to reassess, not automatic evidence of an untreated bacterial infection. [CDC adult outpatient respiratory prescribing](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/adult-outpatient.html)

**Observation and delayed prescribing are selected strategies**, not a compromise for every uncertain infection. Specify which symptoms or time course should trigger starting the medicine, which findings instead require examination, and how follow-up will occur. Confirm access to both the prescription and reassessment. Do not use this approach for suspected sepsis, a seriously ill young infant, likely deep infection, or a high-risk host who needs urgent evaluation. The diagnosis and its severity criteria determine whether observation is suitable. [CDC active monitoring and delayed-prescribing implementation](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/media/pdfs/core-elements-outpatient-508.pdf)

**Clinical Case.** A four-year-old has one day of mild unilateral otalgia, mild membrane bulging, no otorrhea or severe symptoms, and normal activity/intake. Confirm observation eligibility, provide analgesia, and ensure access to treatment if worsening or no improvement over two to three days. Postauricular swelling, marked illness, or neurologic symptoms requires examination rather than merely filling the prescription. [CDC selected pediatric otitis observation](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/pediatric-outpatient.html), [AAP observation follow-up](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/131/3/e964/30912)

Use a diagnosis-specific effective duration, not ten days for every infection or an unsupervised stop when symptoms improve. Resistance alone does not automatically require a longer course; host factors, source control, and response matter. Explain any revised stop date and remove superseded prescriptions. [Duration and refinement of resistant-infection therapy](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/amr-guidance/)

At reassessment, check diagnosis, site activity, access/adherence, adverse effects, and source control. Persistent symptoms can reflect expected resolution, resistance, malabsorption, complications, or a mimic. Resolve these questions before adding drugs; assign responsibility for cultures and document action on important results.

At the practice level, CDC's updated framework includes leadership commitment, accountability, expertise, action, tracking, reporting, and education. Measure prescribing quality as well as volume, and include balancing outcomes such as return visits or hospital admissions. A low prescribing rate alone does not prove safe care. Peer feedback, diagnostic support, allergy evaluation, and feasible communication systems help make appropriate decisions consistent across in-person, urgent-care, and telemedicine visits. [Current CDC program implementation guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/core-elements/outpatient-antibiotic-stewardship.html)

### The immunocompromised host needs a different safety margin

Specify the immune problem rather than writing only immunocompromised: neutropenia after chemotherapy, impaired cellular immunity, B-cell depletion, transplant therapy, functional or anatomic asplenia, and prolonged systemic immunosuppression create different risks. Ask about the actual agent, dose, last treatment, recent rejection therapy, prophylaxis, prior opportunistic disease, vaccination, and exposure. Inflammatory signs may be attenuated, and a normal-looking skin site or absent high fever does not reliably exclude serious disease. Involve the treating oncology, transplant, or infectious-disease team early when the illness exceeds a routine localized infection.

CDC's vaccine framework illustrates why precision matters: systemic steroid dose and duration affect live-vaccine safety, whereas inhaled or topical treatment is not automatically equivalent to prolonged high-dose systemic treatment. Do not turn a vaccine-specific dose threshold into a universal rule declaring everyone below it safe from infection. Prevention plans require the current vaccine and treatment-specific guidance, and live vaccines may be contraindicated in significant immune suppression. See 01E for the primary vaccination discussion. [CDC altered-immunocompetence guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-best-practices/altered-immunocompetence.html)

**Febrile neutropenia is an emergency.** The ASCO/IDSA adult cancer framework defines fever as a single oral temperature >=38.3 C or >=38.0 C sustained for one hour. These criteria are not a reason to wait when a patient is unwell. [Current ASCO/IDSA fever definition](https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/outpatient-management-of-fever-and-neutropenia.pdf)

An ANC <500 cells/microliter, or <1,000 with a predicted fall below 500 within 48 hours, signals particularly dangerous neutropenia. Current eviQ guidance uses >=38.0 C as its presentation trigger; follow the treating center's emergency plan rather than waiting for a higher reading or prolonged fever. Afebrile deterioration can also signal infection. Arrange urgent assessment and prompt treatment; do not delay transfer for an outpatient differential count. [Current cancer-care recognition and escalation](https://www.eviq.org.au/clinical-resources/oncological-emergencies/123-neutropenic-fever-patient-evaluation-risk-as)

**Clinical Case.** Eight days after chemotherapy, a patient reports chills and oral temperature 38.4 C without cough or a visible skin infection. Arrange immediate oncology/emergency assessment despite an earlier reassuring count; current neutropenia may blunt local inflammatory findings.

**Example: ANC.** WBC 1,200 cells/microliter, neutrophils 20%, and bands 5% give ANC = 1,200 x (20 + 5)/100 = **300 cells/microliter**, indicating severe neutropenia. Use neutrophil/band fractions or the laboratory ANC, not lymphocytes, total WBC alone, or a prior count.

Outpatient treatment of febrile neutropenia is a **specialist-selected pathway**, not routine management based on a reassuring appearance. ASCO/IDSA recommends initial empirical antibacterial therapy within one hour of triage and observation for at least four hours before discharge for appropriate candidates. Risk assessment combines clinical judgment, a validated tool when applicable, comorbidities, expected neutropenia, oral tolerance, caregiver and transport support, and access to rapid review. High-risk or unstable patients need hospital treatment. Previous fluoroquinolone prophylaxis and local resistance can affect oral regimen eligibility; do not prescribe a memorized outpatient combination without checking those conditions. [ASCO/IDSA outpatient eligibility and monitoring](https://www.idsociety.org/globalassets/idsa/practice-guidelines/outpatient-management-of-fever-and-neutropenia.pdf)

Asplenia creates lifelong susceptibility to rapidly progressive infection, particularly from encapsulated bacteria. Vaccination and prophylaxis reduce risk but do not eliminate it. Fever or systemic illness needs immediate assessment and treatment under the patient's emergency plan; a prescribed rescue antibiotic must not become a reason to remain at home instead of seeking care. Maintain medical-alert information and individualized prevention, and use current U.S. vaccine guidance rather than importing another country's schedule. The 2024 BSH guideline supports rapid treatment, education, and risk-based prevention; its exact prophylaxis schedules are not universal U.S. rules. [Current asplenia infection-prevention guidance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjh.19361)

In transplant or advanced cellular immune suppression, persistent fever, hypoxemia, progressive headache, unusual lesions, or prolonged gastrointestinal symptoms may require testing beyond ordinary community pathogens. Check prophylaxis adherence without assuming prophylaxis excludes breakthrough disease. Coordinate any change in immunosuppressants with the prescribing team; abruptly stopping them can cause serious harm. Antimicrobials may also interact with the underlying regimen. Broaden the differential and expedite evaluation when appropriate rather than simply adding an antibiotic with a wider bacterial spectrum. [Immune-status and exposure-specific assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travelers-with-additional-considerations/immunocompromised-travelers.html)

### Close the loop after the visit

Explain diagnosis, uncertainty, expected recovery, and urgent symptoms such as inability to drink/take medicine, dyspnea, confusion, or severe rash. Identify responsibility for tests and after-hours contact. Address pharmacy/cost, work/caregiving, and language barriers.

Explain dosing and advise against sharing, saving, or borrowing antibiotics. Important adverse effects require reassessment, not self-substitution. Clinician-directed changes differ from unplanned nonadherence. Review hygiene, vaccination, wound/device care, and transmission precautions. [CDC appropriate antibiotic use and patient counseling](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/about/)

Revisit diagnosis and treatment as results and clinical status change, balancing prompt care of dangerous disease with avoidance of unnecessary antimicrobials.
