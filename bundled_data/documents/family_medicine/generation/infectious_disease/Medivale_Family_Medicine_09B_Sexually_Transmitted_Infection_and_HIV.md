# Medivale Family Medicine

## 09B. Sexually Transmitted Infection and HIV

Distinguish screening, symptomatic diagnosis, contact management, and urgent exposure prevention. Infection may be asymptomatic, involve several sites, or follow a recent negative test. Explain the sampled sites and timing limitations rather than promising that one panel excludes every infection.

CDC guides STI care, NIH guides HIV/OIs, and CDC nPEP and U.S. Public Health Service occupational PEP remain separate pathways. The adult/adolescent regimens below require adjustment for pregnancy, renal function, allergy, interactions, site, severity, and formulation; they are not neonatal or small-child prescriptions. [CDC current STI framework](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/default.htm)

### Obtain a useful history without making assumptions

Provide privacy, permission, and clear confidentiality limits. Ask about partners, practices, protection, prior infections, pregnancy intentions, contact timing/sites, injection equipment, PrEP/PEP, partner diagnoses, and treatment. Use preferred terms and anatomy rather than inferring exposures from identity or relationship status. [CDC sexual-history framework](https://www.cdc.gov/sti/hcp/clinical-guidance/taking-a-sexual-history.html)

Explain how exposure site determines sampling and timing determines PEP eligibility. Assess coercion/safety without delaying urgent care for a detailed account; offer a chaperone and explain examination steps. Apply adolescent consent, reporting, billing, and portal-confidentiality rules. Possible acquired STI in a child requires specialist assessment, not adult prescribing or assumed transmission.

Screening schedules belong to the patient's anatomy, exposures, age, pregnancy, and local context. CDC recommends annual chlamydia/gonorrhea screening for sexually active women younger than 25 and older women with increased risk. For men who have sex with men, screening includes indicated exposure sites at least annually, with three-to-six-month testing when ongoing risk warrants it. Syphilis and HIV assessment accompany appropriate STI care. Symptoms require diagnostic evaluation regardless of whether a screening interval has elapsed. See 01D for the broader prevention framework. [CDC population- and site-specific screening](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/screening-recommendations.htm)

### Test the exposed compartment

Use validated site-specific NAATs for chlamydia/gonorrhea; a specimen evaluates only its sampled compartment. Offer validated self-collection when available.

| Exposure or clinical question | Useful specimen approach | Limitation to explain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urethral infection | First-catch urine or an appropriate urethral specimen | A routine bacterial urine culture is not a substitute |
| Vaginal/cervical infection | Vaginal NAAT swab, including validated self-collection | A normal urinalysis does not exclude cervicitis |
| Receptive anal exposure or proctitis | Rectal testing for indicated organisms | Negative genital testing does not clear the rectum |
| Oral exposure | Pharyngeal gonorrhea testing when indicated | Routine throat chlamydia screening is not generally recommended |

Treat throat chlamydia detected during pharyngeal gonorrhea testing despite no routine throat-chlamydia screening recommendation. HIV/syphilis blood tests do not replace indicated lesion or site specimens. [CDC extragenital testing principles](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/chlamydia.htm)

**Clinical Case.** A gonorrhea contact has a negative urine NAAT but reports oral exposure without throat testing. Obtain indicated site-specific testing and assess contact-treatment timing; absent dysuria and negative urine testing do not clear the pharynx.

Pelvic pain with cervical-motion, uterine, or adnexal tenderness can justify presumptive PID treatment; negative cervical NAAT does not exclude upper-tract disease. Exclude ectopic pregnancy when relevant. Fever, severe illness, abscess concern, or inability to tolerate treatment can require admission. PID therapy is broader/longer than cervicitis treatment (14B). [CDC low-threshold PID assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/pid.htm)

Acute scrotal pain requires consideration of torsion even when there is a possible STI exposure. Proctitis can cause rectal pain, discharge, bleeding, or tenesmus and needs an exposure-specific examination and testing plan. Arthritis with pustular lesions or tenosynovitis raises concern for disseminated gonorrhea rather than a single uncomplicated infection; see 07A. Do not let a familiar positive screening result explain away a time-critical surgical or systemic problem.

### Treat uncomplicated infection and arrange the next test

Treatment, cure testing, and reinfection testing are distinct. These uncomplicated regimens do not cover PID, dissemination, meningitis, endocarditis, or neonatal disease.

| Diagnosis and population | Selected regimen | Important boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uncomplicated chlamydia, nonpregnant adult/adolescent | Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days | Alternatives require attention to adherence and lower rectal efficacy of azithromycin |
| Chlamydia during pregnancy | Azithromycin 1 g orally once | Pregnancy-specific test of cure is required |
| Uncomplicated gonorrhea, weight <150 kg | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once | Add appropriate chlamydia therapy if not excluded |
| Uncomplicated gonorrhea, weight >=150 kg | Ceftriaxone 1 g IM once | Site and complications still determine follow-up |

For chlamydia, avoid sex until the seven-day regimen is complete, symptoms resolve, and partners are treated; wait seven days after single-dose therapy. Retest at three months. Routine cure testing is unnecessary in nonpregnant patients unless adherence, symptoms, or reinfection raises concern. Pregnancy requires NAAT test of cure about four weeks after treatment and three-month retesting. Earlier NAAT can detect nonviable material. [CDC chlamydia follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/chlamydia.htm)

For gonorrhea, abstain for seven days after treatment and until partners are treated and symptoms resolve. Retest at three months. Pharyngeal infection requires test of cure at **7-14 days**; early NAAT positivity can reflect residual material. Routine cure testing is not required for appropriately treated uncomplicated genital/rectal infection. Persistent symptoms or suspected failure requires culture and susceptibility testing, not only another NAAT; involve STI experts/public health. Assess reinfection before assuming resistance. Pregnancy requires pregnancy-appropriate chlamydia treatment when indicated. [CDC gonorrhea regimen and site-specific follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-adults.htm)

FDA-approved oral **zoliflodacin** treats uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea from age >=12 years and weight >=35 kg; **gepotidacin** has that indication from age >=12 years and weight >=45 kg with limited/no alternatives. Neither approval establishes pharyngeal, rectal, or disseminated treatment. Ceftriaxone remains the main CDC regimen. Check current labeling, pregnancy, interactions, and follow-up before using newer agents. [FDA indication and safety boundaries](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-two-oral-therapies-treat-gonorrhea)

### Treat the transmission network, not just the positive result

For chlamydia/gonorrhea, arrange evaluation and presumptive treatment of partners from the preceding **60 days**; include the most recent partner even if contact was earlier. Discuss safe notification, STI testing, and prevention. [CDC partner-management principles](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/chlamydia.htm)

Where lawful, expedited partner therapy helps when prompt examination is unlikely. It cannot replace evaluation of pelvic pain, pregnancy concerns, severe allergy, or other complications. Provide written precautions and explain that a partner's oral regimen may differ from the index patient's injection. Use shared decision-making when EPT could miss HIV diagnosis/prevention opportunities. [CDC EPT implementation](https://www.cdc.gov/sti/hcp/clinical-guidance/expedited-partner-therapy.html)

For gonorrhea EPT in a suitable adult partner unable to obtain timely care, CDC uses **cefixime 800 mg orally once**. Add doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for seven days if chlamydia has not been excluded; possible pregnancy requires a pregnancy-appropriate alternative and clinical assessment. This access strategy does not make cefixime equivalent to ceftriaxone for pharyngeal disease. Provide written allergy, complication, and follow-up instructions. [CDC EPT regimen](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/gonorrhea-adults.htm)

**Clinical Case.** After treatment, a patient resumes sex before the partner is treated and tests positive three months later. Assess exposure, adherence, symptoms, and partner access. Reinfection does not establish antibiotic failure; arrange care for both partners before reflexive escalation.

Persistent urethritis or recurrent cervicitis warrants reassessment for re-exposure, adherence, and alternative organisms. Test for **Mycoplasma genitalium** in indicated recurrent disease, not as universal asymptomatic screening. It lacks a cell wall, so beta-lactams do not treat it. Resistance-guided sequential therapy is preferred when available; a lone 1-g azithromycin dose is not appropriate. Complicated disease and treatment failure merit specialist input. [CDC M. genitalium evaluation and resistance-directed care](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/mycoplasmagenitalium.htm)

When an FDA-cleared NAAT detects uncomplicated M. genitalium and resistance testing is unavailable, the CDC adult sequence is **doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for seven days, then moxifloxacin 400 mg orally daily for seven days**. With documented macrolide susceptibility, the second stage can instead be azithromycin 1 g once followed by 500 mg daily for three more days. Review QT-prolonging drugs, fluoroquinolone toxicities, pregnancy, and hepatic disease before prescribing; contraindication or failure needs expert selection. M. genitalium-associated PID uses a different duration. Routine cure testing is unnecessary after a recommended regimen in an asymptomatic patient, but persistent symptoms need reassessment. [CDC sequential regimens and follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/mycoplasmagenitalium.htm)

Trichomoniasis may be asymptomatic or cause discharge, irritation, or urethritis. A negative wet mount does not exclude it; use sensitive testing when indicated. CDC recommends oral metronidazole **500 mg twice daily for seven days for women**, versus **2 g once for men**. Treat current partners, avoid sex until treatment is complete and symptoms resolve, and retest sexually active women at about three months. Do not assume vaginal metronidazole gel treats infection throughout the relevant compartments. Pregnancy, recurrence, and drug intolerance need their specific pathway; see the later vaginitis discussion. [CDC trichomoniasis care](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/trichomoniasis.htm)

### Syphilis: determine stage before choosing duration

Treponema pallidum produces systemic infection with several clinical phases. Primary disease often causes a painless chancre, but lesions can be atypical or painful. Secondary disease can cause generalized rash, mucosal lesions, lymphadenopathy, or patchy hair loss. An asymptomatic positive patient may have latent infection, not necessarily late disease. Neurologic, ocular, or auditory involvement can occur at **any stage**. [CDC clinical stages](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/syphilis.htm)

Early latent infection requires evidence supporting acquisition within the preceding year, such as documented seroconversion, compatible recent symptoms, an infected partner, or a clearly restricted exposure interval. A high RPR titer alone cannot establish early latent disease. Without such evidence, classify an asymptomatic untreated patient as late latent or unknown duration and examine for overlooked active lesions. [CDC latent-stage assignment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/latent-syphilis.htm)

Serology uses **treponemal and nontreponemal tests together**. A traditional sequence begins with RPR/VDRL and confirms reactivity with a treponemal assay. A reverse sequence starts with a treponemal immunoassay, then obtains quantitative RPR/VDRL; discordance needs a different treponemal assay, commonly TP-PA. Interpret the sequence with prior treatment and recent exposure. Treponemal antibodies often persist after successful treatment, so they are not a useful cure marker. Use the same nontreponemal method, preferably the same laboratory, to assess change. A compatible illness with initially negative testing can require repeat or direct testing; ask the laboratory about a prozone when suspicion is high. [CDC laboratory interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/rr7301a1.htm)

**Clinical Case.** A reactive treponemal screen, negative RPR, and reactive TP-PA occur in an asymptomatic patient recalling remote injections without records. Retrieve treatment documentation and assess re-exposure. Negative RPR does not prove cure; undocumented adequate treatment without evidence of recent acquisition follows the unknown-duration pathway. [CDC reverse-sequence management](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/syphilis.htm)

For adults without neurologic/ocular/otic involvement, the stage determines the selected penicillin course. Confirm the **benzathine penicillin G** formulation; a similarly named benzathine-procaine combination is not interchangeable. Allergy alternatives require a nonpregnant-patient pathway and reliable follow-up, not casual substitution.

| Stage or manifestation | Selected adult treatment | Decision that matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary or secondary syphilis | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM once | HIV alone does not require extra doses |
| Early latent syphilis | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM once | Recent acquisition must be supported |
| Late latent or unknown duration | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM weekly for 3 doses | Total 7.2 million units; track each visit |
| Neurosyphilis, ocular or otic disease | Aqueous crystalline penicillin G IV for 10-14 days | A routine benzathine injection is inadequate |

Primary/secondary disease ordinarily has clinical and quantitative serologic follow-up at **6 and 12 months**; latent disease at **6, 12, and 24 months**. HIV and pregnancy can require different follow-up schedules. Persistent/recurrent symptoms or a sustained fourfold titer rise suggests reinfection or failure and requires reassessment. Some patients have a slower decline or persistently low serofast titers; lack of immediate seronegativity does not alone prove failure. [CDC early treatment and follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/p-and-s-syphilis.htm), [latent treatment and monitoring](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/latent-syphilis.htm)

**Worked titer example.** An RPR change from 1:64 to 1:16 is a fourfold decrease: 64/16 = **4**, corresponding to two serial dilution steps. A later change from 1:16 to 1:64 is a fourfold rise. A shift from 1:32 to 1:16 is only twofold. Interpret the direction with stage, elapsed time, symptoms, exposures, and the pretreatment value; do not compare an RPR number directly with a VDRL number or a treponemal assay's index.

Visual change, cranial nerve findings, altered cognition, meningitic symptoms, or hearing change needs urgent manifestation-specific evaluation. Neurologic findings usually require CSF assessment. Confirmed isolated ocular disease does not require an abnormal CSF result before treatment; isolated otic disease with a normal neurologic examination does not routinely require lumbar puncture. Coordinate ophthalmology/ENT and infectious-disease care. The recommended adult IV neurosyphilis regimen supplies **18-24 million units/day**, divided every four hours or continuously, for 10-14 days. [CDC neurosyphilis, ocular and otic pathways](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/neurosyphilis.htm)

During pregnancy, penicillin is the proven treatment for preventing congenital syphilis; a genuine allergy requires specialist evaluation/desensitization and penicillin treatment. For early disease, CDC permits an additional benzathine dose one week later. In a three-dose pregnancy course, intervals **longer than nine days require restarting**. Coordinate fetal assessment when indicated without delaying therapy. Fever or myalgia after treatment can be a Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, not penicillin allergy; contractions or reduced fetal movement needs obstetric attention. [CDC pregnancy treatment and safety](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/syphilis-pregnancy.htm)

**ACOG recommends universal first-visit, third-trimester, and birth syphilis screening**, broader than CDC's risk-based repeat wording. Follow state/reporting requirements. Recent contacts may need presumptive treatment before seroconversion. [ACOG prenatal screening](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-advisory/articles/2024/04/screening-for-syphilis-in-pregnancy), [CDC syphilis partner care](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/syphilis.htm)

For sexual contact **within 90 days before a partner's primary, secondary, or early-latent syphilis diagnosis**, give presumptive early-syphilis treatment even with negative serology. For contact more than 90 days earlier, presumptive treatment is indicated when results are not immediately available and follow-up is uncertain; otherwise use the examination and tests. The notification look-back is separate: primary disease uses three months plus symptom duration, secondary six months plus symptom duration, and early latent one year. [CDC syphilis contact decisions](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/syphilis.htm)

### Genital ulcers and herpes require more than a visual label

Painful clustered vesicles suggest HSV; an ulcer that is classically painless suggests syphilis. Neither appearance is definitive, and infections can coexist. Consider trauma, aphthae, inflammatory disease, drug reactions, malignancy, and exposure-specific infections such as mpox, LGV, chancroid, or donovanosis. Persistent, unusual, or destructive lesions may need biopsy or specialist assessment. Obtain indicated syphilis, HSV, and HIV testing and consider presumptive treatment when the clinical/public-health risk of delay is important. [CDC ulcer evaluation](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/genital-ulcers.htm)

Sample an active lesion for **type-specific HSV NAAT**, or culture when appropriate testing is unavailable. A negative healing-lesion sample does not exclude infection. HSV IgM is not recommended; selected type-specific serology requires interpretation and confirmation of low-positive results. HSV-1 antibody cannot locate infection to mouth versus genitals. For a first genital episode, one adult option is **valacyclovir 1 g orally twice daily for 7-10 days**, extending if incompletely healed. Renal impairment changes dosing. [CDC HSV diagnosis and first-episode therapy](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/herpes.htm)

HSV persists in sensory ganglia; antivirals do not eradicate latency. Discuss episodic versus suppressive therapy, asymptomatic shedding, and avoiding contact during lesions/prodrome. Condoms and suppression reduce, not eliminate, transmission. Genital HSV-1 usually recurs less than HSV-2. New late-pregnancy infection, labor-time lesions/prodrome, and neonatal exposure require dedicated obstetric/pediatric care. [CDC herpes counseling and pregnancy considerations](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/herpes.htm)

For recurrent genital HSV-2 in a suitable immunocompetent nonpregnant adult, an episodic option is **valacyclovir 500 mg orally twice daily for three days**, started during prodrome or within one day of lesions. Suppression can use 500 mg or 1 g orally daily; 500 mg daily may be less effective with at least ten recurrences yearly. Revisit the need annually. These are not HIV-specific or pregnancy-suppression doses; renal impairment requires adjustment. [CDC recurrent HSV regimens](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/herpes.htm)

Prevention also includes indicated HPV/hepatitis vaccination and barrier protection. HPV vaccination prevents new infection; it does not treat an existing wart or remove the need for indicated cancer screening. Do not call every genital bump HPV. Inspect atypical, pigmented, ulcerated, indurated, or nonresponding lesions and consider biopsy. See 01E and the later reproductive/anal-health discussions for vaccination, screening, and lesion-specific management. [CDC anogenital-wart assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/anogenital-warts.htm)

**Doxy PEP is not HIV PEP.** CDC recommends discussing it with MSM and transgender women who had gonorrhea, chlamydia, or syphilis in the preceding twelve months. After shared decision-making, the regimen is doxycycline **200 mg within 72 hours after sex, no more than 200 mg in 24 hours**. Include baseline and three-to-six-month site-specific STI testing and reassessment. It is not treatment of an established STI or an automatically generalizable prevention regimen for every population. Discuss photosensitivity, esophageal irritation, interactions, resistance uncertainty, and pregnancy considerations. [CDC bacterial-STI doxy PEP guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/rr7302a1.htm)

### Recognize HIV before advanced immunodeficiency

HIV establishes persistent infection affecting CD4 helper T cells, with replication and immune injury during asymptomatic periods. Acute infection can mimic influenza/mono: fever, pharyngitis, rash, lymphadenopathy, ulcers, or GI symptoms. Recent exposure and compatible illness warrant testing even after a negative antibody result; appearance or identity does not determine eligibility. [Acute HIV recognition](https://www.hivguidelines.org/guideline/hiv-acute/)

The laboratory diagnostic sequence starts with a **HIV-1/2 antigen-antibody assay**, followed after a reactive result by an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay. A negative or indeterminate differentiation result needs diagnostic NAT to resolve possible acute infection. If the initial test is negative but acute HIV is suspected, obtain HIV RNA rather than simply waiting for an annual screen. A baseline negative test soon after exposure cannot exclude infection from that exposure. PrEP/PEP can alter the evolution of test results; resolve ambiguity promptly with expert help rather than continuing inadequate prevention-only treatment. [CDC HIV diagnostic approach](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/hiv.htm), [CDC/APHL diagnostic NAT update](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/129018)

**Clinical Case.** Fever, mouth ulcers, rash, and fatigue develop three weeks after sexual exposure despite a negative home antibody test. Obtain laboratory antigen-antibody testing and HIV RNA, assess alternatives, and arrange precautions/follow-up. Early antibody testing leaves acute HIV unresolved.

Confirm diagnosis and prior antiretroviral exposure; obtain HIV RNA, CD4, genotype, CBC, renal/liver studies, urinalysis, hepatitis and indicated STI/TB/OI tests. Assess pregnancy/interactions. **Viral load measures replication; CD4 measures immune risk.** Most results need not delay suitable rapid ART, but require prompt review, adjustment, and comprehensive follow-up. [NIH September 2026 baseline evaluation](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-arv/baseline-evaluation)

ART is recommended for all people with HIV, generally at diagnosis or as soon as feasible, not only after a CD4 threshold or AIDS illness. Suspected cryptococcal or TB meningitis changes timing because immediate immune recovery can cause harmful inflammation. Evaluate serious neurologic symptoms and coordinate OI-specific treatment rather than treating rapid start as an inflexible rule. After ART begins, new inflammation may be immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, but resistance, toxicity, and a new infection remain alternatives. [NIH ART initiation and OI exceptions](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-arv/initiation-antiretroviral-therapy)

NIH's September 2026 initial-treatment framework generally favors second-generation integrase-based therapy. Common options include bictegravir/TAF/FTC or dolutegravir plus a tenofovir/FTC-or-3TC backbone. Here, FTC is emtricitabine, 3TC is lamivudine, and TDF/TAF are tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/alafenamide. Selected dolutegravir/lamivudine treatment requires documented absence of chronic HBV and relevant resistance; baseline viral load alone is not a current exclusion. It is not a reflex rapid-start choice while those prerequisites are unknown. Prior long-acting cabotegravir PrEP raises integrase-resistance concerns and changes initial selection pending genotype. HIV/HBV coinfection needs an HBV-active backbone, and all choices require interaction, kidney, pregnancy, and resistance review. [NIH current initial-regimen selection](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-arv/what-start-initial-combination-regimens)

### Opportunistic clues and preventive treatment

Adult **AIDS is established by CD4 <200 cells/microliter or an AIDS-defining condition**, including selected cancers; both are not required. ART is also indicated above 200. [NIH AIDS definition](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/glossary/acquired-immunodeficiency-syndrome-aids)

Progressive dyspnea, new hypoxemia, persistent headache, focal neurologic findings, visual change, dysphagia, prolonged diarrhea, wasting, or unusual skin lesions should prompt evaluation for advanced disease and other causes. Oral candidiasis and esophageal candidiasis are not interchangeable findings. CD4-associated patterns guide suspicion, but they do not prove a pathogen or replace urgent organ-specific assessment.

Pneumocystis pneumonia often causes subacute dry cough, fever, dyspnea, and hypoxemia. Bilateral interstitial or ground-glass changes support the differential but are **not pathognomonic**; obtain appropriate respiratory testing. Severe illness needs hospital care and treatment without waiting for every confirmation. TMP-SMX is preferred, with renal/toxicity review; adjunctive corticosteroids are indicated for room-air **PaO2 <70 mm Hg or A-a gradient >=35 mm Hg**. Do not confuse the short uncomplicated-STI courses above with the **21-day** HIV-associated PCP course. [NIH current Pneumocystis guidance](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-opportunistic-infections/pneumocystis?view=full)

Primary prophylaxis prevents a first OI; active treatment and secondary prevention use different regimens. Apply condition-specific start/stop/restart criteria.

| Infection | When primary prophylaxis is relevant | Important exception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pneumocystis | CD4 <200 cells/microliter when not on ART or starting ART | For those already on ART, incorporate viral suppression and restart criteria |
| Toxoplasma encephalitis | Positive Toxoplasma IgG and CD4 <100 | A positive IgG alone does not diagnose encephalitis |
| Disseminated MAC | CD4 <50 and no ART, persistent viremia, or no suppressive option | Not routinely needed when effective ART is started immediately |

TMP-SMX can prevent both PCP and toxoplasmosis in appropriately selected patients; not every PCP alternative protects against toxoplasmosis. Check G6PD status before dapsone/primaquine-containing alternatives. Neurologic disease with enhancing brain lesions requires a differential including toxoplasmosis, lymphoma, and other infections, not automatic treatment from a scan label. [NIH toxoplasmosis prevention and evaluation](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-opportunistic-infections/toxoplasmosis)

One preferred PCP prophylaxis regimen is **TMP-SMX 160/800 mg orally once daily**; a daily 80/400-mg tablet is another NIH option. Check renal function, potassium, interacting medicines, and severe-reaction history. For patients already on ART, start/restart prophylaxis at CD4 below 100 regardless of HIV RNA, or at CD4 100-200 with sustained detectable viremia. The usual stopping criterion is recovery to at least 200 for at least three months on ART; selected suppressed patients at 100-200 need individualized criteria. Prophylaxis is not a treatment dose. Active PCP usually warrants ART within two weeks when feasible, unlike the CNS-OI timing exceptions above. [NIH PCP prevention and ART timing](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-opportunistic-infections/pneumocystis)

Do not start MAC prophylaxis simply because a count is below 50 while ignoring the ART plan. Fever, weight loss, anemia, or systemic symptoms may instead require evaluation for active disseminated disease. Exclude active MAC when indicated before single-agent prophylaxis, which could select resistance. [NIH May 2026 MAC guidance](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-opportunistic-infections/disseminated)

Longitudinal HIV care covers virologic response, adherence/toxicity, mental health, substances, access, cardiovascular/metabolic and renal health, cancer, vaccines, and dental/sexual care. Use condition-specific schedules; avoid live intranasal influenza vaccine. Significant immune suppression alters live-vaccine eligibility but does not prohibit all patient/household vaccination. See 01E and 09C. [HIVMA/IDSA primary-care framework](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/primary-care-management-of-people-with-hiv/)

**Undetectable equals untransmittable:** sustained ART suppression prevents sexual HIV transmission. It does not cure HIV, prevent STIs, or establish zero injection-equipment/breastfeeding risk. Continue viral-load monitoring and dedicated pregnancy/infant-feeding counseling; do not assume undocumented suppression. [CDC treatment-as-prevention evidence](https://www.cdc.gov/hivpartners/php/hiv-treatment/index.html)

### Match PrEP to exposure, physiology, and a feasible schedule

Offer PrEP without requiring a stigmatizing risk label. It protects people without HIV, not established infection or a recent exposure needing PEP. Confirm regimen-appropriate blood testing, assess acute symptoms, and review HBV, kidney function, and indicated STI tests. [CDC PrEP clinical assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/hivnexus/hcp/prep/index.html)

The following labeled adult/adolescent regimens require weight at least 35 kg and HIV exclusion; loading and missed-dose schedules are product-specific.

| PrEP approach | Practical role | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily oral FTC/TDF | FTC 200 mg/TDF 300 mg once daily; sexual and injection-related prevention in CDC guidance | Renal assessment; prevention labeling does not recommend use below CrCl 60 mL/min |
| Daily oral FTC/TAF | FTC 200 mg/TAF 25 mg once daily; selected sexual-exposure prevention | Not indicated for receptive vaginal exposure; routine eligibility includes CrCl >=30 mL/min |
| Long-acting cabotegravir IM | 600 mg gluteal IM at initiation, one month later, then every two months | Sexual-exposure prevention; HIV testing with each injection; optional oral lead-in is separate |
| Long-acting lenacapavir SC | Day 1: 927 mg SC as two injections plus 600 mg orally; day 2: 600 mg orally; then 927 mg SC every 26 weeks | Sexual-exposure prevention; continuation window +/-2 weeks, with product-specific testing and missed-dose plan |

The FTC/TDF renal boundary applies to prevention, not a general renal-dosing rule for all HIV treatment. Use Cockcroft-Gault estimated clearance for this pathway, not an unexamined substitution of indexed laboratory eGFR. The manufacturer label does not recommend PrEP below 60 mL/min; CDC's summary has inconsistent >60 versus >=60 wording. Recheck a borderline or changing value. [Current manufacturer FTC/TDF label](https://www.gilead.com/-/media/files/pdfs/medicines/hiv/truvada/truvada_pi.pdf) [CDC PrEP assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/hivnexus/hcp/prep/index.html)

FTC/TAF's receptive-vaginal exclusion is an indication limit, not a reason to offer no prevention. Stopping tenofovir-containing PrEP in someone with chronic HBV can cause hepatitis flare; arrange continued HBV management when switching or stopping. For ordinary daily oral PrEP follow-up, reassess HIV at least every three months, adherence, exposure sites, and renal monitoring needs. Event-driven 2-1-1 dosing is not CDC's recommended universal alternative to daily treatment. [CDC oral PrEP follow-up](https://www.cdc.gov/hivnexus/hcp/prep/index.html), [current FTC/TAF label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=06f66e98-e6ee-4538-9506-6c1282cc14c1)

Daily oral PrEP reaches maximal protective drug levels after about **seven days for receptive anal sex**, and up to **21 days for receptive vaginal sex or injection exposure**; insertive-sex timing is not established. Use additional protection during initiation and assess a recent exposure for PEP. Check clearance at least annually for all oral users and at least every six months from age 50 or with baseline clearance below 90 mL/min, more often with additional renal risks. [CDC onset and monitoring](https://www.cdc.gov/hivnexus/hcp/prep/index.html)

Cabotegravir's label includes adults and adolescents weighing >=35 kg. Obtain required HIV testing before initiation and every injection; a negative antigen-antibody test needs RNA confirmation as specified in the label. Establish an alternative-prevention and testing plan for missed injections or discontinuation because drug concentrations decline slowly and resistance can emerge. An optional oral tolerability lead-in is not the same as coverage for every missed-injection scenario. [Current cabotegravir PrEP labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4338428e-43d4-4e02-ac9d-bd98e738a7da)

CDC recommends six-monthly **lenacapavir** PrEP at weight >=35 kg. Assess acute HIV/interactions and continue follow-up/STI prevention. Switching from tenofovir requires an HBV plan. [CDC lenacapavir recommendation](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7435a1.htm)

The current lenacapavir prevention label requires HIV testing before initiation and each injection; baseline negative antigen-antibody results require RNA confirmation, which may return after administration. Its initiation includes oral doses, not an injection alone. Use the current product-specific instructions for interaction-related supplemental dosing, delayed injections, bridging, and restart; do not borrow cabotegravir's rules. Discuss injection-site nodules and the long pharmacologic tail. Pregnancy and breastfeeding decisions require current individualized guidance rather than assuming all PrEP products have identical evidence. [Current lenacapavir prevention label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=1c241af1-ce62-4b0a-9eb7-f6b626174f01)

**Clinical Case.** A patient with chronic HBV wants injectable PrEP because rotating shifts impair daily adherence. Coordinate injectable eligibility/testing with continued HBV-active treatment and liver follow-up before stopping tenofovir.

### Act promptly after a possible HIV exposure

Clarify time, fluid, route, source HIV/suppression information, barriers, and prior PrEP use. Contact with intact skin or nonbloody saliva is not equivalent to a percutaneous blood exposure or condomless receptive sex. Source status may be unknown; assess substantial risk case by case without waiting for a source test when PEP is indicated. Start **as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours**, for a **28-day** course. Draw baseline tests but do not delay the first dose for laboratory results. Presentation after 72 hours still requires testing, counseling, and an ongoing-prevention plan, with expert discussion when circumstances are unusual. [CDC current PEP urgency and assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/hivnexus/hcp/pep/index.html)

Preferred nPEP combines **bictegravir/FTC/TAF** or **dolutegravir plus tenofovir and FTC-or-3TC**. Match formulation/dose to age, renal function, pregnancy, interactions, prior prophylaxis, and resistance. Ensure the full course; neither two-drug ART nor one long-acting PrEP injection substitutes for PEP. [CDC 2025 nPEP regimen framework](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/rr/rr7401a1.htm)

For a suitable adult with adequate renal function, concrete 28-day oral examples are **bictegravir/FTC/TAF 50/200/25 mg once daily**, or **dolutegravir 50 mg once daily plus FTC/TDF 200/300 mg once daily**. The ordinary BIC combination requires estimated CrCl at least 30 mL/min; the daily FTC/TDF PEP dose requires at least 50, not the 60-mL/min PrEP threshold. Severe renal disease/dialysis and hepatic disease need an expert-selected regimen. Check interactions before discharge: polyvalent-cation antacids can impair integrase-inhibitor absorption, and dofetilide or rifampin can preclude an otherwise convenient choice. These are guideline-supported **off-label PEP** regimens, not FDA-approved PEP indications. [CDC nPEP dosing and organ-function tables](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/rr/rr7401a1.htm)

Baseline assessment includes HIV testing, renal/liver studies, HBV, pregnancy when relevant, and exposure-directed STI/HCV evaluation. Recent injectable PrEP exposure affects diagnostic testing; the corrected nPEP guidance calls for baseline diagnostic NAT in addition to antigen-antibody testing when injectable PrEP was used in the preceding **six months**. [CDC corrected testing recommendation](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7435a3.htm)

Arrange early contact at about **24 hours** to assess access and tolerability, then follow-up testing generally at **4-6 and 12 weeks after exposure**, using laboratory antigen-antibody testing plus diagnostic NAT. Selected adherent early starters may qualify for an interim-test exception; do not omit follow-up without checking the complete criteria. A negative baseline test cannot establish that PEP was unnecessary. Plan transition directly to PrEP when ongoing exposure is anticipated, with appropriate HIV testing and counseling about diagnostic uncertainty. [CDC nPEP follow-up and transition](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/rr/rr7401a1.htm)

Workplace exposure follows the **2025 U.S. Public Health Service occupational pathway**. Wash exposed skin, irrigate mucosa, report promptly, and obtain urgent assessment; do not test a discarded needle. Begin indicated PEP without waiting for source results. Reassess within 72 hours. With a documented undetectable source viral load, expert-supported shared decision-making addresses omitting or stopping PEP; sexual-transmission evidence does not establish universal zero needlestick risk. Final antigen-antibody/NAT testing is at twelve weeks. Interim testing at four-to-six weeks is indicated when PEP started more than 24 hours after exposure or doses were missed. Evaluate HBV/HCV separately. [Current occupational PEP recommendations](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/258795/cdc_258795_DS1.pdf)

The occupational guideline adds baseline NAT, with expert consultation, for cabotegravir PrEP in the preceding **twelve months**. Do not substitute nPEP's corrected six-month injectable-PrEP look-back for this occupational recommendation.

**Clinical Case.** A hollow-bore needlestick occurs after hours with source results unavailable until morning. Clean, report, assess, and start indicated PEP promptly. The exposure service can later refine/stop treatment. Arrange follow-up before discharge.

### Make follow-up part of the initial treatment

Specify result ownership, partner access, when sex can resume, and urgent symptoms. Confirm private contact and address cost, transport, childcare, and work barriers. Infection neither dates acquisition nor proves infidelity.
