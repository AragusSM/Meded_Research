# Medivale Family Medicine

## 05D. Liver Disease

### Separate injury, excretion, synthesis, and fibrosis

A liver panel does not measure one interchangeable property called liver function. ALT and AST mainly indicate cell injury; alkaline phosphatase (ALP) can suggest cholestasis; bilirubin reflects production, processing, and excretion. Albumin and PT/INR help assess synthesis but have important nonhepatic influences. Fibrosis is scarring and needs its own assessment. A normal ALT does not exclude substantial fibrosis, and a very high ALT does not by itself establish irreversible failure. Determine which process is abnormal before deciding what to investigate.

AST can come from muscle, while ALP can come from bone or placenta. GGT can support a hepatic source of elevated ALP but is not a specific diagnosis. Low albumin can accompany inflammation, renal protein loss, or poor nutrition; elevated INR can reflect anticoagulation or vitamin K deficiency. Confirm unexpected results and review the trajectory, medications, alcohol, viral exposures, metabolic risks, and relevant examination findings. [AASLD approach to liver chemistries](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/back-basics/how-approach-elevated-liver-enzymes)

The R ratio compares proportional elevations: R = (ALT / ALT upper limit of normal) / (ALP / ALP upper limit of normal). In the drug-injury framework, R at least 5 is hepatocellular, R at most 2 is cholestatic, and intermediate values are mixed. Use results obtained at the same relevant time; the pattern can evolve. This classifies injury rather than naming its cause. Bilirubin is not in the formula. [AASLD drug-injury classification](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9936988/)

**Pattern calculation.** ALT 320 U/L with an upper limit of 40 is eightfold elevated; ALP 240 U/L with an upper limit of 120 is twofold elevated. R is 8/2 = 4, a mixed pattern. Compare multiples of normal, not raw enzyme values. Symptoms, exposures, bilirubin, and synthetic function guide investigation of the cause.

| Main abnormality | Mechanistic question | Useful next direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Predominant aminotransferase elevation | Are hepatocytes injured? | Viral, metabolic, immune, drug, ischemic causes |
| Hepatic ALP elevation | Is bile formation or drainage impaired? | Ultrasound and targeted cholestatic evaluation |
| Isolated indirect bilirubin | Excess production or reduced conjugation? | Hemolysis assessment and benign inherited patterns |
| Direct bilirubin with other abnormalities | Excretory injury or obstruction? | Interpret enzymes, symptoms, imaging together |
| Rising INR or new confusion | Is acute organ failure developing? | Urgent hospital assessment |

Severity is a clinical judgment. New confusion, hypoglycemia, worsening coagulopathy, hypotension, severe pain, or systemic illness should not wait for a routine repeat panel. Acute liver failure in an adult generally involves recent liver injury with encephalopathy and INR at least 1.5 without preexisting cirrhosis; it requires urgent transplant-center discussion. Acute deterioration in established cirrhosis is a different syndrome but can be equally dangerous. Falling aminotransferases are not reassuring if mental status, INR, bilirubin, or circulation is worsening. [AASLD acute liver failure assessment](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/back-basics/defining-and-managing-acute-liver-failure)

### Interpret bilirubin without confusing it with urobilinogen

Heme breakdown produces unconjugated bilirubin, which travels bound to albumin and is not normally filtered into urine. Hepatic conjugation makes bilirubin more water-soluble for biliary excretion. Conjugated bilirubin can enter urine when it accumulates in blood; this helps explain dark urine in hepatobiliary disease. Fractionate the serum total into direct and indirect components. Direct bilirubin is a useful clinical approximation, not a reason to ignore the rest of the panel. [MedlinePlus urine bilirubin](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/bilirubin-in-urine/)

Urobilinogen is a different substance formed when intestinal bacteria act on bilirubin. Some returns through the circulation, and some is excreted in urine. Urinary urobilinogen can rise in hemolysis or liver disease and can be low with obstruction that prevents bile reaching the bowel. It is not simply conjugated bilirubin appearing under another name. Neither its presence nor absence alone assigns a jaundiced patient to one diagnostic category. [MedlinePlus urobilinogen interpretation](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/urobilinogen-in-urine/)

Indirect predominance suggests increased red-cell breakdown, impaired uptake, or reduced conjugation. If hemolysis is plausible, assess CBC, reticulocytes, smear, LDH, and haptoglobin in context. Gilbert syndrome causes reduced UGT1A1 activity and intermittent mild unconjugated elevation, often during illness, fasting, or dehydration, with otherwise reassuring assessment. Explain its benign course once competing causes are reasonably excluded; genetic testing and liver imaging are not automatic requirements for every typical presentation. Severe or atypical abnormalities, anemia, or other abnormal liver tests require a different evaluation. [MedlinePlus Gilbert syndrome](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/gilbert-syndrome/)

Direct hyperbilirubinemia can result from hepatocellular disease, sepsis, or impaired bile drainage. Ask about itch, pale stool, pain, fever, weight change, procedures, and medicines. Ultrasound is usually the initial structural study for a cholestatic presentation. Duct dilation, a concerning mass, or persistent suspicion may lead to MRCP, EUS, or therapeutic ERCP. A normal first ultrasound does not exclude every ductal disorder. Fever, jaundice, and upper abdominal pain raise concern for cholangitis; painless progressive jaundice with weight loss warrants prompt malignancy evaluation. Biliary emergencies are developed in 05A.

**Clinical case.** Weeks of pruritus and pale stools followed by rigors and upper abdominal pain requires urgent assessment for obstruction and infection, even with a recent supplement exposure. Stable isolated indirect hyperbilirubinemia with a reassuring hemolysis assessment instead follows a different pathway.

### Investigate a pattern rather than ordering one indiscriminate panel

Persistent unexplained abnormalities need etiologic assessment even when mild. Tailor viral testing, iron studies, ultrasound, immune testing, and selected inherited-disease investigations to the clinical setting. Marked acute aminotransferase elevation raises additional concern for ischemic injury, toxins, acute viral disease, or severe immune-mediated injury. Evaluate circulatory events and cardiac congestion; do not attribute every abnormality in a patient with obesity to metabolic disease. Muscle symptoms or recent strenuous exercise may justify a muscle-injury assessment rather than an exclusively hepatic explanation. [ACG abnormal liver chemistry evaluation](https://gi.org/guideline/evaluation-of-abnormal-liver-chemistries/)

Autoimmune hepatitis can occur across ages and may coexist with other autoimmune disease. Elevated IgG and compatible antibodies support suspicion, but no antibody alone establishes the diagnosis; specialist assessment commonly includes biopsy and exclusion of competing causes. Treatment is immunosuppressive and requires monitoring rather than empiric long-term steroids for an unexplained positive ANA. [NIDDK autoimmune hepatitis diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/autoimmune-hepatitis/diagnosis)

Hereditary hemochromatosis is an iron-overload disorder, not simply an elevated ferritin result. Transferrin saturation, ferritin, family history, and appropriate genetic assessment help distinguish overload from an inflammatory or metabolic ferritin rise. Confirm the diagnosis and organ involvement before arranging disease-specific iron removal. [NIDDK hemochromatosis assessment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/hemochromatosis/diagnosis)

Wilson disease should remain in the differential for unexplained liver disease, particularly with neurologic, psychiatric, hemolytic, or family findings. Ceruloplasmin can be low but is not sufficient alone; urine copper, ophthalmologic examination, genetic testing, and sometimes hepatic assessment contribute. Acute liver failure with hemolysis is an urgent specialist problem, not an opportunity for a prolonged outpatient trial. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency is another inherited consideration, especially with compatible lung or family disease. [NIDDK Wilson disease diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/wilson-disease/diagnosis)

### Distinguish immune-mediated bile-duct disease from a removable obstruction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) affects small intrahepatic bile ducts and often presents through cholestatic tests, fatigue, or itch before cirrhosis. Antimitochondrial antibodies and the clinical pattern support diagnosis after obstruction is excluded; uncertain or overlapping disease may require additional specialist testing. Ursodiol is first-line disease-modifying treatment. Assess biochemical response, symptoms, bone health, and progression; inadequate response requires a current specialist-directed treatment plan rather than assuming that the first prescription ends follow-up. [NIDDK PBC diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/primary-biliary-cholangitis/diagnosis), [NIDDK PBC treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/primary-biliary-cholangitis/treatment)

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) produces inflammatory stricturing of intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic ducts and is associated with IBD. MR cholangiography is important for duct assessment; biopsy has a role in selected cases, including suspected small-duct disease. A patient with colitis and persistent cholestasis needs assessment even if bowel symptoms are controlled. PSC requires specialist follow-up for strictures, infection, associated bowel disease, and malignancy risk. It is not managed as though every narrowing were a simple gallstone. [NIDDK PSC diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/primary-sclerosing-cholangitis/diagnosis)

### Viral hepatitis: identify the infection and close the care loop

Hepatitis A spreads primarily by the fecal-oral route and causes acute rather than chronic infection. Symptoms may include malaise, anorexia, nausea, abdominal discomfort, dark urine, and jaundice, while young children can be asymptomatic. Test for acute infection when clinically indicated rather than infer the virus from travel alone. Most illness receives supportive care, but poor intake, worsening synthesis, or altered mentation changes disposition. Coordinate reporting and contact assessment with public health. [CDC hepatitis A overview](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)

IgM anti-HAV supports recent infection in an appropriate symptomatic/exposure setting; total or IgG antibody alone reflects prior exposure or vaccination rather than proving acute disease. For susceptible contacts exposed within two weeks, give single-antigen vaccine promptly at age 12 months or older. Add immune globulin for immunocompromise or chronic liver disease; above age 40, addition depends on risk assessment. Infants younger than 12 months or people with a vaccine contraindication receive immune globulin instead. Do not use Twinrix for HAV postexposure prophylaxis. Section 09C provides the IG dose and exposure workflow. Do not wait for the entire vaccine series before providing time-sensitive protection. [CDC HAV testing](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html), [CDC HAV postexposure pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/hepatitis-a.html)

HBV is transmitted through infected blood and body fluids, including perinatal exposure. Infection acquired in infancy is much more likely to persist than infection acquired in adulthood. CDC adult screening uses HBsAg, anti-HBs, and total anti-HBc together. HBsAg identifies current infection; anti-HBs indicates immunity in the appropriate context; anti-HBc indicates natural exposure, not a vaccine response. Persistent HBsAg for at least six months supports chronic infection, but a single negative IgM does not establish the complete timeline. [CDC HBV screening framework](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/rr/rr7201a1.htm)

| HBV results | Usual interpretation | Important follow-through |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HBsAg negative; core negative; surface antibody positive | Vaccine-associated immunity | Interpret with vaccination history |
| HBsAg negative; core positive; surface antibody positive | Resolved natural infection | Reactivation remains possible with immune suppression |
| HBsAg positive; core positive; IgM core positive | Usually acute infection | IgM can also occur during a flare |
| HBsAg positive; core positive; IgM core negative | Current, often chronic infection | Establish duration, DNA, ALT and stage |
| All three screening tests negative | Susceptible if no documented vaccine protection | Offer indicated vaccination |
| HBsAg negative; core positive; surface antibody negative | Isolated core result | Several explanations; assess context, not one label |

HBeAg and anti-HBe add information but do not replace HBV DNA: HBeAg-negative infection can still replicate and cause injury. An isolated core result can reflect remote infection with waning surface antibody, false positivity, a window period, or occult infection. Clarify exposure, symptoms, immune status, and further testing. [CDC HBV serology interpretation](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)

Chronic HBV care integrates DNA, ALT trajectory, fibrosis/cirrhosis, family history, coinfection, and patient goals. AASLD/IDSA 2025 broadens shared treatment decisions in indeterminate disease and selected apparently immune-tolerant patients; normal ALT is not permission to discharge everyone from monitoring. High-barrier oral agents include entecavir, TDF, and TAF, selected for renal, bone, pregnancy, and other considerations. Viral suppression is not the same as eradication, and stopping treatment can provoke a flare. In HBeAg-negative noncirrhotic adults with sustained suppression, the guideline suggests continuing until HBsAg loss rather than routine withdrawal. [AASLD/IDSA chronic HBV update](https://doi.org/10.1097/hep.0000000000001549)

Immune-active noncirrhotic disease warrants treatment: the AASLD framework uses ALT at least twice its upper limit (35 U/L in men, 25 U/L in women) together with HBV DNA at least 20,000 IU/mL when HBeAg-positive or at least 2,000 IU/mL when HBeAg-negative. These are not exclusion thresholds: significant fibrosis/cirrhosis and indeterminate disease require their own treatment assessment. If apparently inactive HBeAg-negative disease is observed rather than treated, follow ALT and DNA every 3-6 months initially, then every 6-12 months after the first year, and reassess phase and fibrosis. [AASLD phase-specific implementation](https://www.aasld.org/sites/default/files/2025-11/CHB%20Educational%20Slide%20Set%20Final%202.pdf)

During pregnancy, that pathway recommends TDF or TAF at gestational week 28 when HBV DNA exceeds 200,000 IU/mL, with TDF having the longer safety record. Maternal treatment does not replace newborn vaccine and HBIG within 12 hours after birth or subsequent infant follow-up. Arrange maternal postpartum monitoring, particularly if prophylactic treatment is stopped. [AASLD HBV implementation recommendations](https://www.aasld.org/sites/default/files/2025-11/CHB%20Educational%20Slide%20Set%20Final%202.pdf), [CDC perinatal prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/perinatal-provider-overview/)

Before significant immune suppression, assess HBV status even when a patient recalls having recovered years earlier. AGA 2025 uses the serologic state and the planned immune therapy to choose antiviral prophylaxis or reliable monitoring. A positive surface antibody does not erase reactivation risk from previous natural infection. Coordinate preventive therapy before treatment rather than waiting for a hepatitis flare. The degree and duration of prevention depend on the regimen, especially with B-cell-depleting therapy. [AGA reactivation guidance](https://gastro.org/press-releases/hepatitis-b-is-a-problem-for-a-growing-number-of-patients-on-immunosuppressive-medications/)

HCV testing usually begins with antibody and automatic reflex RNA testing. Antibody indicates exposure, not necessarily current infection; detectable RNA establishes current viremia. Recent exposure can precede antibody formation, so RNA is appropriate when acute infection is suspected despite a negative antibody. A person who cleared infection or was cured can remain antibody-positive and become reinfected. Repeat RNA, not antibody alone, when assessing reinfection. [CDC HCV testing](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-c/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)

For eligible treatment-naive adults without cirrhosis, AASLD/IDSA offers glecaprevir/pibrentasvir 300/120 mg orally once daily with food for eight weeks or sofosbuvir/velpatasvir 400/100 mg orally once daily for twelve weeks. Assess fibrosis and interactions; obtain CBC, hepatic panel, and eGFR within six months, plus pretreatment quantitative RNA, HIV and HBV testing, and pregnancy assessment when applicable. Prior treatment, HBsAg positivity, pregnancy, suspected HCC, transplant, or cirrhosis changes the pathway. HIV treatment containing TDF with eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 also requires additional monitoring outside this simplified pathway. Watch for hypoglycemia on diabetes treatment and INR changes on warfarin. Confirm cure with RNA at least twelve weeks after completion; ongoing exposure warrants annual RNA and testing with new liver-test abnormalities. [AASLD/IDSA simplified noncirrhotic HCV treatment](https://www.hcvguidelines.org/guidance/simplified-hcv-treatment-for-treatment-naive-adults-without-cirrhosis/)

Do not wait six months merely to prove chronicity before arranging treatment of confirmed acute HCV; current guidance favors treatment after diagnosis of viremia. Decompensated cirrhosis requires a specialist regimen, and protease-inhibitor-containing combinations are not recommended in decompensated disease. Treat substance-use needs and access barriers alongside infection; continued risk is a reason to provide harm reduction, not abandon linkage to care. HCV cure does not cancel cirrhosis surveillance. [AASLD/IDSA acute HCV management](https://www.hcvguidelines.org/guidance/management-of-acute-hcv-infection/), [AASLD/IDSA decompensated cirrhosis](https://www.hcvguidelines.org/guidance/patients-with-decompensated-cirrhosis/)

HDV requires HBV to sustain infection; consider coinfection or superinfection when HBV disease is unexpectedly severe or risk is present, using specialist-directed antibody/RNA evaluation. HBV vaccination prevents the prerequisite HBV infection but does not treat established HBV/HDV. HEV is commonly fecal-orally transmitted, with food-associated infection also occurring in the United States. Pregnancy and immune suppression change its risk; persistent infection can occur in immune-compromised hosts. Consider it in otherwise unexplained acute hepatitis, not only after international travel. [CDC hepatitis D](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-d/about/index.html), [CDC hepatitis E](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-e/about/index.html)

### Metabolic and alcohol-associated liver disease can coexist

MASLD means steatotic liver disease with cardiometabolic dysfunction; MASH adds steatohepatitis, with inflammation and hepatocellular injury. Fibrosis stage is central to prognosis. Assess diabetes, adiposity, blood pressure, lipids, sleep-related disease, medicines, and alcohol rather than assuming the imaging phrase fatty liver describes a harmless incidental finding. A normal BMI does not exclude metabolic disease. Conversely, steatosis does not prove that a second cause of liver injury is absent.

Use the AGA 2026 tiered pathway for at-risk adults. FIB-4 below 1.3, or below 2.0 at age 65 or older, generally supports primary-care follow-up with repeat assessment every one to two years. Elevated results need a second-tier test, usually elastography or ELF. Liver stiffness below 8 kPa or ELF below 9.2 generally indicates low risk in this pathway; higher results prompt hepatology assessment. These are triage results, not interchangeable biopsy stages or cancer diagnoses. [AGA MASLD care pathway](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-care-pathway-for-the-risk-stratification-and-management-of-patients-with-masld/)

FIB-4 uses age, AST, ALT, and platelet count. Acute illness can distort it, and performance is limited in younger adults; reconcile a low result with the entire risk picture. Ultrasound can identify steatosis but cannot reliably exclude MASH or stage fibrosis. Noninvasive fibrosis tools reduce the need for biopsy, while uncertainty, discordant results, or another suspected disease can justify specialist evaluation. Avoid carrying one test's cutoff into a different disease or using a changing platelet count as proof of changing scar tissue.

Management addresses both hepatic and cardiovascular risk: sustainable nutrition, activity, weight treatment when indicated, diabetes management, and appropriate lipid/BP therapy. Exercise can help even without dramatic weight change. Discuss alcohol directly; metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure may contribute together, termed MetALD in the appropriate setting. AGA's September 2026 advice emphasizes combined etiologic assessment and integrated behavioral/metabolic care rather than forcing every patient into one exclusive cause. [AGA MetALD update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42776095/)

Selected adults with noncirrhotic MASH and F2-F3 fibrosis can receive disease-directed treatment. Resmetirom is a thyroid hormone receptor-beta agonist with an accelerated-approval indication, not a treatment for every ultrasound showing fat. Monitor for liver injury and gallbladder events, and review interactions: concomitant rosuvastatin/simvastatin are limited to 20 mg/day and atorvastatin/pravastatin to 40 mg/day. Clopidogrel requires adjustment of resmetirom, while gemfibrozil is not recommended with it. Avoid use in decompensated cirrhosis/Child-Pugh B-C; safety and efficacy in MASH cirrhosis are not established. [Current resmetirom prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=e67ea09f-a840-439c-86c8-f98585f978b2)

Wegovy semaglutide injection also has an accelerated-approval indication for adults with noncirrhotic MASH and F2-F3 fibrosis. Do not extrapolate this indication to oral semaglutide, every GLP-1 drug, a higher obesity dose, or an unstudied combination. Product selection, titration, pregnancy assessment, and monitoring require the indication-specific label; 04D develops weight-treatment prescribing. Histologic improvement is not proof that all long-term clinical outcomes have been established. [AASLD semaglutide guidance](https://doi.org/10.1097/hep.0000000000001608), [Current Wegovy indication-specific label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=ee06186f-2aa3-4990-a760-757579d8f77b)

**Clinical case.** A 58-year-old with diabetes has normal ALT, falling platelets, and a nodular liver. Assess fibrosis, portal hypertension, and alternative causes. Normal enzymes and treatment for weight or glucose do not replace cirrhosis assessment and surveillance.

Alcohol-associated disease ranges from steatosis through hepatitis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Quantify beverage size, frequency, binges, and prior withdrawal without blame. An AST-predominant pattern can support the history but cannot prove the cause. Alcohol-associated hepatitis is a clinical syndrome often involving recent jaundice after sustained heavy exposure; exclude infection, obstruction, and other causes. Severe disease requires inpatient severity assessment, nutrition, infection evaluation, and selected corticosteroid treatment rather than steroids for every AST elevation. ACG uses early Lille response assessment to stop ineffective steroid exposure. [ACG alcohol-associated liver disease guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11040545/)

Treatment includes sustained support for abstinence and alcohol use disorder, with a safe withdrawal plan when needed. Behavioral care and appropriate medication should be integrated with liver care, taking renal function, opioids, and hepatic severity into account. Refer for nutrition and selected transplant assessment rather than relying on a lecture or an inflexible abstinence interval as the sole evaluation criterion. Metabolic disease, viral infection, and alcohol may all contribute to progression. [ACG alcohol-associated disease management](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11040545/)

### Medication and supplement injury is a diagnosis requiring alternatives

Construct a timeline for prescriptions, OTC combinations, supplements, dose changes, and recently stopped drugs. Injury can develop after a medication is discontinued. Distinguish predictable dose-related toxicity, such as acetaminophen injury, from idiosyncratic reactions. Exclude viral disease, obstruction, ischemia, and immune causes rather than diagnose drug injury from chronology alone. Stop the likely offending nonessential agent with appropriate medical coordination, monitor the trajectory, and generally avoid deliberate rechallenge. Jaundice, systemic hypersensitivity, or impaired synthesis requires escalation. [AASLD drug and supplement injury guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9936988/)

Suspected acetaminophen toxicity needs urgent toxicology/hospital assessment and timely N-acetylcysteine when indicated. Include staggered excess and hidden combination products; a patient may not recognize that several cold or pain medicines contain the same ingredient. A single-ingestion nomogram cannot simply be applied to repeated excess or an unknown ingestion time. Do not wait for overt jaundice or a definitive medication history before seeking help in a compatible acute injury. [AASLD acute liver failure management](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/back-basics/defining-and-managing-acute-liver-failure)

Avoid the opposite error of withdrawing useful treatment from every patient with chronic liver disease. Mild aminotransferase elevations in stable MASLD are not a blanket statin contraindication; cardiovascular risk remains important. Distinguish compensated disease from acute injury or decompensation and use the actual product label and interaction assessment. Review herbal products as pharmacologically active exposures, not as automatically safer substitutes. Document the suspected reaction and follow-up so another clinician does not inadvertently restart the product. [AASLD statin safety discussion](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/why-series/why-are-we-still-afraid-statins-chronic-liver-disease)

### Cirrhosis changes the care plan even when symptoms are quiet

Cirrhosis involves advanced scarring and architectural distortion. Portal resistance and vascular changes contribute to collateral vessels, splenic sequestration, ascites, and impaired effective circulation. Patients can remain compensated for a substantial period; ascites, overt encephalopathy, or variceal hemorrhage marks decompensation. Symptoms such as edema, bruising, muscle loss, or confusion can emerge gradually. Diagnose the cause and stage while managing complications; coexisting etiologies are common. [NIDDK cirrhosis presentation and causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/cirrhosis/symptoms-causes)

New decompensation warrants hepatology and transplant consideration rather than waiting until all available scores become extreme. MELD-based assessment estimates risk but does not capture every burden, including recurrent encephalopathy or refractory fluid problems. Treatment of the cause can improve the course, yet surveillance and complication prevention remain necessary. Coordinate patient goals, caregiver needs, transport, medication access, and an emergency plan. Palliative symptom support can accompany disease-directed and transplant evaluation rather than signify abandonment of treatment.

| New finding in cirrhosis | Immediate concern | Practical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ascites with illness or hospital admission | Infection or another trigger | Prompt diagnostic paracentesis |
| Confusion or reduced alertness | HE or competing emergency | Assess in hospital; find the precipitant |
| Hematemesis, melena, collapse | Potential variceal or other hemorrhage | Emergency stabilization and GI care |
| Rising creatinine or low urine output | AKI, including HRS | Urgent cause/volume assessment |
| Progressive breathlessness | Hydrothorax or nonhepatic disease | Assess oxygenation and need for drainage |

### Ascites: understand circulation and investigate infection

Ascites reflects portal-hypertension physiology and renal sodium retention, not merely low serum albumin. AGA 2025 recommends prompt diagnostic paracentesis for new ascites and for hospital admission related to ascites or encephalopathy. Send cell count and culture with albumin-based assessment. A serum-ascites albumin gradient of at least 1.1 g/dL supports portal-hypertensive physiology; it does not prove cirrhosis, since cardiac and vascular disorders can also cause it. Interpret total protein and the complete clinical context.

Use sodium restriction and diuretics at the lowest effective doses with weight, renal, electrolyte, and symptom monitoring. Spironolactone and a loop diuretic are commonly paired. Refractory disease may need repeated therapeutic drainage, selected TIPS, and transplant evaluation. When more than five liters are removed, AGA advises albumin 6-8 g per total liter removed; smaller-volume drainage may also warrant albumin in vulnerable patients. [AGA ascites and volume management](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/management-of-ascites-volume-overload-and-hyponatremia-in-cirrhosis/)

For example, removal of 6 L calls for 36-48 g of albumin using that range, not 6-8 g for only the last liter. An elevated INR from cirrhosis does not measure the whole bleeding balance and should not trigger reflex prophylactic plasma merely to normalize the number; assess the procedure and any separate coagulopathy. Do not delay an indicated diagnostic tap simply to chase a cirrhotic INR target. [AGA albumin advice](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/management-of-ascites-volume-overload-and-hyponatremia-in-cirrhosis/), [AASLD procedural bleeding assessment](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/clinical-pearls/peri-procedural-management-bleeding-risk-cirrhosis)

SBP may present with abdominal pain or fever, but worsening encephalopathy or kidney function can be the main clue. Ascitic neutrophils at least 250 cells/mm3 warrant treatment for presumed infection even when culture is negative, while assessing for a surgically treatable source when the course or fluid findings suggest one. Obtain cultures without delaying antibiotics in an unstable patient. Antibiotic choice follows severity, acquisition setting, and local resistance; selected patients also receive albumin. After recovery, assess the need for secondary prophylaxis rather than automatically prescribing indefinite antibiotics to everyone with fluid. [AASLD paracentesis and SBP assessment](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/why-series/why-timing-matters-paracentesis-admission-cirrhosis)

**Clinical case.** A patient with cirrhosis and ascites develops confusion and poor intake over two days without fever or tenderness. Arrange hospital assessment for infection, bleeding, dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, and other causes, including paracentesis for SBP. Do not assume isolated HE and simply increase lactulose.

### Kidney injury in cirrhosis is not automatically hepatorenal syndrome

Review fluid losses, sepsis, hemorrhage, nephrotoxins, diuretics, obstruction, and intrinsic renal disease. ADQI/ICA 2024 recognizes HRS-AKI in advanced cirrhosis with ascites after appropriate assessment, lack of improvement after clinically indicated volume resuscitation, and no stronger alternative primary explanation. It does not require systematically giving albumin for 48 hours to everyone before making the diagnosis. In a patient already overloaded, reflex volume expansion can be harmful. HRS can coexist with other renal pathology. [ADQI/ICA kidney-injury consensus](https://www.journal-of-hepatology.eu/cms/10.1016/j.jhep.2024.03.031/attachment/dd8e41ea-7027-4e27-9ef9-4b852b5e275f/mmc2.pdf)

Hospital treatment may involve carefully selected vasoconstrictor therapy with albumin and close cardiopulmonary monitoring, while addressing the trigger and considering transplantation. Avoid applying this regimen to every creatinine rise or uncomplicated ascites. Review medicines that compromise perfusion or increase AKI risk, especially NSAIDs and inappropriate diuretic escalation. Severe hyponatremia also requires a supervised plan: neither unrestricted fluid administration nor rapid sodium correction is a routine outpatient solution. Coordinate renal and liver teams when the diagnosis or volume state is uncertain.

Terlipressin is one hospital option for adult HRS with rapidly declining kidney function, but can cause fatal respiratory failure. Its U.S. label contraindicates treatment during hypoxia/worsening respiratory symptoms or ongoing coronary, peripheral, or mesenteric ischemia. Do not initiate with oxygen saturation below 90%; use continuous oximetry and stop if it falls below 90%. Volume overload and grade 3 acute-on-chronic liver failure increase risk. Albumin is not a harmless automatic add-on when the patient is overloaded. [Current terlipressin label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=3a35b86c-f451-4fac-8499-43019e4da354)

### Encephalopathy requires a clinical diagnosis and a precipitant search

HE can alter attention, sleep, behavior, orientation, and consciousness. Do not diagnose overt HE from isolated asterixis alone. Exclude hypoglycemia, intoxication, infection, hypoxia, intracranial disease when indicated, and other metabolic causes. A high ammonia level alone does not establish the diagnosis or grade its severity, and serial levels should not drive treatment. New overt confusion or reduced alertness needs hospital assessment, not an instruction to keep working or driving until a routine appointment.

For acute overt HE, ACG's 2026 HE pathway uses lactulose, with PEG as an acute-care alternative, and recommends adding rifaximin to lactulose. This is not a home instruction to substitute a low-dose constipation regimen for monitored acute treatment. For outpatient prevention, titrate lactulose to two to three soft stools daily, using consistency as well as frequency; excessive diarrhea can cause dehydration and worsen HE. The October 2026 cirrhosis-prevention guideline suggests adding rifaximin after overt HE occurs while receiving lactulose. Identify and treat infection, bleeding, constipation, sedating medicines, renal dysfunction, and electrolyte changes rather than treating stool output alone. [ACG hepatic encephalopathy guidance](https://webfiles.gi.org/GuidelineHighlights/ACGHepaticEncephGH.pdf), [ACG cirrhosis prevention](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000004186)

Give the patient and caregiver a plan that names the usual mental baseline, warning changes, medicine access, and who to call. Difficulty tolerating lactulose should prompt a practical reassessment, not a judgment that the patient is uncooperative. Persistent subtle cognitive difficulty can warrant formal testing and counseling about driving or safety-sensitive work. Do not restrict protein as a reflex treatment for HE; preserve nutrition and muscle function while treating the neurologic syndrome.

The labeled adult rifaximin dose for reducing overt-HE recurrence is 550 mg orally twice daily, distinct from its IBS-D course. Severe hepatic impairment increases exposure, and renal-impairment pharmacokinetics have not been studied; review interactions and avoid assuming that poor absorption eliminates all risk. [Rifaximin recurrence-prevention label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=c5e8e2fd-7087-4b78-9181-cc259c0be2f1)

### Prevent complications and keep surveillance active

In compensated cirrhosis with clinically significant portal hypertension, nonselective beta blockade can prevent decompensation in appropriately selected patients; carvedilol is commonly preferred. Selection and dosing depend on blood pressure, renal status, and contraindications, not simply an elevated INR. Patients unable to receive the appropriate beta-blocker strategy need endoscopic assessment and an individualized variceal plan. High-risk varices can receive carvedilol or ligation for primary bleeding prevention. Acute bleeding management is covered in 05C. [AASLD portal hypertension pathway](https://www.aasld.org/practice-guidelines/portal-hypertension-bleeding-cirrhosis), [ACG cirrhosis prevention guideline](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000004186)

ACG October 2026 recommends liver ultrasound plus AFP every six months for patients with cirrhosis who are candidates for HCC treatment. An abnormal study needs diagnostic follow-up rather than waiting for the next surveillance date. AFP alone is not an adequate substitute; limited ultrasound visualization may require an alternative imaging plan. Selected HBV populations need HCC surveillance even without cirrhosis, and some remain at risk after HBsAg loss. Viral suppression or HCV cure does not remove an established cirrhosis indication. [ACG surveillance recommendation](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000004186), [AASLD HBV surveillance update](https://doi.org/10.1097/hep.0000000000001549)

Under AASLD's HCC recall pathway, a lesion smaller than 1 cm with otherwise reassuring surveillance is usually reassessed with ultrasound/AFP in 3-6 months. A lesion at least 1 cm, AFP at least 20 ng/mL, or rising AFP calls for diagnostic multiphasic contrast CT or MRI. AFP is neither a cancer diagnosis nor a reason to ignore a suspicious lesion when normal. The imaging plan must also account for ultrasound quality, kidney function, and contrast safety. [AASLD HCC recall guidance](https://doi.org/10.1097/hep.0000000000000466)

Assess HAV and HBV immunity and provide indicated vaccination. Review influenza, COVID-19, pneumococcal, zoster, and RSV eligibility against current age/risk schedules and product recommendations rather than an old liver-clinic checklist. Counsel on smoking cessation, alcohol treatment, safe exercise, bone health, and unnecessary supplements. ACG 2026 emphasizes dietitian-supported nutrition, adequate protein, and structured activity. Do not start aspirin solely to prevent HCC. [ACG preventive care](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000004186), [CDC hepatitis B vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/vaccine-administration/index.html)

Sarcopenia may be masked by ascites or obesity. Assess intake, strength, weight history, food access, dentition, and symptoms that prevent eating. Avoid prolonged fasting; a late snack and regular meals can support intake. Sodium advice must be balanced against palatability and malnutrition, and fluid restriction is not automatic for every patient with ascites. Review OTC analgesics and sedatives explicitly. At each follow-up, confirm the next surveillance date, renal/electrolyte monitoring, the response to cause-specific treatment, and whether a referral or new result remains unresolved. [AASLD outpatient cirrhosis care](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/back-basics/back-basics-outpatient-management-cirrhosis)
