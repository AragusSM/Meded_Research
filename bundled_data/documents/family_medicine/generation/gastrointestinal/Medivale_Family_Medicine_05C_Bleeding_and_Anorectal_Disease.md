# Medivale Family Medicine

## 05C. Bleeding and Anorectal Disease

### Describe the bleeding, then assess the patient

Overt bleeding is visible: hematemesis is vomiting blood, melena is black tarry stool, and hematochezia is passage of red or maroon blood per rectum. Occult loss is not visible and may appear as iron-deficiency anemia or a positive stool test. Ask what the patient actually saw, how often, and whether the amount or symptoms are changing. A photograph can clarify the description, but it cannot establish severity or exclude an internal source. Blood swallowed from the nose and nonblood stool discoloration are alternative explanations that require a compatible history, not automatic reassurance.

Color suggests a source but does not prove it. Brisk upper bleeding can produce hematochezia; a proximal intestinal source can produce dark stool. Iron and bismuth can darken stool without causing melena, yet medication-related discoloration can coexist with real bleeding. The upper tract, small bowel, and colon/anorectum should remain separate diagnostic regions. A source that escapes initial endoscopy is not necessarily absent or inevitably malignant. [NIDDK bleeding presentation and causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gastrointestinal-bleeding/symptoms-causes)

| Presentation | Important possibilities | Immediate clinical question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vomited blood or coffee-ground material | Ulcer, mucosal injury, varices | Is airway or circulatory support needed? |
| Black tarry stool | Often upper bleeding; other intestinal sources possible | Is this true melena with ongoing loss? |
| Red blood with faintness or poor perfusion | Major lower or brisk upper bleeding | Can the patient be stabilized for investigation? |
| Small recurrent rectal bleeding | Anorectal disease, inflammation, neoplasia | Does the apparent local source explain the whole presentation? |

Among lower-tract causes, diverticular hemorrhage often causes abrupt, painless substantial bleeding, whereas angioectasias can cause intermittent painless or occult loss. Cramping pain followed by bloody stool raises concern for ischemic colitis as well as infection or IBD; pain out of proportion to examination raises the separate emergency of mesenteric ischemia. Repeated retching before hematemesis suggests a Mallory-Weiss tear, but severe chest/upper abdominal pain after vomiting also raises concern for esophageal rupture. These patterns guide evaluation, not exclusion of other sources. [NIDDK GI bleeding causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gastrointestinal-bleeding/symptoms-causes), [ASGE GI bleeding assessment](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/key-resources/blog/view/trainee-advisor/2026/08/19/gi-emergencies-for-the-trainee--a-practical-approach-to-upper-and-lower-gastrointestinal-bleeding)

Assess mental status, pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, urine output, respiratory status, and repeated observations. Syncope, hypotension, confusion, chest pain, ongoing large-volume loss, or severe abdominal pain requires emergency care. In the office, activate emergency transport when unstable; do not send the patient to drive to a laboratory or await a routine referral. Shock can precede a striking hemoglobin decline because whole blood is initially lost together. A single early normal result cannot establish that bleeding is minor.

The acute evaluation usually includes serial CBC measurements, renal function/electrolytes, coagulation assessment when relevant, and blood-bank preparation according to severity. Review previous hemoglobin, liver disease, prior bleeding, endoscopic procedures, abdominal surgery, cardiovascular disease, and renal disease. Record NSAIDs, aspirin, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, supplements, and the last doses. Stabilization, source localization, and medication decisions occur together; no laboratory panel should delay resuscitation. Endoscopy, imaging, and selected small-bowel studies answer different questions. [NIDDK diagnostic evaluation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gastrointestinal-bleeding/diagnosis)

**Clinical case.** A 49-year-old anticoagulated after pulmonary embolism passes maroon stool, nearly faints, and is clammy and hypotensive despite an initially unchanged hemoglobin. Stabilize circulation urgently and coordinate bleeding/thrombosis management. Anticoagulation increases bleeding risk but does not identify its source.

### Upper and lower bleeding require different initial pathways

For suspected upper bleeding, the ACG pathway uses validated risk assessment; a Glasgow-Blatchford score of 0-1 can identify selected very-low-risk patients for outpatient management after evaluation and reliable follow-up. Hospitalized upper-bleeding patients generally use a restrictive transfusion threshold of 7 g/dL, with endoscopy within 24 hours after presentation and appropriate resuscitation. These are not instructions to wait for a numerical threshold during exsanguination. [ACG upper GI bleeding guideline](https://socgastro.org.br/novo/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/ACG_Clinical_Guideline__Upper_Gastrointestinal_and.14-1.pdf)

Active myocardial infarction is a distinct transfusion population, not simply a history of coronary disease. AABB 2025 conditionally suggests a liberal strategy when hemoglobin is below 10 g/dL in hospitalized patients with acute MI, based on low-certainty evidence. Apply the clinical context and specialist judgment rather than exporting the usual stable upper-bleeding threshold to every ischemic patient. Conversely, this does not make 10 g/dL a universal target for all GI bleeding. [AABB acute MI guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40825204/)

For acute lower bleeding, ASGE 2026 favors colonoscopy when stable initially or after resuscitation; ongoing instability favors contrast CT with or without angiography. Colonoscopy should occur when the patient can tolerate the procedure and full bowel preparation, rather than to satisfy a rigid clock. Hematochezia with initial instability also requires evaluation for an upper source unless imaging directly establishes lower bleeding. Persistent bleeding despite endoscopic or radiologic treatment may require surgery. [ASGE acute lower GI bleeding guideline](https://www.giejournal.org/article/S0016-5107%2826%2906857-4/fulltext)

A useful distinction is between locating active extravasation and inspecting the mucosa. CT angiographic evaluation is valuable during substantial ongoing loss, whereas colonoscopy can inspect and treat lesions after adequate preparation. Neither a negative snapshot during intermittent bleeding nor a poorly visualized colon establishes a permanent absence of disease. The result must be interpreted with the timing of the event, the technical quality of the study, and the patient's subsequent course. Do not ask a patient with ongoing shock to finish a home preparation before reassessment.

After high-risk ulcer hemostasis, ACG recommends high-dose PPI treatment for three days followed by twice-daily oral therapy through two weeks after index endoscopy. Active bleeding or a nonbleeding visible vessel warrants endoscopic treatment; epinephrine is not sufficient alone. Recurrent bleeding usually prompts repeat endoscopy, with transcatheter embolization when endoscopic control fails. The guideline does not make a recommendation for or against routine pre-endoscopic PPI treatment. [ACG ulcer hemostasis and acid suppression](https://socgastro.org.br/novo/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/ACG_Clinical_Guideline__Upper_Gastrointestinal_and.14-1.pdf)

Suspected variceal hemorrhage needs a different hospital bundle: resuscitation, prompt vasoactive treatment such as octreotide, antibiotic prophylaxis, and urgent endoscopy after stabilization. AASLD describes endoscopy within 12 hours in this setting. Esophageal and gastric varices do not necessarily receive the same endoscopic procedure; gastric bleeding may require specialized endoscopic or radiologic expertise. Arrange transfer when local capability is insufficient. Cirrhosis and longer-term portal-hypertension management follow in 05D. [AASLD variceal emergency care](https://www.aasld.org/liver-fellow-network/core-series/clinical-pearls/endoscopic-management-gastric-varices)

Definitive care depends on the lesion, not merely the appearance of blood. Endoscopic methods can clip or coagulate bleeding sites; interventional radiology can target a vessel, and surgery remains necessary in selected uncontrolled or structural disease. After control, remove avoidable injury and treat the cause: for example, reassess NSAID exposure and confirm H. pylori eradication after an infected ulcer. A discharge prescription alone is not evidence that the risk of recurrent loss has been addressed. [NIDDK treatment overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gastrointestinal-bleeding/treatment)

### Antithrombotic treatment needs an explicit restart plan

Record the indication for each antithrombotic: primary prevention, prior infarction, recent stenting, atrial fibrillation, mechanical valve, or recent venous thrombosis have different consequences of interruption. ACG/CAG suggests not withholding cardiac aspirin used for secondary prevention during GI bleeding; if it was interrupted, resume it on the day endoscopic hemostasis is confirmed. This is not a rule to continue every drug in every unstable patient, nor does it automatically apply to aspirin taken only for primary prevention. [ACG/CAG antithrombotic guideline](https://gi.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/ACG-CAG-Guideline.pdf)

Major anticoagulant-associated bleeding requires a hospital decision about temporary interruption, selective reversal, and subsequent resumption. Consider bleeding control, the last dose, kidney function, and thrombotic indication; consult the relevant teams, especially with a recent stent, mechanical valve, or recent embolus. Do not replace that assessment with a universal reversal prescription or permanently stop therapy without discussing why it was needed. The discharge record should state which medicines were changed, who will reassess them, and when. Patients need practical instructions for recurrent bleeding as well as signs of thrombosis.

### Iron deficiency is a finding that needs a cause

Repeated small losses can deplete iron before severe anemia or microcytosis develops. Review GI and menstrual bleeding, donation, diet, pregnancy, surgery, and medicines; heavy periods and a GI disorder can coexist. Interpret CBC, ferritin, and selected transferrin saturation together: ferritin can be preserved by inflammation or kidney disease, while low serum iron alone does not prove depleted stores. Section 15C develops the broader anemia differential and replacement monitoring; here the priority is finding a GI source. [NIH iron assessment](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/)

In patients with anemia, AGA recommends a ferritin cutoff below 45 ng/mL to identify iron deficiency, rather than using 15 alone. This threshold is not a universal rule for healthy-person screening or all inflammatory/renal populations. Men and postmenopausal women generally need bidirectional endoscopy. In asymptomatic premenopausal women, AGA conditionally favors that evaluation over iron alone, with shared decision-making about alternative causes and the patient's preferences. [AGA GI evaluation of iron-deficiency anemia](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/gastrointestinal-evaluation-of-iron-deficiency-anemia/)

After unrevealing bidirectional endoscopy without another cause, AGA suggests noninvasive H. pylori testing and treatment if positive. When celiac disease is plausible, start with appropriate serology and pursue biopsy when indicated. In an uncomplicated asymptomatic patient with negative bidirectional evaluation, a trial of iron generally precedes routine capsule endoscopy. Ongoing overt bleeding, recurrent deficiency, concerning symptoms, or a result likely to change antithrombotic management warrants a more individualized small-bowel plan. [AGA evaluation after negative initial studies](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/gastrointestinal-evaluation-of-iron-deficiency-anemia/)

Do not use a screening stool test as permission to ignore established iron-deficiency anemia. Screening asks whether an asymptomatic eligible person needs further assessment; anemia creates a different diagnostic question. Likewise, seeing a hemorrhoid does not prove that it caused enough loss to explain the entire hemoglobin trajectory. Document the proposed cause, what has been excluded, and what finding would reopen the investigation. If the patient declines endoscopy, explain the unresolved risk and arrange a definite follow-up rather than label the evaluation completed.

Replace iron while pursuing the cause. AGA advises oral iron at most once daily; alternate-day dosing can improve tolerability for some people, and ferrous sulfate is an inexpensive option without a proven universally superior oral formulation. Its pathway also advises vitamin C to aid absorption. IV iron is appropriate when oral treatment is not tolerated, iron stores fail to improve, or absorption is unlikely to be adequate. Treat ongoing blood loss and the responsible disease as well. [AGA iron treatment advice](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/management-of-iron-deficiency-anemia/)

Check elemental-iron content, tolerability, and the early hemoglobin response; 15C provides the practical replacement schedule. Poor response warrants reassessment of actual intake, continued loss, malabsorption, and the diagnosis. A rising hemoglobin does not establish a benign source or complete an indicated endoscopic evaluation.

**Clinical case.** A 41-year-old with light periods develops recurrent iron deficiency after responding to iron. Review bleeding and malabsorption and arrange appropriate diagnostic assessment. Neither treatment response nor a previous negative screening stool test establishes the cause or removes the indication for evaluation.

### Examine anorectal symptoms without premature closure

Ask whether pain occurs during stool passage, persists afterward, or is continuous; distinguish bleeding, prolapse, discharge, itch, and a palpable lump. Obtain consent, offer an appropriate chaperone, and explain each step. Inspection may reveal a fissure, thrombosed external hemorrhoid, inflamed opening, or suspicious lesion. Gentle digital examination and anoscopy can add information when tolerated. Do not force a painful examination merely to complete a checklist; severe pain or suspected deep disease may require specialist examination and analgesia.

| Pattern | Leading consideration | Important distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Painless red blood, sometimes prolapse | Internal hemorrhoid | Other bleeding sources may coexist |
| Sudden tender external lump | Thrombosed external hemorrhoid | Not the same as uncomplicated internal disease |
| Cutting pain with stool, lingering afterward | Anal fissure | Atypical location or persistence needs assessment |
| Constant or escalating pain, swelling or fever | Abscess | Deep infection can lack a visible lump |
| Recurrent drainage from an opening | Fistula | Assess tract anatomy and associated disease |

### Hemorrhoids: treat symptoms, not every normal cushion

Hemorrhoidal cushions are normal vascular tissue; disease means symptoms such as bleeding, prolapse, or discomfort. Internal hemorrhoids arise above the dentate line and commonly bleed without sharp pain. External thrombosis below it produces a painful lump. Internal prolapse is described as grade I without prolapse, II reducing spontaneously, III requiring manual reduction, and IV remaining prolapsed. Grade describes prolapse, not the amount of blood lost or the safety of ignoring anemia. Rectal varices in portal hypertension are a different process. [ASCRS hemorrhoid patient information](https://www.fascrs.org/Web/Patients/Diseases-and-Conditions/A-Z/Hemorrhoids-Expanded-Information.aspx)

ASCRS begins treatment with fiber, suitable fluid intake, and defecation habits that reduce straining and prolonged toilet sitting. Persistent grade I-II and selected grade III internal disease can receive office procedures; rubber-band ligation is generally the most effective office option. Selected external or combined advanced disease warrants excisional surgery. Some thrombosed external hemorrhoids benefit from early excision, but symptom course and patient preference matter rather than a compulsory time cutoff for everyone. [ASCRS hemorrhoid guideline](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Toolkit/2851101/all/Management_of_Hemorrhoids__2024_?q=2024+clinical+guidelines+practice)

Short-term topical products may relieve symptoms without correcting prolapse or identifying the source of blood. Discuss postoperative or postprocedure expectations and urgent warning symptoms; marked pain, fever, or urinary difficulty after banding warrants prompt assessment. Persistent bleeding after apparently successful hemorrhoid treatment needs reconsideration of the source. Patients with abdominal symptoms, anemia, concerning history, or an unmet diagnostic/screening indication should not have those questions closed because hemorrhoids are visible. [ASCRS hemorrhoid guideline](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Toolkit/2851101/all/Management_of_Hemorrhoids__2024_?q=2024+clinical+guidelines+practice)

### Fissures: interrupt the pain and spasm cycle

An anal fissure is a tear in the lining, often associated with hard stool or diarrhea. Pain provokes sphincter spasm, which can perpetuate poor local healing and fear of defecation. Small streaks of blood may accompany severe cutting pain during and after bowel movements. The common posterior midline site is useful, but anterior fissures also occur. Avoid calling every painful anal condition a fissure: constant progressive pain, a mass, discharge, or systemic illness changes the differential. [ASCRS fissure patient information](https://www.fascrs.org/Web/Patients/Diseases-and-Conditions/A-Z/Anal-Fissure-Expanded-Information.aspx)

ASCRS distinguishes acute fissures lasting less than six weeks from chronic disease. Begin with soft, easy-to-pass stool, fiber and appropriate fluids, and comfort measures such as warm baths. Chronic fissures can be treated with topical calcium-channel blockers or nitrates; calcium-channel blockers have similar effectiveness with fewer headaches. Botulinum toxin and surgery are specialist options when appropriate. Lateral or multiple fissures require broader evaluation for conditions including Crohn's disease, infection, and malignancy. [ASCRS anal fissure guideline](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Evidence-Based-Guidelines-and-Expert-Consensus/3982013/2/Management_of_Anal_Fissures__2023_)

Lateral internal sphincterotomy can heal chronic fissures, but the continence tradeoff matters. Prior obstetric sphincter injury, preexisting incontinence, previous anal procedures, and IBD affect selection. Tailored techniques and alternatives are individualized rather than treating surgery as an inevitable next step for every patient. Explain how and where a prescribed topical medicine is applied, check tolerance, and arrange review of healing rather than indefinite self-treatment of a presumed benign tear. [ASCRS anal fissure guideline](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Evidence-Based-Guidelines-and-Expert-Consensus/3982013/2/Management_of_Anal_Fissures__2023_)

### Abscesses and fistulas require source control

An anorectal abscess often begins with infection of an anal gland. Patients may have throbbing pain, swelling, fever, or malaise. Deep collections may lack superficial swelling. Immunosuppression or diabetes can alter presentation and increase the consequences of delay. Drainage is central; antibiotics alone do not reliably empty a collection. [ASCRS abscess and fistula guidance](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Evidence-Based-Guidelines-and-Expert-Consensus/3982014/0/Management_of_Anorectal_Abscess__Fistula_in_Ano__and_Rectovaginal_Fistula__2022_)

Arrange prompt surgical assessment. Imaging is selective, particularly for suspected occult, recurrent, or complex infection and selected immunosuppressed or Crohn's patients. Adjunct antibiotics are generally reserved for cellulitis, systemic infection, or immunosuppression rather than automatically replacing or following every uncomplicated drainage. A persistent draining tract may represent a fistula; treatment must account for sphincter involvement and continence. Recurrent or complex disease should raise the question of associated Crohn's disease, not just another course of tablets. [ASCRS abscess and fistula guidance](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Evidence-Based-Guidelines-and-Expert-Consensus/3982014/0/Management_of_Anorectal_Abscess__Fistula_in_Ano__and_Rectovaginal_Fistula__2022_)

**Clinical case.** An immunosuppressed patient has worsening rectal pain without visible swelling. Arrange urgent assessment for deeper infection rather than empiric hemorrhoid treatment. Rapid progression, toxicity, or disproportionate pain requires emergency surgical evaluation; track healing and recurrent drainage after source control.

Discharge, tenesmus, ulcers, and relevant exposure can instead suggest proctitis. CDC recommends appropriate anorectal examination and testing for gonorrhea, chlamydia, herpes, and syphilis, with HIV assessment and pathogen-specific management. Obtain a respectful exposure history rather than infer risk from identity alone. Infectious proctitis can resemble IBD; an untested assumption followed by immune suppression can be harmful. The STI chapter develops treatment and partner care. [CDC proctitis guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/proctitis.htm)

### A positive screening test needs completed follow-up

A positive FIT does not diagnose cancer, but it calls for colonoscopy to determine the source. Repeating FIT until a negative result appears does not complete that pathway. Discuss what the result means without telling the patient either that cancer is certain or that a hemorrhoid has already explained it. Track the referral through the actual procedure and report, including barriers such as transport, work, bowel-preparation access, language, and the need for an escort. An order marked sent is not a completed investigation. [NCI positive FIT follow-up](https://www.cancer.gov/news-events/cancer-currents-blog/2022/positive-fit-stool-test-colonoscopy)

Keep three indications separate: average-risk screening, evaluation of symptoms or anemia, and surveillance after known lesions. A patient who develops alarm findings after a prior examination may need diagnostic reassessment even when a routine screening interval has not elapsed. Conversely, a high-quality negative examination and resolved clinical concern should not automatically lead to repetitive testing without a new indication. The report should identify the indication, extent and preparation quality, findings, pathology, completeness of removal, and a reasoned follow-up plan. Screening eligibility and hereditary assessment are developed in 01B.

### Polyp follow-up depends on pathology and examination quality

USMSTF intervals assume average risk, adequate complete examination and excision. Hereditary syndromes, IBD, cancer, malignant polyps, and relevant family history need separate planning. SSL means sessile serrated lesion.

| Finding | Next colonoscopy |
| --- | --- |
| Normal | 10 years |
| 1-2 tubular adenomas <10 mm | 7-10 years |
| 3-4 adenomas <10 mm | 3-5 years |
| 5-10 adenomas <10 mm | 3 years |
| Adenoma at least 10 mm, villous, or high-grade dysplasia | 3 years |
| More than 10 adenomas | 1 year; consider genetics |
| 1-2 SSLs <10 mm | 5-10 years |
| 3-4 SSLs <10 mm | 3-5 years |
| 5-10 SSLs <10 mm; SSL at least 10 mm/dysplastic; traditional serrated adenoma | 3 years |
| Hyperplastic polyp at least 10 mm | 3-5 years; favor 3 with quality or pathology uncertainty |
| Up to 20 rectosigmoid hyperplastic polyps <10 mm | 10 years |
| Piecemeal adenoma/SSL at least 20 mm | 6 months |

[USMSTF post-polypectomy recommendations](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/publications/guidelines/recommendations-for-follow-up-after-colonoscopy-and-polypectomy-a-consensus-update-by-the-us-multi-society-task-force-on-colorectal-cancer)

The examination must reach the cecum and adequately detect lesions larger than 5 mm, with confidence in complete excision. Up to 20 hyperplastic polyps smaller than 10 mm proximal to the sigmoid also have a ten-year interval, although the evidence is weaker than for distal lesions. After piecemeal removal of a lesion at least 20 mm, the six-month check is only the first surveillance: the next is one year after that examination, then three years after the second. Later intervals also depend on prior findings, not merely the latest polyp count. [USMSTF quality and serial-surveillance qualifications](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/publications/guidelines/recommendations-for-follow-up-after-colonoscopy-and-polypectomy-a-consensus-update-by-the-us-multi-society-task-force-on-colorectal-cancer)

Preparation quality is part of the result, not a footnote. USMSTF 2025 recommends repeating an examination within 12 months when preparation is inadequate to assign standard intervals. After an abnormal noncolonoscopy screening result, repeat as soon as possible, generally within three months. Plan a better preparation strategy and address prior failure, constipation, instructions, medication issues, and access; do not simply issue the same unexplained instructions again. Split dosing is the preferred general approach, with individualized product and safety assessment. [USMSTF bowel preparation update](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/publications/guidelines/optimizing-bowel-preparation-quality-for-colonoscopy--consensus-recommendations-by-the-us-multi-society-task-force-on-colorectal-cancer)

**Clinical case.** Two small adenomas removed during an inadequately prepared colonoscopy do not establish a low total polyp burden. Confirm early repeat examination and improve preparation; both pathology and examination quality determine surveillance.

Retrieve final histology before calling a polyp harmless. Conventional adenomas and serrated precursors have different patterns, and a large lesion labeled hyperplastic may need expert pathology review if the clinical context is discordant. Document size, number, histology, removal method, and any concern about residual tissue. An invasive cancer within a polyp requires assessment of cancer-specific features and adequacy of treatment, not just a standard benign-polyp recall date. Multiple lesions over successive examinations may be more informative than the count at a single visit.

### Colorectal warning findings are diagnostic problems at any age

Persistent rectal bleeding, new sustained bowel-habit change, unexplained iron deficiency, unintentional weight loss, and a rectal or abdominal mass warrant evaluation. Obstructive symptoms, severe distention, vomiting, peritoneal signs, or major loss require urgent escalation. Age-based screening rules do not justify waiting to investigate a symptomatic younger adult. Right-sided lesions can present through chronic occult loss, while distal lesions may cause visible blood or obstructive symptoms; neither pattern is exclusive. The patient's complete course matters more than matching a single stereotyped presentation. [NCI colon cancer presentation](https://www.cancer.gov/types/colorectal/patient/colon-treatment-pdq)

Colonoscopy with tissue diagnosis generally establishes the lesion; subsequent staging and treatment depend on site, extent, pathology, and patient goals. CEA is useful in selected cancer-management settings but is not a screening test or a reliable rule-out test for a symptomatic patient. A normal value must not cancel an indicated examination. Tumor mismatch-repair/microsatellite assessment can affect inherited-risk evaluation and systemic therapy selection. Refer promptly for multidisciplinary care rather than infer treatment from location alone. [NCI professional colon cancer guidance](https://www.cancer.gov/types/colorectal/hp/colon-treatment-pdq)

Ask about relatives' cancer types, ages at diagnosis, and known genetic results, and update the family record when pathology becomes available. Concern about inherited risk can change care for relatives as well as the patient. After referral, primary care still manages anemia, nutrition, medication safety, distress, practical barriers, and preventive needs. Track the diagnostic report and the agreed next action. A visible anorectal lesion, improvement with iron, or a negative biomarker should not become an excuse to lose a patient with unresolved warning findings to follow-up.
