# Medivale Family Medicine

## 05B. Bowel Symptoms

### Define what changed before assigning a bowel diagnosis

Patients use diarrhea for watery stool, frequent small stools, urgency, or leakage; they use constipation for hard stool, straining, infrequency, or incomplete emptying. Ask for the usual pattern and the change from it. Record duration, stool consistency, blood, nocturnal symptoms, pain, fever, weight trajectory, and ability to eat and drink. Acute diarrhea usually lasts less than two weeks; persistence beyond that point broadens the infectious and noninfectious differential, while symptoms beyond four weeks require a chronic-diarrhea approach. Frequency alone does not measure severity or prove the mechanism.

Review recent antibiotics, travel, sick contacts, food and water exposure, healthcare contact, sexual exposure when relevant, bowel surgery, immune status, and all medications. Metformin, magnesium products, laxatives, antibiotics, and sugar alcohols can cause loose stools; opioids, anticholinergic medicines, iron, and some calcium-channel blockers can promote constipation. A new symptom after a dose change deserves attention, but an apparent medication explanation does not cancel bleeding, weight loss, or systemic illness. Ask about access to toilets, food insecurity, caregiving, and the functional effect of symptoms. [NIDDK diarrhea assessment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/diarrhea/diagnosis), [NIDDK constipation causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/constipation/symptoms-causes)

Hypotension, confusion, oliguria, severe dehydration, peritoneal findings, major bleeding, marked distention, or persistent vomiting requires urgent assessment. Sudden pain with bloody stool may be ischemic rather than infectious. Constipation with vomiting and inability to pass flatus raises concern for obstruction, not a need for stronger home laxatives. An older patient passing liquid stool may have leakage around impaction. In children, hydration, growth, bilious vomiting, and congenital or neurologic causes require age-specific assessment. Do not infer safety from a familiar IBS label when the symptom pattern has changed.

### Acute diarrhea: replace losses and test selectively

Most short, uncomplicated episodes are self-limited. Assess intake, urine output, pulse, perfusion, mental status, and orthostatic symptoms rather than relying on one sign such as dry lips. Oral rehydration replaces water and electrolytes; use a properly prepared oral rehydration solution for clinically important losses when the patient can drink. Give small frequent amounts if nausea limits intake. Severe depletion, shock, altered consciousness, ileus, or failure of oral replacement requires monitored IV isotonic fluid. Heart failure and kidney disease modify the amount and reassessment plan. [IDSA hydration recommendations](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/infectious-diarrhea/)

Glucose-coupled sodium absorption helps explain why an appropriate rehydration solution remains useful even while diarrhea continues. Water alone does not replace the full electrolyte loss, and very sugary drinks can aggravate an osmotic load. Resume an adequate diet as tolerated rather than prescribe prolonged fasting or a nutritionally incomplete restrictive diet. Continue usual breast milk or appropriate feeding in children under pediatric guidance. Loperamide can reduce stool frequency in selected immunocompetent adults with uncomplicated watery diarrhea, but avoid it with fever, blood, suspected inflammatory colitis, or ileus. The IDSA acute-infectious-diarrhea pathway advises against antimotility drugs in patients younger than 18 years; adult OTC availability is not a pediatric treatment recommendation. [IDSA supportive and ancillary care](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/infectious-diarrhea/)

Stool testing becomes useful with blood, fever, severe pain, sepsis, an outbreak, prolonged symptoms, or a vulnerable host. Select tests for the exposure and question rather than order every available panel. Molecular detection can identify genetic material without proving that every detected organism caused the illness; susceptibility testing and public-health culture requirements may still matter. Fecal leukocytes or lactoferrin should not be used to name the pathogen in acute infectious diarrhea. Most uncomplicated watery illness does not need empiric antibiotics, and bloody diarrhea is not an automatic antibiotic indication. [IDSA infectious diarrhea testing and treatment](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/infectious-diarrhea/)

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli is an important exception to the instinct to treat visible blood with antibiotics. Avoid antibiotics and antimotility agents in suspected or confirmed STEC disease because they can increase complication risk. Worsening fatigue, pallor, bruising, or reduced urine after diarrheal illness raises concern for hemolytic uremic syndrome, with hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney injury. Arrange urgent reassessment rather than assume that decreasing stool frequency means recovery. [CDC E. coli treatment safety](https://www.cdc.gov/ecoli/treatment/index.html)

With diagnosed STEC, arrange serial hemoglobin, platelet, electrolyte, and renal-function assessment to detect evolving HUS; suspected hemolysis also warrants a smear. Severe febrile dysentery, sepsis, or severe illness in an immunocompromised patient can justify antimicrobials before all results return, with cultures and exposure-directed selection. This exception must not become routine treatment of every bloody stool. Counsel on handwashing, safe food handling, and public-health restrictions for food handling, healthcare, or childcare work; symptom resolution alone may not satisfy local clearance rules. [IDSA pathogen exceptions and follow-up](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/infectious-diarrhea/)

Travel-associated diarrhea is treated according to functional severity, not merely a stool count or the fact that travel occurred. CDC does not recommend antibiotics for mild illness; they can be considered for moderate illness and are advised for severe illness, including dysentery. Azithromycin is generally preferred for severe disease, with choice informed by resistance and the travel setting. Rifaximin-type noninvasive regimens are not suitable substitutes when fever or dysentery suggests invasive infection. Antibiotic prophylaxis is not routine, and treatment carries adverse-effect, resistance, and C. difficile risks. Food/water precautions reduce some exposures but cannot eliminate risk. [CDC travelers' diarrhea pathway](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/preparing-international-travelers/travelers-diarrhea.html)

When appropriate for an adult traveler, oral azithromycin can be given as 1,000 mg once (a single or divided dose that day) or 500 mg daily for three days. Check QT risk, interactions, and regional resistance. A nonabsorbed antibiotic is not an interchangeable choice for invasive disease. Pregnancy and childhood require their own selection and dosing. [CDC adult travelers' diarrhea regimens](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/preparing-international-travelers/travelers-diarrhea.html)

Persistent diarrhea after travel is a different problem from another day of acute illness. Giardia and other parasites, resistant infection, temporary carbohydrate malabsorption, and postinfectious gut-brain symptoms are possibilities. Use exposure-directed stool testing and evaluate persistent symptoms rather than repeat empiric antibacterial courses. A past enteric infection can precede IBS, but ongoing infection and alarm features must still be considered. [CDC post-travel diarrhea](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/post-travel-evaluation/post-travel-diarrhea.html)

### C. difficile requires symptoms as well as a laboratory result

Disruption of the intestinal microbial community permits toxin-mediated C. difficile disease, particularly after antibiotic exposure, although community cases also occur. Test a compatible new diarrheal illness, not formed stool or an asymptomatic patient. A positive molecular test alone may reflect colonization; clinical context and a locally validated multistep testing approach matter. Conversely, a discordant result cannot be dismissed automatically when suspicion is high. Severe illness can progress to ileus or megacolon, in which reduced stool output is not reassuring. [AGA 2026 adult C. difficile update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42383946/)

For initial nonfulminant adult infection, AGA favors fidaxomicin 200 mg orally twice daily for ten days; vancomycin 125 mg orally four times daily for ten days is an acceptable alternative. Fulminant disease, including shock, ileus, or megacolon, requires multidisciplinary hospital care: vancomycin 500 mg orally/enterally every six hours plus metronidazole 500 mg IV every eight hours, with rectal vancomycin when ileus impairs delivery. IV vancomycin is not a substitute for luminal treatment. Do not use metronidazole alone for routine nonfulminant disease. After clinical recovery, do not order a cure test. A first recurrence needs fidaxomicin or a vancomycin taper plan; after a second recurrence, offer microbiota-restoration assessment, earlier in selected high-risk patients. Approved recurrence-prevention products are not treatment substitutes for fulminant colitis or unscreened home stool transfer. [AGA C. difficile treatment and recurrence advice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42383946/)

**Clinical case.** An 81-year-old develops frequent watery stools, poor intake, and lightheadedness after pneumonia treatment. Assess volume status, medicines and laxatives, renal function, appropriate stool testing, and disposition. After clinical recovery, persistent test positivity alone does not warrant retreatment.

### Chronic diarrhea: organize the mechanism without forcing a single category

Watery, fatty/malabsorptive, and inflammatory patterns help select an evaluation, but mixed mechanisms are common. Ask whether symptoms continue overnight, whether particular foods reproduce them, and whether there is weight loss, anemia, fever, blood, or a family history of celiac disease, IBD, or cancer. An osmotic load tends to lessen when the substrate is removed; secretion can persist despite fasting. Do not ask an ill patient to fast as a home diagnostic experiment. Stool electrolyte measurements are selective tools, not mandatory tests for every chronic loose stool.

| Dominant pattern | Examples to consider | Useful next question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Watery, food-related | Lactose/sugar alcohols, medication effects | Is there a reproducible exposure or dose relationship? |
| Persistent watery or nocturnal | Bile acid diarrhea, microscopic colitis, selected infections | Are targeted tests or colonic biopsies needed? |
| Greasy/bulky stool with weight loss | Celiac disease, pancreatic insufficiency, other malabsorption | Which nutrient or digestive function is failing? |
| Blood, urgency, systemic features | IBD, infection, ischemia, malignancy | Is urgent inflammatory or structural evaluation required? |

For an immunocompetent adult with chronic watery diarrhea and no alarm or malabsorptive findings, the AGA laboratory pathway supports fecal calprotectin or lactoferrin, Giardia testing, celiac testing with an IgA-deficiency-aware strategy, and assessment for bile acid diarrhea. It does not support routine broad ova/parasite testing without relevant travel or exposure. Its limited population is essential: blood, weight loss, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, or a concerning family history requires broader evaluation. In this pathway, ESR or CRP alone is not the preferred screen to exclude IBD. [AGA chronic watery diarrhea testing](https://gastro.org/press-releases/aga-releases-guideline-on-the-evaluation-of-chronic-diarrhea-1/)

Fecal inflammatory markers are not disease labels. Infection, medication exposure, and other inflammation can affect them; a low result does not overrule a concerning clinical picture or eliminate every small-bowel disorder. Microscopic colitis commonly causes chronic watery diarrhea with a normal-looking colon. Diagnosis requires biopsies, which show lymphocytic or collagenous patterns. Review potentially contributing medicines and other causes. Budesonide is a common treatment after diagnosis; persistent symptoms require reassessment rather than an assumption that a normal colonoscopic photograph ruled out colitis. [NIDDK microscopic colitis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/microscopic-colitis)

Bile acids reaching the colon can stimulate secretion, including after ileal disease/resection or in primary bile acid diarrhea. Pancreatic insufficiency causes maldigestion rather than primary colonic inflammation. Lactase deficiency leaves unabsorbed lactose in the lumen, producing an osmotic load and bacterial fermentation; transient deficiency can follow infection. These mechanisms explain why a single label such as food intolerance is inadequate. Select testing or a monitored treatment trial according to availability and the full presentation, with dietitian help when avoiding foods threatens adequate intake. Pancreatic disease is developed in 05A; blood loss and malignancy assessment follow in 05C.

### Test for celiac disease before removing gluten

Celiac disease is an immune-mediated response to gluten in a genetically susceptible person, with small-intestinal injury and impaired nutrient absorption. Presentation ranges from diarrhea, bloating, and weight loss to iron deficiency, low bone density, fatigue, or dermatitis herpetiformis without prominent diarrhea. Childhood growth failure and delayed puberty can be clues. Family history, type 1 diabetes, and selective IgA deficiency raise suspicion. The diagnosis is not interchangeable with wheat allergy or a patient's report that avoiding bread improves symptoms. [NIDDK celiac presentation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/celiac-disease/symptoms-causes)

Perform serology while the patient is eating gluten. A usual starting strategy is tTG-IgA together with total IgA; deficient IgA requires appropriate IgG-based testing. A negative IgA antibody result is not reassuring when the patient cannot produce adequate IgA. Strong clinical suspicion can justify specialist evaluation despite negative serology. HLA testing is mainly useful in selected uncertain situations: absence of permissive variants makes celiac disease unlikely, whereas their presence does not establish it. [NIDDK professional celiac testing](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/professionals/clinical-tools-patient-management/digestive-diseases/celiac-disease-health-care-professionals)

The ACG adult pathway generally confirms suspected disease with upper endoscopy and multiple duodenal biopsies. Selected nonbiopsy pathways are specialist decisions with specific requirements, not permission to diagnose every positive antibody as celiac disease. Histology must be interpreted with serology, gluten exposure, and other causes of villous injury. Do not recommend a gluten-free diet before the planned diagnostic assessment; doing so can make both serology and biopsy misleading. A patient already avoiding gluten may need a supervised diagnostic plan rather than an improvised challenge. [ACG celiac guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36602836/), [NIDDK celiac diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/celiac-disease/diagnosis)

**Clinical case.** A 37-year-old with type 1 diabetes, iron deficiency, and loose stools has negative tTG-IgA but marked IgA deficiency. Obtain IgG-based evaluation and GI review while maintaining gluten exposure for diagnostic testing; the negative IgA assay does not exclude celiac disease.

Treatment is a sustained gluten-free diet with qualified nutrition counseling, not simply buying products labeled healthy. Discuss wheat, barley, rye, cross-contact, labels, supplements, school/work meals, cost, and nutritionally balanced alternatives. Assess iron and other relevant deficiencies, weight or growth, bone health, and associated conditions. Follow symptoms and serology, but persistent or recurrent symptoms need review of ongoing exposure, the original diagnosis, and alternative disease; symptom improvement alone does not prove mucosal healing. Refractory disease requires specialist assessment rather than ever more restrictive self-treatment. [NIDDK celiac treatment and follow-up](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/celiac-disease/treatment)

### Make a positive IBS diagnosis and explain what it means

IBS is a disorder of gut-brain interaction, not imagined pain and not a synonym for every unexplained bowel symptom. Altered sensation, motility, central processing, and responses after infection can contribute. Stress may modify symptoms without being the sole cause. Describe a sensitive, dysregulated bowel rather than imply that the patient must either have visible inflammation or no illness. IBS itself does not cause GI bleeding or explain progressive weight loss, and it does not remove ordinary cancer-screening obligations. [ACG IBS overview](https://gi.org/guidelines/irritable-bowel-syndrome/)

Use the current Rome V pattern: recurrent but not continuous abdominal pain or discomfort, averaging at least three days monthly during the last three months, with onset at least six months earlier, linked to at least two of defecation, altered stool frequency, and altered stool form. Symptoms should not occur only with menses. Classify constipation-predominant, diarrhea-predominant, mixed, or unclassified bowel habits using abnormal stool patterns, ideally before bowel-altering treatment obscures them. These criteria support a positive diagnosis; they do not replace evaluation of warning findings. [Rome V bowel criteria](https://theromefoundation.org/rome-v-criteria/)

For formal subtyping, assess stool form on days with abnormal bowel movements, off bowel-altering treatment when feasible. IBS-C has more than 25% hard/lumpy and less than 25% loose/watery stools; IBS-D reverses these proportions; IBS-M exceeds 25% in both categories. Do not call every fluctuation between normal and loose stool mixed IBS. [Rome V subtype definitions](https://theromefoundation.org/rome-v-criteria/)

A compatible history, focused examination, and targeted tests can establish IBS without endless exclusionary imaging or routine colonoscopy in a low-risk patient. With diarrhea, assess celiac disease and possible intestinal inflammation as appropriate. Nocturnal diarrhea, bleeding, anemia, weight loss, a palpable mass, significant family history, or a new progressive pattern requires a different investigation. Neither anxiety nor a long symptom history makes new alarm findings harmless. [ACG IBS diagnostic pathway](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000001036)

Begin with goals meaningful to the patient, such as predictable stools, fewer painful episodes, or confidence during work and travel. Regular meals, physical activity, sleep, and a tolerable soluble-fiber trial can help. A low-FODMAP intervention is a structured trial, not permanent avoidance of many food groups: restrict for no more than four to six weeks, then reintroduce and personalize. Stop an ineffective dietary intervention. Restriction may be unsuitable with malnutrition, an eating disorder, or food insecurity. Commercial food-IgG panels should not be used to construct a broad elimination diet. [AGA IBS dietary advice](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-diet-in-irritable-bowel-syndrome-ibs/)

Choose medication for the target symptom and subtype. For IBS-C, an osmotic laxative may improve stool frequency without adequately treating pain. The AGA pathway supports linaclotide and selected use of plecanatide, lubiprostone, or tenapanor; treatment choice also depends on diarrhea risk, indication-specific dosing, age restrictions, and access. Increasing stool frequency is not the same as restoring acceptable overall function. Persistent evacuation difficulty may require pelvic floor assessment rather than another secretagogue. [AGA IBS-C medication guidance](https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S0016-5085%2822%2900390-0/fulltext)

For IBS-D, loperamide can address frequency or urgency, while rifaximin can improve broader symptoms in selected adults; an initial responder with recurrence may be considered for retreatment. Eluxadoline is inappropriate without a gallbladder and has important alcohol, pancreatic, biliary, and hepatic restrictions. Alosetron is reserved for selected women with severe refractory IBS-D and requires attention to ischemic colitis and severe constipation risks. A low-dose tricyclic can target pain, with anticholinergic, sedation, and cardiac effects considered. Treat comorbid anxiety/depression on its own merits rather than assume that an SSRI is an established IBS treatment. [AGA IBS-D medication guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/pharmacological-management-of-irritable-bowel-syndrome-with-diarrhea-ibs-d/), [Current eluxadoline label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=7821bd40-4c84-4984-951b-6436ae20421a)

Gut-directed psychological treatments, including adapted cognitive behavioral approaches or hypnotherapy, can improve symptoms through the gut-brain system. Offering them should not communicate that the medical evaluation was fake or that the symptoms are the patient's fault. Discuss access, preferences, treatment burden, and a follow-up interval. A diary can identify a useful pattern, but continuous symptom surveillance may be counterproductive for some patients. [ACG gut-directed IBS treatment](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000001036)

The adult IBS-D rifaximin course is 550 mg orally three times daily for 14 days; an initial responder with recurrent symptoms may receive up to two repeat courses under the label. This is not the lower-dose travelers' diarrhea regimen or the twice-daily HE recurrence-prevention regimen in 05D. Reassess a changed or alarming symptom pattern before retreatment. [Rifaximin indication-specific prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=c5e8e2fd-7087-4b78-9181-cc259c0be2f1)

### Constipation: distinguish slow passage from difficult evacuation

Constipation is not defined solely by fewer than a daily bowel movement. Hard stools, excessive straining, incomplete evacuation, blockage, and manual assistance matter even when frequency appears normal. Separate a longstanding stable pattern from new or progressive disease. Review medicines, mobility, intake, diabetes, neurologic disease, hypothyroidism when suggested, and prior pelvic procedures. Severe pain, vomiting, blood, weight loss, anemia, or a concerning rectal examination changes the pathway. Do not order a universal endocrine panel or colonoscopy solely because constipation exists; use the presentation and screening status. [NIDDK constipation diagnostic assessment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/constipation/diagnosis)

| Predominant problem | Clues | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hard stool or reduced frequency | Low fiber intake, medicines, slow transit | Correct contributors and trial a suitable bowel regimen |
| Difficult evacuation | Prolonged straining, blockage, digital assistance | Consider anorectal coordination testing and biofeedback |
| Impaction with leakage | Retention, rectal loading, small liquid stools | Treat the impaction before labeling primary diarrhea |
| Possible obstruction | Distention, vomiting, no flatus, severe pain | Urgent evaluation; do not simply add bulk fiber |

A careful digital rectal examination can assess impaction, masses, tone, and the response to simulated evacuation, but a normal examination does not exclude a defecatory disorder. Begin with feasible nutrition, appropriate fluid intake, activity, and a regular unhurried toileting opportunity. Extra fluid beyond adequate hydration is not a cure for every mechanism. Bulk fiber requires tolerability and suitable intake; it is not appropriate treatment for suspected obstruction. Older adults may need help with medication reconciliation, mobility, toilet access, and a written maintenance plan after disimpaction. [NIDDK constipation treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/constipation/treatment)

For adult chronic idiopathic constipation, AGA/ACG supports polyethylene glycol, with fiber and selected alternatives according to response. Bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate can provide short-term or rescue treatment. Senna, magnesium oxide, and lactulose are options with different evidence and tolerability; avoid magnesium accumulation in renal insufficiency. After inadequate OTC treatment, consider linaclotide, plecanatide, prucalopride, or selected lubiprostone. This is not a mandatory sequence requiring every inexpensive agent to fail. Diarrhea, nausea, interactions, renal function, and obstruction precautions affect selection. IBS-C and opioid-induced constipation are distinct prescribing contexts. [AGA/ACG chronic constipation guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10544839/)

A usual adult PEG 3350 starting dose is 17 g orally daily, adjusted to stool response. Long-term clinician-directed CIC treatment differs from a short OTC self-treatment course. Reassess persistent symptoms, bleeding, or worsening pain rather than escalate indefinitely. Suspected impaction requires examination and a disimpaction plan before maintenance, not treatment of overflow liquid stool with loperamide. [AGA/ACG PEG dosing and implementation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10542656/)

Before labeling constipation refractory, the AGA 2026 pathway calls for adequate mechanism-directed treatment, investigation of secondary contributors, and usually anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion and indicated pelvic floor biofeedback. Colonic transit testing characterizes persistent disease. Surgery requires confirmed slow transit, exclusion of an untreated defecatory disorder, and assessment of broader motility and psychosocial factors. One poorly tolerated laxative trial does not justify colectomy. [AGA refractory constipation pathway](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/evaluation-and-management-of-refractory-constipation/)

**Clinical case.** A 59-year-old has soft stools on polyethylene glycol but strains for 30 minutes and uses digital assistance. Evaluate outlet dysfunction with examination and appropriate anorectal testing rather than simply increase laxatives. Confirmed dyssynergia responds to coordination-focused biofeedback; judge improvement by easier evacuation as well as frequency.

### IBD: establish the inflammatory phenotype and its complications

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorders. Crohn's can affect any GI segment and may involve the bowel wall deeply, creating strictures, fistulas, and abscesses. Ulcerative colitis affects the colon, typically with continuous mucosal inflammation beginning at the rectum. Clinical and treated patterns have exceptions, and a single finding such as rectal sparing does not independently settle the diagnosis. Family history and immune/environmental factors contribute; smoking is particularly unfavorable in Crohn's. [NIDDK Crohn's disease framework](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/crohns-disease/definition-facts), [NIDDK ulcerative colitis framework](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/ulcerative-colitis/definition-facts)

| Feature | Crohn's disease | Ulcerative colitis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution | Any GI segment; discontinuous disease can occur | Colon; commonly continuous from the rectum |
| Injury pattern | Transmural consequences are important | Predominantly mucosal inflammation |
| Complications that organize care | Stricture, fistula, abscess, perianal disease | Severe colitis, toxic dilation, bleeding |
| Effect of surgery | Can treat complications; recurrence remains possible | Colectomy removes colonic disease; other consequences still need care |

IBD may present with diarrhea, blood, urgency, pain, fatigue, or weight loss; not all Crohn's disease produces bloody stool. Rectal inflammation can cause tenesmus and repeated small evacuations rather than a large-volume diarrhea. Growth failure, oral ulcers, arthritis, eye inflammation, skin findings, anemia, and nutritional deficits may precede a clear bowel diagnosis. Severe distention, fever, tachycardia, peritoneal signs, obstruction, or suspected abscess needs urgent hospital evaluation. Antimotility drugs should not be used casually in severe active colitis. [NIDDK ulcerative colitis presentation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/ulcerative-colitis/symptoms-causes)

Diagnosis integrates stool studies, blood tests, ileocolonoscopy with biopsies, and appropriate small-bowel imaging. Exclude infection, including C. difficile when appropriate, rather than assume every deterioration is an inflammatory flare. Blood markers and fecal calprotectin help characterize activity but do not independently establish the phenotype. Capsule testing can be unsafe when a stricture is suspected. A biopsy without granulomas does not exclude Crohn's; serologic antibody panels do not replace endoscopic and histologic assessment. [NIDDK Crohn's diagnostic assessment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/crohns-disease/diagnosis)

Treatment has two separate tasks: induction of control and maintenance of steroid-free remission. For mild ulcerative proctitis, rectal mesalamine 1 g/day targets the involved mucosa; left-sided disease often needs rectal plus oral mesalamine, while extensive disease needs oral treatment. Inadequate response may require budesonide MMX or another induction agent; moderate-to-severe disease warrants advanced therapy. Systemic, rectal, and budesonide corticosteroids are not maintenance therapy. Check renal function before mesalamine and periodically during treatment. Follow objective inflammation as well as symptoms: symptomatic improvement alone may leave active mucosal disease. [ACG 2025 ulcerative colitis pathway](https://webfiles.gi.org/GuidelineHighlights/UC-highlights-final.pdf), [Mesalamine suppository renal precautions](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=87de237c-febb-42ca-b2a4-b444396f8640)

For Crohn's, select treatment according to location, inflammatory burden, complications, and progression risk. Ileal-release budesonide can induce control in selected mild ileocecal disease; oral mesalamine is not a general substitute for effective Crohn's therapy. Fistulas, abscesses, and fibrostenotic obstruction need coordinated medical, drainage, and surgical assessment, not repeated steroid courses. Resection does not cure the underlying tendency to recur; plan postoperative assessment and prevention according to risk. [ACG 2025 Crohn's pathway](https://gi.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/ACG_CrohnsHighight2025.pdf)

For moderate-to-severe outpatient Crohn's disease, the current AGA living guideline supports upfront advanced therapy rather than a compulsory step-up period of corticosteroids or immunomodulator monotherapy. Selection depends on previous treatment, efficacy, fistulizing disease, comorbidities, infection and malignancy risk, route, pregnancy plans, and access. Biologics and small molecules are not one interchangeable class. A guideline preference does not erase a product's indication or safety restrictions, and an insurance-required sequence should not be mistaken for the ideal biologic treatment pathway. [AGA current Crohn's living guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/pharmacological-management-of-moderate-to-severe-crohns-disease/)

Acute severe ulcerative colitis needs hospitalization, infection assessment, IV corticosteroids, pharmacologic thrombosis prevention unless contraindicated, and early specialist/surgical involvement. Failure to respond by day three requires assessment for rescue with infliximab or cyclosporine, not an indefinite steroid trial. Perforation, uncontrolled bleeding, or toxic deterioration can demand surgery. Routine broad-spectrum antibiotics or parenteral nutrition solely to rest the bowel are not substitutes for disease-specific care. [ACG severe ulcerative colitis care](https://webfiles.gi.org/GuidelineHighlights/UC-highlights-final.pdf)

Primary care remains important between flares. Reconcile the actual treatment, watch for infection and medication toxicity, update appropriate vaccines before immunomodulation when feasible, and coordinate TB/hepatitis evaluation for relevant agents. Avoid indiscriminate live vaccination during significant immunosuppression. Address skin/cervical prevention, mental health, bone risk, and tobacco use. Colonic IBD surveillance is risk-based and differs from average-risk screening; duration, extent, inflammatory activity, family history, and PSC change the plan. A young chronological age does not eliminate disease-related cancer risk. [ACG IBD preventive care](https://webfiles.gi.org/preventative-care-highlights-fin.pdf)

Track nutrition and growth as well as inflammation. Iron deficiency, B12 deficiency with ileal disease/resection, and inadequate protein or energy intake can coexist with treatment-related risks. Avoid blanket dietary restriction, and involve a dietitian when symptoms, strictures, or malabsorption limit intake. Symptoms in apparent remission can have noninflammatory causes, but check activity before assigning IBS overlap. Pregnancy planning and pediatric growth concerns require coordinated specialty care; patients should not abruptly stop effective therapy on their own because they are considering pregnancy. [NIDDK Crohn's nutrition](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/crohns-disease/eating-diet-nutrition)

### Diverticular disease: decide who needs antibiotics and who needs source control

Diverticulosis means colonic outpouchings; their presence does not prove that chronic abdominal discomfort is diverticulitis. Diverticulitis involves inflammation, typically causing focal lower abdominal pain with variable fever or bowel change. Abscess, perforation, fistula, and obstruction change the problem to complicated disease. Similar symptoms can arise from cancer, IBD, urinary disease, or a pelvic process. Severe illness, peritoneal findings, inability to drink, or an immunocompromised host lowers the threshold for hospital care. Clinical assessment alone can misclassify the episode. [NIDDK diverticular disease mechanisms](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/diverticulosis-diverticulitis/definition-facts)

The ACG 2026 pathway recommends CT at the first presentation to confirm the diagnosis, evaluate alternatives, and characterize complications. Selected stable, immunocompetent, nonfrail outpatients with uncomplicated disease, adequate intake, no systemic inflammatory response, and reliable follow-up can be managed without antibiotics. Antibiotics are appropriate with immunocompromise, frailty, medical complexity, inability to tolerate intake, worsening illness, higher-risk markers/imaging, or inadequate follow-up support. When outpatient antibiotics are indicated, one adult option is amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg orally every 12 hours for four to seven days. Abscess or perforation may require drainage or surgery. [ACG 2026 diverticulitis pathway](https://webfiles.gi.org/GuidelineHighlights/Diverticulitis_Guideline_Highlight.pdf)
