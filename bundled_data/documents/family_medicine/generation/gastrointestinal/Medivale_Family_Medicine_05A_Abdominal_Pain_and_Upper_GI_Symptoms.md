# Medivale Family Medicine

## 05A. Abdominal Pain and Upper GI Symptoms

### First decide whether the patient can safely remain in the office

Abdominal pain is a symptom, not an organ diagnosis. Establish onset, progression, location, radiation, relation to meals or bowel movements, and associated vomiting, bleeding, fever, urinary symptoms, chest symptoms, or reproductive symptoms. Ask about prior surgery, hernias, vascular disease, immunosuppression, alcohol, cannabis, medicines, and possible pregnancy. Sudden maximal pain, syncope, hypotension, peritoneal signs, ongoing bleeding, or marked systemic illness warrants emergency evaluation. Older or immunocompromised patients may have serious disease without impressive fever or tenderness. Adequate analgesia should accompany evaluation rather than be withheld to preserve examination findings. [AAFP acute abdominal assessment](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2023/0600/acute-abdominal-pain-adults.html)

Location organizes possibilities but does not prove them. Epigastric discomfort can arise from the stomach, duodenum, pancreas, biliary tract, or heart. Right upper quadrant pain can reflect gallbladder disease, hepatitis, or a process outside the abdomen. Periumbilical pain that later localizes to the right lower quadrant suggests evolving appendiceal inflammation, but not every patient follows that sequence. Lower abdominal pain may be intestinal, urinary, or pelvic. Distention, vomiting, and failure to pass stool or flatus raise concern for obstruction, especially after surgery or with a hernia. Examine the whole patient, not only the painful quadrant.

Testing should answer a clinical question. Selected studies include a blood count, electrolytes, renal function, liver tests, lipase, urinalysis, and pregnancy testing when applicable. An ECG and cardiac evaluation may be more important than another GI test in an at-risk patient with upper abdominal discomfort. A reassuring single laboratory value is not a substitute for reassessment when the trajectory remains concerning. Arrange a definite return plan for evolving or unexplained pain. Urinary and pelvic causes are developed in 06B and 14B; pediatric surgical presentations are in 13B-13C.

Appendicitis can begin with poorly localized pain before focal right lower quadrant tenderness develops; anorexia, nausea, and fever support but are not required for suspicion. Do not exclude it because diarrhea is present or an early leukocyte count is normal. Suspected disease needs same-day surgical-capable assessment. CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast is usually appropriate in nonpregnant adults; ultrasound or MRI without IV contrast is preferred during pregnancy. A nondiagnostic ultrasound is not a negative diagnosis. Confirmed uncomplicated appendicitis may be treated surgically or, in selected patients after informed discussion, with antibiotics under a defined surgical follow-up plan. This is not an empiric office antibiotic trial for unexplained pain. [ACR right lower quadrant imaging](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69357/Narrative/), [WSES 2025 appendicitis guideline](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.6218)

Mechanical obstruction causes proximal accumulation of gas and fluid, vomiting, and intravascular depletion; previous surgery suggests adhesions, but examine for an incarcerated hernia and consider malignancy. Passing some stool or diarrhea does not exclude partial obstruction. Arrange hospital assessment, bowel rest, IV fluid/electrolyte replacement, and decompression when indicated. CT helps identify the transition, cause, and signs of ischemia. Peritonitis, strangulation, ischemia, or a closed-loop process requires urgent surgical management; a monitored nonoperative trial is appropriate only in selected patients without these findings. Do not prescribe home bulk fiber or escalating laxatives for suspected obstruction. [WSES adhesive obstruction guideline](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13017-018-0185-2)

Ultrasound is the initial imaging study for suspected biliary disease. An inconclusive study does not establish a benign diagnosis; the next examination depends on the suspected process, pregnancy status, and whether inflammation, duct obstruction, or another abdominal lesion is being sought. MRCP evaluates ducts without therapeutic intervention, while cholescintigraphy can clarify selected suspected gallbladder inflammation. CT is useful for many alternative diagnoses. Do not order all of these sequentially without a reason. [ACR right upper quadrant imaging](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69474/Narrative/)

Severe pain with relatively little tenderness can be an early vascular warning, particularly with atrial fibrillation, atherosclerosis, or a low-flow state. A normal lactate does not exclude early mesenteric ischemia. When the clinical suspicion is meaningful, obtain emergency evaluation and CT angiography without waiting for peritonitis or a rising biomarker. A routine unenhanced abdominal CT is not an adequate substitute for a vascular study. [WSES mesenteric ischemia recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13017-022-00443-x)

**Clinical case.** A 76-year-old with atrial fibrillation develops abrupt, persistent central abdominal pain despite a soft abdomen and normal initial lactate. Suspect mesenteric ischemia and arrange emergency assessment; do not wait for peritonitis or biomarker elevation.

### Separate reflux, dyspepsia, and impaired swallowing

**Heartburn** is retrosternal burning; **regurgitation** is return of gastric contents toward the mouth. **Dyspepsia** involves upper abdominal discomfort, early satiation, or postprandial fullness. **Dysphagia** is difficulty swallowing; **odynophagia** is painful swallowing. Distinguish the dominant complaint: food sticking requires a swallowing assessment rather than treatment as severe heartburn.

Reflux occurs when the antireflux barrier permits gastric contents to move into the esophagus. Transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxation, a hiatal hernia, pressure effects, and impaired clearance can contribute. GERD means reflux causing troublesome symptoms or complications, not merely any brief physiologic reflux episode. Possible consequences include erosive injury, stricture, and Barrett's metaplasia. Symptom intensity and mucosal damage do not reliably move together: marked symptoms can coexist with a normal endoscopic appearance. [NIDDK reflux definitions and complications](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/acid-reflux-ger-gerd-adults/definition-facts)

### Treat typical uncomplicated GERD, then reassess the indication

For typical heartburn and regurgitation without alarm findings, the ACG pathway starts with an eight-week trial of a once-daily PPI before a meal. Take conventional PPIs 30-60 minutes before eating rather than reflexively at bedtime. Dysphagia, bleeding, or unexplained weight loss changes the evaluation toward endoscopy; chest pain requires appropriate exclusion of cardiac disease. Persistent symptoms should first prompt confirmation of adherence and administration timing, then appropriate investigation rather than endless empiric escalation. Severe erosive esophagitis, particularly Los Angeles grade C or D, generally requires long-term PPI maintenance or an appropriate antireflux intervention. [ACG GERD guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8754510/)

Lifestyle care should solve the patient's actual problem. Weight reduction can improve reflux when excess weight contributes. Avoid eating near bedtime, and consider head-of-bed elevation for nocturnal symptoms. Identify reproducible food triggers rather than imposing a long universal prohibited-food list that undermines nutrition or adherence. Tobacco cessation has benefits beyond reflux. An occasional antacid may provide short relief but does not replace assessment of frequent, severe, or alarming symptoms. H2-receptor antagonists can help selected patients; renal function and the pattern of use affect prescribing. [NIDDK reflux nutrition](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/acid-reflux-ger-gerd-adults/eating-diet-nutrition), [NIDDK reflux treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/acid-reflux-ger-gerd-adults/treatment)

Treatment response alone does not establish that all symptoms are acid-mediated. In unproven GERD with persistent symptoms and nondiagnostic endoscopy, reflux monitoring off acid suppression can help establish or refute abnormal exposure. In selected patients with already proven GERD, impedance-pH testing on therapy addresses a different question: whether ongoing symptoms correlate with reflux despite treatment. Explain the intended question before ordering the study. Functional heartburn, reflux hypersensitivity, and behavioral disorders need a positive management plan rather than a statement that nothing is wrong. Specialist-directed neuromodulation or behavioral care may be more useful than increasing acid suppression. [AGA personalized GERD assessment](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/personalized-approach-to-the-evaluation-and-management-of-gastroesophageal-reflux-disease-gerd/)

Chronic cough, throat clearing, and hoarseness have many causes. Their presence does not establish laryngopharyngeal reflux, and improvement with a PPI does not prove causation. Evaluate pulmonary, upper-airway, medication, and voice-related contributors. When extraesophageal symptoms occur without typical reflux symptoms, consider objective reflux testing before prolonged empiric PPI treatment. Repeated PPI trials after an unsuccessful adequate course can postpone the actual diagnosis. [AGA extraesophageal reflux advice](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/diagnosis-and-management-of-extraesophageal-gastroesophageal-reflux-disease-gerd/)

At follow-up, record why a PPI is still needed. Some patients can reduce to the lowest effective regimen or stop after symptom control, while others need maintenance or protection from GI bleeding. Do not deprescribe automatically in complicated reflux, Barrett's esophagus, or a patient at substantial bleeding risk. Discuss possible transient rebound symptoms after withdrawal. A decision to stop should rest on absence of an indication, not the assumption that every association reported in observational PPI studies proves drug-induced harm. [AGA PPI deprescribing update](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2021.12.247)

Barrett's esophagus is intestinal metaplasia in an appropriate esophageal segment, not a synonym for cancer. Screening is selective rather than an automatic consequence of any heartburn. Once established, GI follow-up depends on the segment, histology, clinical fitness, and preferences. The AGA 2025 pathway supports surveillance for appropriate nondysplastic Barrett's and daily PPI therapy, but advises against surveillance of a columnar-lined segment shorter than 1 cm with intestinal metaplasia and no neoplasia. A nonspecific irregular junction should not generate an unnecessary lifelong surveillance label. [AGA Barrett's surveillance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/surveillance-of-barretts-esophagus/)

### Investigate dyspepsia according to risk, not a borrowed age rule

The ACG/CAG dyspepsia pathway generally recommends upper endoscopy for new dyspepsia at age 60 or older. Earlier examination may be appropriate with higher gastric cancer risk, including relevant family history or childhood residence in a high-incidence region. For a lower-risk adult younger than 60, test for H. pylori and treat confirmed infection; if negative or still symptomatic afterward, try a PPI. The guideline's case-by-case approach to alarm features in younger dyspepsia patients must not be used to defer evaluation of actual GI bleeding or progressive dysphagia. [ACG/CAG dyspepsia guideline](https://www.cag-acg.org/_Library/clinical_cpgs_position_papers/CAG_CPG_Dyspepsia_AJG_Aug2017.pdf)

Peptic ulcer disease involves a defect in gastric or duodenal mucosa, not just discomfort or microscopic gastritis. H. pylori infection and NSAIDs are the leading causes. Pain can change with meals, but meal response does not reliably distinguish gastric from duodenal ulcers or exclude a dangerous lesion. Ulcers can first appear through bleeding or another complication. Ask specifically about over-the-counter ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, and combination products; patients may not classify them as medicines. Concurrent infection and NSAID exposure can compound risk. [NIDDK ulcer causes and presentation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/peptic-ulcers-stomach-ulcers/symptoms-causes)

An NSAID reduces protective prostaglandin activity, making the mucosa more vulnerable to injury. Medication review should therefore address the cause, not merely add an acid suppressant indefinitely. Consider alternative pain strategies, the necessity and dose of ongoing NSAID exposure, prior ulcer history, and interacting bleeding-risk medicines. Aspirin used for secondary cardiovascular prevention has a different benefit-risk context from self-prescribed aspirin for pain; coordinate interruption and resumption during bleeding rather than give a blanket stop-forever instruction. Anticoagulants can worsen bleeding but do not themselves explain away a lesion. Bleeding management is detailed in 05C.

H. pylori is also linked to chronic gastritis, gastric cancer, and gastric MALT lymphoma. Testing is useful only if the clinician can interpret the result and arrange treatment. Urea breath or stool antigen testing generally assesses active infection; serology cannot reliably distinguish persistent infection from past exposure or confirm eradication. Endoscopic biopsies are appropriate when endoscopy is otherwise indicated. Recent acid suppression, antibiotics, or bismuth can make active-infection testing falsely negative. [NIDDK ulcer diagnostic tests](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/peptic-ulcers-stomach-ulcers/diagnosis)

Under ACG 2024, treat confirmed active H. pylori infection. With unknown susceptibility, a preferred adult regimen is optimized bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days: a standard-dose PPI twice daily, bismuth subcitrate 120-300 mg or subsalicylate 300-524 mg four times daily, tetracycline 500 mg four times daily, and metronidazole 500 mg three or four times daily, all orally. Do not automatically substitute doxycycline or use clarithromycin/levofloxacin without demonstrated susceptibility. Selected patients without penicillin allergy have rifabutin-based or vonoprazan-amoxicillin alternatives. After failure, review adherence, prior agents, and susceptibility rather than repeat the same ineffective course. Confirm cure with an active-infection test at least four weeks after treatment; withhold PPI/PCAB therapy for two weeks and antibiotics/bismuth for four weeks beforehand. [ACG H. pylori treatment and cure testing](https://gi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ACG-Hpylori-Guidelines-Highlights-2024-FINAL.pdf.pdf)

Provide a written dosing schedule and check access to every component. Tetracycline-containing treatment is not a routine pregnancy regimen; severe renal impairment also changes selection. The fixed-dose bismuth/metronidazole/tetracycline product has a separate labeled ten-day schedule and is contraindicated in pregnancy and severe renal impairment: do not confuse its capsule count with the separate-component 14-day regimen. Review interactions, including warfarin, and product-specific alcohol, mineral/antacid, and breastfeeding precautions. [Current fixed-combination prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=5cdacbf5-67be-42eb-bd54-8acc32ea1abc)

**Clinical case.** A negative stool antigen test nine days after treatment while taking a PPI does not confirm eradication. Arrange correctly timed testing and safe medication holds. Persistent dyspepsia alone also does not establish persistent infection.

Ulcer care includes acid suppression and treatment of the cause. When feasible, stop the offending NSAID; if it must continue, select protection with the full bleeding and cardiovascular context in mind. Gastric ulcers require malignancy assessment, with biopsy of suspicious lesions and retrieval of the actual pathology. Repeat endoscopy is particularly important for suspicious appearance despite benign biopsy, persistent symptoms, uncertain cause, or an ulcer not sampled initially. An ulcer still unhealed after 8-12 weeks of appropriate treatment needs repeat biopsy; a benign first sample does not guarantee benign disease. [ASGE peptic ulcer guidance](https://www.asge.org/docs/default-source/education/practice_guidelines/doc-the-role-of-endoscopy-in-the-management-of-patientswith-peptic-ulcer-disease.pdf?sfvrsn=6)

Recurrent disease warrants review of adherence, ongoing exposures, missed infection, and less common causes. Multiple unusual or refractory ulcers with diarrhea can suggest a gastrin-mediated syndrome; gastrin interpretation is complicated by PPIs and should not begin with unsupervised withdrawal. [NIDDK ulcer treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/peptic-ulcers-stomach-ulcers/treatment), [NIDDK Zollinger-Ellison syndrome](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/zollinger-ellison-syndrome)

Sudden severe pain with a rigid abdomen suggests perforation. Hematemesis, melena, faintness, or circulatory compromise suggests bleeding. Recurrent vomiting with retained food and distention can signal outlet obstruction. These complications cannot be managed as routine office dyspepsia. A negative fecal occult blood test does not safely exclude an overt bleeding episode. Avoid treating symptom relief after an antacid as evidence against a surgical or vascular emergency.

When appropriate investigation finds no explanatory structural lesion, functional dyspepsia remains a genuine disorder involving sensation and gastroduodenal function. Explain the diagnosis without dismissing the symptoms. Persistent symptoms after infection treatment and a PPI may justify a low-dose tricyclic or selected prokinetic and psychological therapy, with adverse effects and comorbidity considered. Routine motility testing is not needed for every dyspepsia patient; prominent vomiting or another specific clue can change that decision. [ACG/CAG functional dyspepsia care](https://www.cag-acg.org/_Library/clinical_cpgs_position_papers/CAG_CPG_Dyspepsia_AJG_Aug2017.pdf)

### A swallowing complaint needs its own history and pathway

Ask what happens from the moment a patient tries to swallow. Trouble initiating, coughing during a swallow, nasal escape, or a wet voice suggests an oropharyngeal problem and possible aspiration. Food that seems to stop after swallowing suggests an esophageal problem, although the perceived neck location can mislead. Ask about solids, liquids, progression, impaction, pain, weight change, aspiration, neurologic symptoms, atopy, prior irradiation, and medicines. A diet quietly changed to soft foods can conceal progression rather than demonstrate improvement.

| Reported experience | Important possibilities | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cannot initiate; coughs or chokes | Oropharyngeal dysfunction, including neurologic disease | Assess airway, aspiration, and swallowing safety |
| Intermittent solid-food sticking | Ring, eosinophilic inflammation, other narrowing | Investigate recurrent symptoms even between episodes |
| Progressively worse solid-food swallowing | Stricture or malignancy | Prompt structural evaluation; do not just increase a PPI |
| Solids and liquids both difficult | Motility disease; advanced obstruction remains possible | Use structural assessment and targeted physiology |
| Painful swallowing | Mucosal injury, infection, pills, ulceration | Evaluate exposures and immune status; pain is not a diagnosis |

These patterns change probability rather than establish a lesion. Endoscopy with appropriate biopsies is usually the initial esophageal structural/mucosal assessment. If a mechanical cause is not found, high-resolution manometry evaluates major motor disorders; a timed barium study and selected FLIP examination provide complementary information. A normal endoscopy does not establish normal esophageal motor function. [ACG esophageal physiologic testing](https://webfiles.gi.org/links/virtgrandround/ACG_Clinical_Guidelines__EsophagealPhysiologicTesting.pdf)

An impacted food bolus is urgent. Inability to handle saliva or complete obstruction requires emergent endoscopic care because of aspiration and other risks; comfortable incomplete esophageal impaction still requires urgent removal, generally within 24 hours. Do not tell a patient to force the bolus down with more food. A medication trial must not delay necessary endoscopy. After removal, investigate the underlying narrowing or inflammation instead of treating the event as a one-time dietary accident. [ASGE food impaction guideline](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/publications/guidelines/management-of-ingested-foreign-bodies-and-food-impactions)

Eosinophilic esophagitis is chronic immune-mediated inflammation that can cause solid-food dysphagia, impactions, and fibrosis. Patients may compensate by slow chewing, prolonged meals, or avoiding certain textures. Diagnosis requires esophageal dysfunction, at least 15 eosinophils per high-power field on biopsy, and evaluation for other explanations. Obtain at least six biopsies from two levels; a reassuring gross appearance does not eliminate the need for sampling when suspected. PPI failure is not a required diagnostic step. Treatment can use a PPI, swallowed topical steroid, or an empiric elimination diet selected with the patient and appropriate nutrition support. Dilation treats narrowing but must accompany anti-inflammatory care. Selected patients need dupilumab. Continue effective maintenance and assess symptoms, endoscopy, and histology rather than symptoms alone. [ACG eosinophilic esophagitis pathway](https://webfiles.gi.org/links/committees/DigitalCommsAndPubs/ACG-EOE-Guidelines-Highlights-2025.pdf)

Achalasia combines impaired junctional relaxation with abnormal or absent peristalsis. Dysphagia to liquids and solids, regurgitation of retained material, and weight loss may be mislabeled as refractory reflux. Endoscopy helps exclude obstruction or malignant pseudoachalasia; high-resolution manometry defines the motor diagnosis and subtype, with timed barium assessment providing complementary information. Definitive options include pneumatic dilation, Heller myotomy, and POEM, selected according to subtype, patient factors, and expertise. Botulinum toxin can suit patients unable to undergo durable therapy. Post-treatment reflux and recurrent dysphagia still need follow-up. [ACG achalasia guideline](https://gi.org/guideline/diagnosis-and-management-of-achalasia/)

Odynophagia raises different concerns. Ask about immunosuppression, HIV, cancer therapy, systemic steroids, and pills taken with little water or immediately before lying down. Candida, HSV, and CMV require different treatment; neither oral findings nor symptoms alone reliably identify every esophageal infection. Drug injury, reflux injury, and immune-mediated inflammation can resemble infection. Significant pain, inability to maintain intake, or high-risk immune status warrants timely evaluation and often endoscopic sampling rather than indiscriminate empiric treatment. [AGA immune and infectious esophageal dysfunction](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/esophageal-dysfunction-due-to-disordered-immunity-and-infection/)

### Approach nausea and vomiting as a mechanism and a safety problem

Vomiting is forceful expulsion, usually with retching; regurgitation is passive return, and rumination is recurrent return of recently ingested food through a different behavioral mechanism. The distinction changes investigation. Establish acute versus persistent or episodic symptoms, relation to meals, weight trajectory, abdominal pain, distention, headache, vertigo, and hydration. Consider pregnancy, metabolic or endocrine illness, infection, CNS disease, and medication effects, not only gastric disease. Exclude obstruction before assigning a chronic motility or gut-brain diagnosis. Opioids, incretin therapies, dopamine agonists, and other drugs deserve a careful temporal review. [UEG/ESNM chronic nausea and vomiting consensus](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ueg2.12711)

Disposition depends on the consequences as well as the cause. Inability to retain fluids or essential medicines, oliguria, syncope, altered mental status, severe pain, hematemesis, significant electrolyte disturbance, or suspected obstruction needs urgent care. Assess glucose and ketones when diabetes or metabolic decompensation is plausible. Repeated gastric losses can produce chloride and potassium depletion with metabolic alkalosis, but mixed illness can alter that pattern. Replacement should reflect volume status, renal function, and measured deficits. An antiemetic does not make an obstructed, septic, or ketoacidotic patient safe for discharge.

For a stable patient, use a medication timeline that includes dose escalation, supplements, alcohol, and cannabis. New vomiting after an incretin dose change is a reason to assess treatment tolerance, not sufficient evidence for permanent diabetic gastroparesis. Similarly, vomiting during cannabis use is not automatically cannabinoid hyperemesis. Its characteristic recurrent episodes in the setting of sustained cannabis exposure require exclusion of relevant alternatives and assessment of response to cessation. Hot bathing may be reported but is not diagnostic. Offer nonjudgmental cessation support and follow-up; repeated temporary emergency relief without addressing exposure is incomplete care. [AGA cannabinoid hyperemesis update](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2024.01.040)

Gastroparesis requires symptoms compatible with gastric retention, objectively delayed emptying, and no mechanical outlet obstruction. Diabetes, prior surgery, and medicines are relevant, but symptoms alone cannot establish delayed emptying. The AGA 2025 pathway favors a four-hour gastric emptying study over a two-hour or shorter examination. Coordinate testing around relevant medication effects and glucose status. Treat nutrition and the underlying contributor; selected patients may use metoclopramide or erythromycin. Invasive pyloric procedures are not routine first-line care and require specialist selection in medically refractory disease. [AGA gastroparesis guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-guidance-on-the-management-of-gastroparesis/)

Practical nutrition care includes smaller, more frequent meals and textures that are better tolerated, with attention to adequate energy, protein, and fluids. A small-particle approach and adjustment of fat and fiber may help; overly restrictive self-prescribed diets can worsen weight loss. Dietitian input is useful when intake is falling. Severe nutritional failure may require enteral support rather than repeated promises that an antiemetic will restore adequate intake. [AGA gastroparesis patient guidance](https://patient.gastro.org/gastroparesis/)

Metoclopramide is not a harmless long-term nausea medicine. The current U.S. tablet label warns of potentially irreversible tardive dyskinesia, with risk increasing with cumulative exposure. Use the shortest appropriate course and reassess; in diabetic gastroparesis, avoid total metoclopramide treatment beyond 12 weeks. Stop and evaluate new abnormal movements. Prior tardive dyskinesia is a contraindication, and suspected obstruction, perforation, or bleeding precludes stimulation of GI motility. Renal function, other neurologic medicines, age, and additional label precautions influence dosing and selection. [FDA 2026 metoclopramide label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/017854s071lbl.pdf)

For adult diabetic gastroparesis, the tablet label's usual oral dose is 10 mg 30 minutes before meals and at bedtime, maximum 40 mg/day. With creatinine clearance below 60 mL/min, including dialysis, its diabetic-gastroparesis dose is 5 mg twice daily, maximum 10 mg/day. Older adults generally start lower, and hepatic impairment/CYP2D6 interactions have separate adjustments. Do not borrow the higher renal-adjusted GERD dose from the same label for gastroparesis. [FDA indication-specific dosage tables](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/017854s071lbl.pdf)

### Distinguish a gallbladder stone from an infected obstructed duct

Gallstones can be incidental or cause pain and complications. Biliary pain is typically a steady episode in the right upper abdomen or epigastrium rather than repeated seconds-long spasms. Nausea and radiation toward the back or shoulder can occur. Persistent pain, fever, jaundice, or systemic illness changes the concern from uncomplicated episodes to inflammation, obstruction, or infection. A stone seen on imaging does not explain every bloating or reflux complaint. Ultrasound evaluates gallbladder stones and associated findings; blood tests and further duct imaging address complications. [NIDDK gallstone presentation](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gallstones/symptoms-causes), [NIDDK gallstone diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gallstones/diagnosis)

| Clinical problem | Dominant process | Direction of care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symptomatic gallbladder stones without inflammation | Intermittent outflow obstruction | Analgesia and planned surgical assessment when stable |
| Acute cholecystitis | Gallbladder inflammation, usually cystic-duct obstruction | Hospital assessment, supportive care and early source treatment |
| Choledocholithiasis | Stone in the common bile duct | Assess obstruction and likelihood; select EUS/MRCP or therapeutic ERCP |
| Acute cholangitis | Biliary infection, commonly with obstruction | Resuscitation, antibiotics and urgent drainage planning |

Uncomplicated symptomatic stones generally warrant discussion of cholecystectomy, whereas truly asymptomatic incidental stones usually do not need treatment. Explain what symptoms should trigger immediate return while awaiting surgery. A nonoperative plan requires an explicit reason and follow-up, not an assumption that every later attack will remain uncomplicated. [NIDDK gallstone treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gallstones/treatment)

Cholecystitis is supported by persistent compatible pain, local inflammatory findings, systemic inflammation, and imaging. Neither a Murphy sign nor a single laboratory result is definitive. The WSES pathway favors early laparoscopic cholecystectomy when feasible, with antibiotics and supportive treatment according to the presentation. Age alone is not a reason to substitute drainage for surgery. Patients who truly cannot undergo surgery may require gallbladder drainage and a reassessed longer-term plan. Acalculous inflammation is particularly relevant in seriously ill patients; absence of stones does not exclude it. [WSES acute cholecystitis guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7643471/)

ERCP carries procedural risk and is mainly therapeutic, not a screening test for every abnormal liver panel. In the ASGE 2019 pathway, high-risk findings that can directly prompt ERCP are a duct stone on imaging, ascending cholangitis, or total bilirubin above 4 mg/dL together with duct dilation. With the gallbladder present, the specified dilation threshold is above 6 mm. Bilirubin above 4 alone does not meet that combined criterion. Intermediate likelihood usually calls for EUS, MRCP, or appropriate operative imaging rather than diagnostic ERCP. [ASGE suspected duct-stone pathway](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/publications/guidelines/asge-guideline-on-the-role-of-endoscopy-in-the-evaluation-and-management-of-choledocholithiasis)

Do not require the complete fever-pain-jaundice triad before recognizing cholangitis. Hypotension, confusion, or organ dysfunction indicates a particularly dangerous presentation. Begin hospital resuscitation and antibiotics while arranging source control. ASGE favors endoscopic drainage when feasible and decompression within 48 hours, with more urgent action according to instability and the clinical context. The time window is not permission to delay treatment of shock. Drainage alone may be the initial procedure when the patient cannot tolerate more extensive stone treatment. [ASGE cholangitis guidance](https://www.asge.org/home/resources/publications/guidelines/asge-guideline-on-the-management-of-cholangitis)

### Recognize pancreatitis and avoid treatment that creates additional harm

Acute pancreatic inflammation often causes persistent severe epigastric pain, sometimes radiating to the back, with nausea and vomiting. Evaluate the presentation with pancreatic enzymes and selected imaging, while looking for gallstones, alcohol exposure, medication contributors, hypertriglyceridemia, and other causes. A modest isolated lipase increase has other explanations and should not override an incompatible clinical picture. Conversely, delayed presentation or particular etiologies can complicate enzyme interpretation. Ultrasound and MRCP answer etiologic questions that are different from CT assessment of pancreatic complications. [NIDDK pancreatitis diagnosis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/pancreatitis/diagnosis)

The usual diagnostic framework requires two of three findings: characteristic pain, lipase or amylase at least three times the laboratory upper limit, or characteristic imaging. Imaging is not mandatory merely to complete all three when the diagnosis is already secure. Early severity assessment concerns organ dysfunction and the clinical course, not how many times above normal the lipase reaches. Persistent organ failure is central to severe disease. [Revised Atlanta classification](https://doi.org/10.1136/gutjnl-2012-302779)

The ACG 2024 pathway uses moderate, reassessed fluid resuscitation, generally favoring lactated Ringer's, with particular caution in heart or kidney disease. Reserve early CT for uncertainty or a course that fails to improve, rather than routinely staging every patient on arrival. In mild disease, begin oral low-fat solid food within 24-48 hours as tolerated; do not wait for enzyme normalization. Use enteral rather than routine parenteral nutrition when oral intake is inadequate. Antibiotics do not treat sterile pancreatic inflammation or sterile necrosis. Biliary pancreatitis with cholangitis warrants ERCP within 24 hours; uncomplicated biliary pancreatitis does not require routine urgent ERCP. Mild gallstone pancreatitis generally calls for cholecystectomy before discharge. [ACG acute pancreatitis pathway](https://webfiles.gi.org/links/journals/AJG-Clinical-Guidelines-Highlights-Acute-Pancreatitis-2024-FINAL.pdf)

After discharge, explain the cause, recurrence prevention, and warning signs rather than simply record that the lipase improved. Coordinate gallstone treatment, alcohol-use care when relevant, medication review, triglyceride management, and nutrition. Persistent symptoms or recurrent unexplained attacks require specialist reassessment. New jaundice, weight loss, or a changed pain pattern should not automatically be labeled another benign attack. [NIDDK pancreatitis treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/pancreatitis/treatment)

Chronic pancreatitis can impair both exocrine digestion and endocrine function. Persistent pain, steatorrhea, weight loss, and diabetes warrant assessment of structure, nutrition, and pancreatic function rather than reliance on serum lipase, which may not be elevated. Provide smoking and alcohol cessation support, address pain thoughtfully, and monitor nutritional consequences. [GeneReviews pancreatic disease overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK190101/)

For suspected exocrine insufficiency, fecal elastase on a semisolid or solid stool is a useful initial test: below 100 micrograms/g supports insufficiency, while 100-200 is indeterminate. A response to empiric enzymes does not establish the diagnosis, and CT/MRI assesses pancreatic structure rather than digestive function. Pancreatic enzyme replacement treats established insufficiency and is taken during meals and snacks. AGA's adult starting approach is at least 40,000 USP lipase units per meal and half that with snacks, then adjustment to intake and response; check the chosen product's dosing limits. Monitor weight and fat-soluble vitamins. Avoid an extremely low-fat diet that worsens malnutrition. Enzymes do not replace evaluation of a new obstructing lesion or automatically treat pancreatic pain. [AGA exocrine pancreatic insufficiency advice](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/epidemiology-evaluation-management-exocrine-pancreatic-insufficiency/)

### Close the encounter with a plan that can actually be followed

Document diagnosis, uncertainty, treatment duration, pending results, and follow-up responsibility. Schedule H. pylori cure testing and PPI reassessment; track dysphagia referrals and nutrition; clarify biliary source control. Bleeding, progressive swallowing difficulty, persistent vomiting, systemic illness with jaundice, syncope, or worsening pain requires reassessment despite a previously reassuring diagnosis.
