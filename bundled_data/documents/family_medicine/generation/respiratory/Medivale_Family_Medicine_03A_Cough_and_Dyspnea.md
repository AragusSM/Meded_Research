# Medivale Family Medicine

## 03A. Cough and Dyspnea

Cough and dyspnea are presenting problems, not diagnoses. Asthma care follows the GINA pathway in 03B; COPD, respiratory infections, and sleep disorders are developed in 03C-03E.

### Decide whether the patient is safe before naming the symptom

Cough clears secretions and foreign material. Dyspnea is the subjective experience of disturbed respiratory demand, mechanical work, gas exchange, or breathing perception. Consider extrapulmonary causes: anemia, HF, ischemia, metabolic acidosis, neuromuscular weakness, obesity, and deconditioning. Anxiety can amplify breathlessness without excluding organic disease.

Assess respiratory rate, pulse, pressure, temperature, oxygenation, mentation, speech, and work of breathing. Establish onset, rest versus exertional symptoms, and lost function. Observe posture, retractions, accessory muscles, chest symmetry, air entry, and perfusion. Inability to finish a phrase requires immediate assessment.

**Urgent warning signs** include severe or rapidly worsening distress, altered consciousness, cyanosis, stridor, a quiet chest with poor air movement, hemodynamic instability, significant hemoptysis, or new chest pain with concerning features. Consider anaphylaxis, upper-airway obstruction, pneumothorax, PE, ACS, pulmonary edema, severe infection, and respiratory failure. Stabilization and emergency transfer take priority over definitive office testing. An existing asthma or anxiety diagnosis does not explain every future episode.

Use onset and trajectory to focus the differential. Sudden pleuritic breathlessness differs from gradual loss of walking capacity. Nocturnal symptoms may reflect asthma, HF, reflux, or sleep-disordered breathing; orthopnea suggests, but is not exclusive to, congestion. Ask about bleeding, fever, weight loss, edema, choking, dysphagia, and thrombotic risks. See 02A, 02D, and 02F for chest pain, HF, and PE.

### Use cough duration without losing the clinical context

| Adult cough duration | Initial reasoning |
| --- | --- |
| Acute, under 3 weeks | Often respiratory infection; first exclude pneumonia and urgent alternatives. |
| Subacute, 3-8 weeks | Postinfectious cough is common; reassess pertussis, asthma, exposures, and other clues. |
| Chronic, over 8 weeks | Use a structured assessment, medication review, imaging, and appropriate lung-function testing. |

These CHEST adult categories describe duration, not severity or a mandatory waiting period. A patient with alarm features needs investigation now, even after only a few days. Establish whether the cough is one uninterrupted illness or repeated episodes with symptom-free intervals. Persistent daily cough in children uses a shorter threshold, generally over 4 weeks, and a pediatric pathway rather than the adult algorithm. [Adult cough classification](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6689094/) [Pediatric cough guidance](https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012-3692%2820%2930325-1/fulltext)

Characterize wet versus dry cough, sputum amount, hemoptysis, timing, triggers, associated wheeze, nasal symptoms, reflux, and exposure history. Ask about smoking, vaping, occupational dusts/fumes, home dampness, infectious contacts, travel, and immune compromise. Cough with meals can suggest aspiration; a sudden onset during eating or play raises concern for a foreign body. A persistent focal wheeze is not automatically generalized asthma. Clubbing, recurrent focal infections, or substantial daily sputum suggests a structural or suppurative process that needs further evaluation.

In children through age 14 with daily cough beyond 4 weeks, CHEST recommends chest radiography and spirometry when developmentally feasible. Chronic wet cough in an otherwise well child without specific warning signs can represent **protracted bacterial bronchitis**, not adult-type reflux cough. An initial 2-week antibiotic course targets usual respiratory bacteria, selected for local susceptibility and pediatric dosing; persistent wet cough after reassessment can need a further 2 weeks. Failure after 4 weeks of appropriate treatment warrants investigation for underlying disease rather than repeated blind prescriptions. Cough with feeding, growth failure, clubbing, recurrent pneumonia, or suspected aspiration/foreign body calls for earlier targeted assessment. Do not empirically apply the adult asthma/rhinitis/reflux sequence to every child. [CHEST pediatric cough recommendations](https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012369220303251/pdf)

In uncomplicated acute bronchitis, exclude pneumonia and give an expected course and safety net. Purulent-looking sputum does not by itself prove bacterial infection. Routine antibiotics are not recommended for uncomplicated bronchitis. Abnormal vital signs or focal consolidation increase concern for pneumonia, but older or immunocompromised patients may present atypically. A reassuring rule derived from otherwise healthy adults should not override a frail patient's deterioration. [CDC outpatient respiratory assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-care/adult-outpatient.html)

A resolving infection may leave heightened cough sensitivity for several weeks. Explain why cough can outlast fever or nasal symptoms, while defining what would change the plan. New fever, hypoxemia, breathlessness, blood, weight loss, or failure to improve calls for reassessment. Paroxysms, post-tussive vomiting, or a whoop can suggest pertussis, but adults may lack the classic sound. Testing, treatment timing, and contact protection belong to the infection pathway; do not assume every prolonged cough needs another nonspecific antibiotic course. [Pertussis-associated cough guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6859243/)

### Review medicines and exposures early

ACE-inhibitor cough may begin after prolonged tolerance and reflects mediator sensitivity, including bradykinin, not pulmonary fluid. When appropriate, stop the culprit and substitute treatment for its indication. Recovery takes weeks and sometimes up to 3 months; indefinite antitussives leave the cause untreated. [ACE-inhibitor cough review](https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012-3692%2815%2952845-6/abstract)

Review drugs causing bronchospasm, aspiration, lung toxicity, or fluid retention, including nonselective beta blockers, sedatives, eye drops, OTC products, and recent changes. ACE-inhibitor cough with tongue/lip swelling or stridor requires emergency angioedema assessment.

Occupational symptoms may appear after a shift. Weekend/vacation improvement supports the diagnosis, but slow recovery does not exclude it. Obtain task-specific exposures: work can cause or exacerbate asthma or another lung disorder. Serial measurements and specialist assessment may outperform one office examination. [CDC work-related assessment](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/asthma/hcp/diagnosing/index.html)

### Investigate persistent cough in a deliberate sequence

For chronic adult cough, examine the upper airway, chest, and relevant systemic findings; review medicines and exposures; obtain chest radiography and spirometry when feasible. Additional tests depend on the findings. A normal radiograph does not exclude every cancer, bronchiectasis, or interstitial process, but routine CT for every patient with normal examination and radiography is not the next step. Persistent unexplained symptoms, warning features, or failure of a reasoned treatment trial can justify more advanced imaging or referral. [ERS chronic cough recommendations](https://publications.ersnet.org/lookup/pmid/31515408)

Common contributors include rhinitis/rhinosinusitis, asthma or eosinophilic airway inflammation, reflux, and irritants; several may coexist. Choose a targeted trial, define meaningful sustained improvement, and reassess. Simultaneous treatments obscure response and add unnecessary medication.

When eosinophilic airway disease is plausible, a defined ICS trial with technique review and reassessment at about 4 weeks is more informative than an indefinite inhaler prescription. Normal spirometry with low type-2 inflammatory markers and no other airway features argues against indiscriminate ICS treatment. CBC and, where available, FeNO can support this assessment; neither substitutes for the clinical differential. Persistent unexplained cough despite appropriate trials merits respiratory referral even with a normal radiograph. [BTS chronic-cough assessment and treatment trials](https://www.brit-thoracic.org.uk/document-library/clinical-statements/cough-in-adults/chronic-cough-in-adults/)

Nasal blockage, sneezing, discharge, or a compatible examination supports upper-airway treatment, such as an intranasal corticosteroid for appropriate rhinitis. Throat clearing or a sensation of drainage alone does not establish the entire diagnosis. Cough-variant asthma may have little audible wheeze; objective variability or further airway testing helps distinguish it. Nonasthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis can cause steroid-responsive cough without the characteristic airflow variability or airway hyperresponsiveness of asthma. Specialist assessment may clarify persistent uncertainty.

Reflux can coexist with cough without being its cause. Heartburn, regurgitation, meal timing, and documented reflux help select treatment. Weight management when indicated, avoiding late meals, and other appropriate reflux measures can be useful. Do not reflexively give prolonged high-dose PPI therapy for isolated cough without typical reflux features or supporting evaluation. Treatment of acid does not address every nonacid or laryngeal mechanism. [CHEST reflux-cough guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6026249/)

Persistent cough despite treatment can reflect cough hypersensitivity, with talking, scents, or temperature changes provoking an urge to cough. Recheck the diagnosis and completed trials before labeling it refractory. Specialist cough-control/speech therapy or selected neuromodulators may help; review drug risks and availability. Assess sleep, urinary leakage, voice, social effects, and syncope. [BTS chronic cough statement](https://www.brit-thoracic.org.uk/document-library/clinical-statements/cough-in-adults/chronic-cough-in-adults/)

### Interpret oxygenation alongside ventilation and oxygen delivery

Pulse oximetry estimates the percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen; it does not measure the work of breathing, carbon dioxide, hemoglobin concentration, or tissue perfusion. A patient with anemia may have a normal saturation but reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. A tired patient can develop hypercapnia despite an acceptable saturation on supplemental oxygen. Conversely, an unexpectedly low reading may reflect a real emergency or measurement error; verify the signal while assessing the patient.

Motion, cold extremities, poor circulation, nail products, and device limitations can distort readings. Oximetry may overestimate saturation in some people with darker skin pigmentation. Interpret trends, the examination, and clinical context; obtain confirmatory assessment when the number conflicts with a concerning presentation. A consumer device reading must not overrule severe symptoms. [FDA oximetry limitations](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/products-and-medical-procedures/pulse-oximeters)

Ordinary two-wavelength oximetry cannot exclude carbon monoxide poisoning. Ask about combustion exposures and other affected household members when headache, dizziness, confusion, or breathlessness occurs in a compatible setting. Safely remove the patient from exposure and arrange emergency assessment, co-oximetry, and treatment. A normal displayed saturation is not reassuring. [CDC carbon monoxide guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

Blood gases help when ventilation, acid-base status, or severe gas-exchange failure is in question; they are not required for every mild cough. The oxygen target depends on the disease and situation. Acute asthma and COPD with hypercapnic risk do not share an interchangeable target, and no oxygen prescription should replace evaluation of an unexplained new requirement. The patient's trajectory and response to treatment remain central.

### Build a focused chronic dyspnea evaluation

Quantify stable dyspnea using an activity such as walking, climbing a flight, dressing, or carrying groceries. Establish baseline and rate of decline. Ask about chest pressure, palpitations, orthopnea, edema, wheeze, cough, weight change, bleeding, weakness, and exposures. Examine cardiopulmonary findings, perfusion, anemia clues, and neuromuscular function. Several modest problems can combine to limit function.

Initial testing commonly includes CBC, basic chemistry, ECG, chest radiography, and spirometry, selected to the presentation. BNP/NT-proBNP and echocardiography are appropriate when HF is suspected; interpret them with the limitations in 02D. Chest radiography is usually the first imaging study for chronic noncardiovascular dyspnea of uncertain origin. CT, diffusion testing, exercise assessment, or specialist referral follows a clinical question rather than a desire to order every available study. [ACR imaging guidance](https://acsearch.acr.org/docs/69448/Narrative/)

Unexplained limitation warrants consideration of pulmonary vascular or early interstitial disease, occult cardiac dysfunction, dysfunctional breathing, and deconditioning. Selected patients benefit from cardiopulmonary exercise testing. A normal radiograph does not establish anxiety; breathlessness alone is not an oxygen indication. Explain remaining uncertainty and follow-up.

### Read pulmonary-function tests as measurements, not labels

FEV1 is the volume exhaled in the first second of a forced maneuver; FVC is the total forced exhaled volume. Their ratio evaluates the pattern of emptying. Inspect acceptability, repeatability, effort, and the flow-volume loop before interpreting a diagnostic label. Cough during the maneuver, early termination, leakage, or incomplete inspiration can produce misleading results. Repeat a poor-quality study rather than building a lifelong diagnosis on it. [ATS/ERS spirometry quality standard](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6794117/)

| Test pattern | Interpretation and next question |
| --- | --- |
| Reduced FEV1/FVC | Supports airflow obstruction; identify the disease and relevant diagnostic framework. |
| Reduced FVC with preserved ratio | Suggests possible restriction, air trapping, or an inadequate maneuver; TLC is needed to confirm restriction. |
| Reduced ratio and reduced TLC | Supports combined obstructive and restrictive physiology. |
| Reduced diffusion capacity | Consider parenchymal, vascular, emphysematous, or hematologic contributors in context. |

For general interpretation, use appropriate reference limits rather than treating 80% predicted as the universal boundary of normal. A low FVC alone is not a diagnosis of pulmonary fibrosis. COPD has its own post-bronchodilator diagnostic framework, discussed in 03C. The ERS/ATS laboratory bronchodilator-response criterion is an increase in FEV1 or FVC greater than 10% of its predicted value. GINA retains a different asthma diagnostic criterion; name the framework instead of mixing denominators. [ERS/ATS interpretive standard](https://publications.ersnet.org/highwire_display/entity_view/node/590225/full)

For example, FEV1 increasing from 1.80 L to 2.10 L is a 0.30-L improvement. Relative to baseline, this is 16.7%. If predicted FEV1 is 3.00 L, it is 10.0% of predicted. The same measurements therefore produce different percentages; 10.0% is not greater than 10%. A numerical response supports a clinical interpretation, but does not by itself distinguish asthma from every other airway disorder.

Record the reference equation and interpret it in context. ATS recommends moving away from race-specific equations toward race-neutral interpretation; do not infer that symptoms or reduced function are normal because of a racial category. Reference uncertainty and changes in reporting can affect longitudinal comparisons. An apparently normal result does not make a patient's loss of function irrelevant. [ATS reference-equation statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10112445/)

### A persistent-cough follow-up example

**Clinical Case.** A 58-year-old has dry cough for 10 weeks after a cold, a normal radiograph, and no weight loss, fever, or dyspnea. Lisinopril began several months earlier. Review its indication, choose a suitable substitute, record cough severity, and reassess. Both infection and medication may contribute.

Persistent cough warrants review of time off the drug, nasal/reflux symptoms, exposures, and spirometry. Hemoptysis or declining exercise capacity requires renewed investigation rather than repeated antibiotics.
