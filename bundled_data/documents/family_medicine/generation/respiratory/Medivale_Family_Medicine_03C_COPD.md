# Medivale Family Medicine

## 03C. COPD

GOLD 2026 guides COPD diagnosis and treatment; ATS and ERS/ATS guidance informs oxygen, rehabilitation, and ventilation. Initial treatment grouping, spirometric grade, and follow-up adjustment answer different questions.

### Confirm persistent obstruction in a compatible clinical setting

COPD reflects interacting airway, alveolar, developmental, and exposure-related abnormalities. Small-airway remodeling increases resistance; emphysema reduces elastic recoil and expiratory airway support. Air trapping and hyperinflation increase work and limit exercise even before severe resting hypoxemia. Mucus and impaired clearance contribute to cough and infection.

Chronic bronchitis describes cough with sputum for at least 3 months in each of 2 consecutive years, after excluding another explanation. It can occur without confirmed COPD, and COPD can occur without meeting that symptom definition. Emphysema describes destruction and enlargement of distal air spaces, an anatomic process rather than a symptom score. These concepts overlap in many patients but are not interchangeable diagnostic tests.

Assess progressive exertional limitation, cough, sputum, respiratory illnesses, and attacks against the prior functional baseline. Record smoking, secondhand smoke, vaping, biomass, dust/fumes, childhood respiratory history, and familial lung/liver disease. COPD can occur without smoking; smokers may have other causes of dyspnea. [NHLBI diagnostic assessment](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/copd/diagnosis)

Confirm COPD-related obstruction with quality-assured **post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70**, interpreted with symptoms and exposures. Borderline or discordant results need repeat testing or further assessment, not an automatic permanent label. General laboratory reference limits and GOLD's disease-specific fixed ratio serve different purposes. Bronchodilator responsiveness can occur in COPD; its presence does not prove asthma, and its absence on one occasion does not exclude asthma. [Current GOLD/GLI spirometry statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13084305/)

A low FEV1 quantifies impairment after the diagnosis is established. GOLD spirometric grades use FEV1 percent predicted: at least 80%, 50-79%, 30-49%, and below 30%. These grades do not by themselves select the entire inhaler regimen or determine oxygen eligibility. A chest radiograph can help identify alternative disease; it cannot replace spirometry. Emphysema on CT or symptoms with a preserved ratio may still warrant assessment and prevention, but do not alone satisfy the spirometric COPD definition.

Consider asthma, HF, bronchiectasis, interstitial or pulmonary vascular disease, anemia, and deconditioning. Examine oxygenation, effort, air entry, wheeze, crackles, edema, perfusion, nutrition, and muscle loss. Clubbing or persistent focal findings warrant investigation beyond COPD.

Offer **alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency testing to all adults with COPD**, not just young never-smokers or a particular ancestry. Unusually early emphysema, limited exposure, basilar disease, and familial liver/lung disease heighten suspicion. AAT normally limits protease-mediated tissue injury; deficiency can damage the lungs, while some abnormal variants also injure the liver. Interpret the level with appropriate confirmatory testing and consider family counseling. Augmentation therapy is a specialist decision, not a substitute for smoking cessation or ordinary COPD care. [Alpha-1 testing recommendations](https://alpha1.org/ats-ers-standards/)

### Assess symptoms and exacerbation risk independently

Track breathlessness/activity with mMRC and broader symptoms with the Chronic Airways Assessment Test (CAAT, formerly CAT). Document attacks, treatment, admissions, and recovery, including urgent-care antibiotic/steroid courses missing from the chart. A reported bad cold may have been an exacerbation.

In GOLD 2026, at least one moderate or severe exacerbation in the preceding year identifies the higher-exacerbation-risk group E for initial maintenance treatment assessment. With no such events, symptoms distinguish A from B; mMRC at least 2 or CAAT at least 10 indicates greater burden. An exacerbation is important even when the patient feels reasonably well today. [GOLD 2026 assessment update](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/KEY-CHANGES-GOLD-2026-10Nov2025.pdf)

| Initial group | Main treatment orientation |
| --- | --- |
| A: lower symptom burden, no moderate/severe event in prior year | Bronchodilator treatment, often long-acting unless symptoms are very occasional. |
| B: greater symptom burden, no moderate/severe event in prior year | Dual long-acting bronchodilation with LABA plus LAMA when suitable. |
| E: at least one moderate/severe event in prior year | Exacerbation prevention, usually LABA plus LAMA; assess whether an ICS-containing regimen is appropriate. |

Reassess response, adherence, device, exposure, and comorbidities after treatment starts. Persistent dyspnea on one long-acting bronchodilator generally calls for dual LABA/LAMA treatment, not automatic addition of ICS. Continuing attacks require the exacerbation-prevention pathway, including review of eosinophils and whether ICS is indicated. Do not mechanically restart the initial grouping process at every visit. [GOLD 2026 report](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/GOLD-REPORT-2026-v1.3-8Dec2025_WMV2.pdf)

### Match inhaled treatment to the problem being treated

LABAs and LAMAs provide sustained bronchodilation through different mechanisms. Combining them often improves symptoms and exercise tolerance more than either alone in symptomatic COPD. Retain an appropriate rapid reliever for breakthrough symptoms; an extra dose of a maintenance product is not automatically an approved rescue treatment. Review antimuscarinic effects such as dry mouth, urinary difficulty, or eye exposure, and beta-agonist effects such as tremor and tachycardia. [ATS pharmacologic guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7193862/)

ICS is an add-on for selected exacerbation-prone patients, not routine monotherapy for COPD and not a treatment chosen solely because FEV1 is low. Blood eosinophils help estimate likely preventive benefit: benefit is generally greater around or above 300 cells/microliter and smaller below 100, with a continuum between them. Interpret counts with exacerbation history, previous treatment, and infection risk. If asthma coexists, preserve appropriate ICS-containing asthma treatment rather than applying a COPD-only withdrawal rule. [GOLD ICS considerations](https://goldcopd.it/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/GOLD_Pocket_Guide_2026.pdf)

When ICS is indicated, triple LABA/LAMA/ICS therapy may be appropriate. Balance benefit against pneumonia, candidiasis, dysphonia, and higher-dose systemic effects. Important adverse effects warrant supervised reassessment; withdrawal can increase attacks, especially with higher eosinophils. A count alone neither mandates triple therapy nor establishes safe withdrawal.

For continuing attacks despite optimized inhaled treatment, specialist-selected options can include roflumilast, long-term macrolide treatment, or newer phenotype-directed therapy. Roflumilast is not an acute bronchodilator; weight loss, GI effects, and psychiatric history matter. Long-term azithromycin requires attention to hearing, QT, interactions, resistance, and possible nontuberculous mycobacterial disease. Current selected biologic options do not mean every eosinophilic COPD patient should receive an injection. Use current product criteria and confirm the reason for ongoing events before adding treatment.

Avoid chronic systemic steroids for stable COPD and consider cumulative harm from bursts. Observe technique and assess inspiratory ability, dexterity, cognition, cost, and refills. Nebulizers may aid delivery but do not increase intrinsic potency or replace teaching. See 03B for devices; COPD drug roles differ.

### Treat prevention, function, and comorbidity as core care

Offer nonjudgmental tobacco counseling, behavioral treatment, and suitable cessation medication. Quitting can slow further damage and reduces other risks despite irreversible emphysema. Address home/work exposures and current age/risk-based influenza, COVID, pneumococcal, RSV, and other vaccination schedules (01E).

Pulmonary rehabilitation combines exercise, education, and support for symptomatic patients and after exacerbation admission; it is not limited to transplant candidates. Address transport, frailty, responsibilities, and availability through feasible center-based or supported programs, and confirm enrollment. [ATS rehabilitation guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10449064/)

Assess weight/nutrition, strength, falls, mood, sleep, bone, and cardiovascular health; investigate new chest pain/edema independently. Separate lung-cancer screening from symptomatic evaluation. Lung-volume reduction/transplant requires physiologic/anatomic selection beyond FEV1. Integrate symptom relief, goals, and advance planning. [NHLBI longitudinal treatment](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/copd/treatment)

### Distinguish long-term oxygen from acute respiratory support

Long-term oxygen improves survival in selected patients with **severe chronic resting hypoxemia**, not simply breathlessness or a low FEV1. ATS defines this as room-air PaO2 at most 55 mm Hg or SpO2 at most 88%, or PaO2 56-59 mm Hg or SpO2 89% with edema, hematocrit at least 55%, or P pulmonale on ECG. Confirm stable disease, prescribe appropriate flow/duration, and reassess. For this indication, use at least 15 hours daily. Moderate resting desaturation (SpO2 89-93% without qualifying features) does not carry the same routine indication. [ATS home oxygen guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7667898/)

Exertional or nocturnal oxygen questions require their own assessment. Oxygen started during an acute illness is not automatically lifelong. The ATS guideline cites reassessment at 1-4 weeks and again at 12-16 weeks after oxygen is started for a COPD exacerbation; stable chronic prescriptions also need reassessment, commonly at 60-90 days after initiation. Recheck rest and exertion requirements. Teach fire prevention, avoidance of smoking/open flames, equipment use, and trip prevention. Discuss portable supply and actual activities. A prescription is not an adequate discharge plan unless equipment and instruction are available. [ATS oxygen reassessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7667898/)

For an acute exacerbation with hypoxemia or hypercapnic risk, controlled oxygen generally targets saturation **88-92%**, with blood-gas reassessment when indicated. Oxygen-induced hypercapnia involves altered ventilation-perfusion relationships and other mechanisms, not merely removal of a simplistic hypoxic drive. Do not withhold needed oxygen from a critically hypoxemic patient; titrate and monitor while arranging appropriate care. A normal saturation on oxygen does not demonstrate adequate ventilation. [ERS/ATS acute respiratory support](https://www.thoracic.org/statements/resources/cc/niv-guidelines.pdf)

### Evaluate an exacerbation and its alternatives

An exacerbation is acute worsening of breathlessness and/or cough/sputum beyond the usual variation. Determine onset, prior baseline, infectious symptoms, sputum change, exposures, medication access, and recent treatment. Assess vital signs, saturation, speech, work of breathing, mentation, hydration, and perfusion. Seek pneumonia, HF, ischemia, arrhythmia, PE, and pneumothorax when the presentation warrants it. These can trigger or coexist with an exacerbation rather than being mutually exclusive diagnoses.

Severe distress, altered consciousness, new cyanosis, poor perfusion, rising oxygen requirement, inability to manage at home, or failure of initial treatment requires urgent monitored evaluation. Obtain radiography, ECG, gases, or other tests according to severity and the differential. Outpatient testing should not delay transfer. A patient who cannot clear secretions or safely use rescue treatment may need more support even if a numerical score appears modest.

Give a short-acting inhaled beta agonist, with a short-acting antimuscarinic when appropriate, using an effective delivery method. For an indicated moderate/severe exacerbation, the usual adult systemic course is prednisone-equivalent 40 mg daily for 5 days, with patient-specific review. Oral treatment is often adequate when the patient can take and absorb it; IV treatment is not automatically superior. Avoid routine methylxanthines and unnecessary prolonged steroid courses. [GOLD acute pharmacologic recommendations](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/GOLD-REPORT-2026-v1.3-8Dec2025_WMV2.pdf)

Antibiotics are selective, not obligatory for every flare. GOLD 2026 supports treatment when increased sputum purulence accompanies another feature such as increased dyspnea, sputum volume, or fever; prior positive exacerbation cultures and invasive/noninvasive mechanical ventilation are additional indications. Choose using prior microbiology, local resistance, allergy, recent antibiotics, and severity. Usual classes include amoxicillin-clavulanate, a tetracycline, or a macrolide. Frequent/severe events or resistant-organism risk justify sputum culture. An uncomplicated outpatient course is generally no longer than 5 days, with reassessment for nonresponse; pneumonia or resistant/complicated infection needs its own regimen. [GOLD acute antibiotic recommendations](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/GOLD-REPORT-2026-v1.3-8Dec2025_WMV2.pdf)

In acute or acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory acidosis, bilevel NIV reduces work of breathing and intubation risk when appropriate. ERS/ATS recommends it for COPD-related respiratory acidosis with pH at most 7.35; persistent PaCO2 above 45 mm Hg and tachypnea despite initial therapy support the decision. Reassess gases, effort, and mental status promptly in a monitored setting with an intubation rescue plan. Inability to protect the airway, severe instability, or deterioration may require invasive support rather than a prolonged unsuccessful mask trial. Chronic hypercapnia without acidosis is not by itself the same acute NIV indication. [ERS/ATS NIV guideline](https://www.thoracic.org/statements/resources/cc/niv-guidelines.pdf)

### Close the recovery loop

After an exacerbation, confirm acute-drug stop dates, maintenance treatment, oxygen needs, and a worsening-symptom plan. Review early, especially after severe illness, checking technique, supply, smoking support, rehabilitation, and steroid/antibiotic harms. Prolonged failure to regain baseline requires a fresh differential.

**Clinical Case.** A 71-year-old has post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC 0.59 and FEV1 62% predicted, no recently treated exacerbation, and substantial walking limitation. Assess dual bronchodilation, delivery, rehabilitation, and comorbidity; the spirometric grade alone determines neither symptom burden nor oxygen need. A later treated attack warrants renewed prevention review despite a quiet follow-up examination.
