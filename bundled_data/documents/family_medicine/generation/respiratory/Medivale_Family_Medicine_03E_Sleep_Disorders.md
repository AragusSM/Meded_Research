# Medivale Family Medicine

## 03E. Sleep Disorders

Adult apnea testing and PAP care follow AASM pathways; ATS guides obesity hypoventilation. Insomnia care centers on CBT-I, with VA/DoD guidance for selected medication use. Pediatric testing and treatment require their own criteria.

### Start with the sleep problem, not a sleeping pill

Sleep complaints reflect insufficient opportunity, disruption, difficulty sleeping despite opportunity, or misaligned biological timing; mechanisms can coexist. Distinguish the worker without enough time to sleep from the patient awake despite ample time in bed. **Sleepiness is a tendency to fall asleep; fatigue is reduced energy or endurance.**

Reconstruct workdays and free days: bedtime, sleep attempt/onset, awakenings, final waking, naps, and sleep opportunity. Include shifts, caregiving, commute, bedroom, light, caffeine, alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, pain, nocturia, and mood. Review sedating antihistamines, stimulants, opioids, glucocorticoids, and other substances. A diary clarifies patterns obscured by estimated nightly hours.

With permission, ask a partner/caregiver about pauses, gasping, movements, or unusual behavior; absence of an observer does not exclude disease. Examine weight, pressure, airway, cardiopulmonary, and neurologic findings. Test suspected medical causes rather than ordering broad panels. Wearables support history but do not replace diagnostic studies.

Safety comes first when a person reports unintended sleep during driving or other hazardous activity. Ask about recent near-misses and crashes, sedatives, and sleep deprivation. Warn an actively sleepy driver not to drive, arrange safer transport and work modifications, and expedite evaluation. A questionnaire score or apnea index cannot independently establish driving fitness. Local reporting rules and commercial-driver standards need separate review; a stimulant prescription is not a driving clearance. [ATS sleepiness and driving guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5446185/)

### Recognize obstructive apnea without relying on a stereotype

In obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), the upper airway repeatedly narrows or closes during sleep despite ongoing effort to breathe. Recurrent arousals fragment sleep; oxygen fluctuations, pressure changes, and sympathetic activation contribute to physiologic stress. Obesity and a crowded airway increase risk, but craniofacial anatomy, enlarged lymphoid tissue, aging, and other factors also matter. A normal BMI does not exclude OSA. [NHLBI sleep-apnea overview](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea)

Clues include habitual snoring, witnessed pauses, waking with choking, unrefreshing sleep, morning headache, nocturia, and unintended dozing. Some patients describe insomnia or fatigue rather than obvious sleep attacks. Explore resistant hypertension, AF, HF, stroke, and relevant endocrine disease without treating every association as proof that apnea caused the condition. Examine nasal obstruction, tonsils, tongue, jaw, and neck; neither an airway photograph nor a neck measurement establishes the diagnosis.

STOP-Bang estimates OSA risk; Epworth measures subjective sleepiness. Neither replaces objective testing, and low sleepiness does not exclude OSA. Asymptomatic screening differs from investigating witnessed apnea or significant sleepiness. [AASM adult diagnostic guideline](https://www.aasm.org/resources/clinicalguidelines/diagnostic-testing-osa.pdf)

### Choose a study that can answer the clinical question

Polysomnography (PSG) measures sleep using brain, eye, and muscle signals while recording breathing, oxygenation, cardiac rhythm, and other relevant channels. It relates events to actual sleep time and stage. A home sleep apnea test (HSAT) usually measures fewer signals. It is a clinical test ordered and interpreted in a care pathway, not a diagnosis generated by an app's automatic score. [AASM home-test position statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6287732/)

| Testing situation | Appropriate direction |
| --- | --- |
| Uncomplicated adult with convincing risk of moderate/severe OSA | Technically adequate HSAT or PSG within a supervised diagnostic pathway. |
| Significant cardiorespiratory disease, suspected hypoventilation, respiratory muscle weakness, chronic opioids, prior stroke, or severe insomnia | Prefer PSG; the problem may extend beyond uncomplicated obstruction. |
| Negative, inconclusive, or technically inadequate HSAT with ongoing concern | Obtain PSG rather than declaring OSA excluded. |
| Routine insomnia without evidence of another sleep disorder | Clinical assessment first; PSG is not an obligatory insomnia test. |

After negative PSG with persistent suspicion, review the differential, recording quality, position/stage exposure, and need for a second study. A sufficient split-night recording can combine diagnosis and titration. [AASM test-selection recommendations](https://www.aasm.org/resources/clinicalguidelines/diagnostic-testing-osa.pdf)

The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) counts apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep. A respiratory disturbance index can also include respiratory effort-related arousals; verify the report's definition. Many home tests report a respiratory event index (REI) based on monitoring rather than EEG-confirmed sleep time. Wakefulness in the denominator can underestimate the event burden. Oxygen nadir, sustained hypoxemia, event type, and sleep distribution also matter; the index is not a complete description of disease.

Typical adult categories are mild at 5 to fewer than 15 events/hour, moderate at 15 to fewer than 30, and severe at 30 or more. Diagnosis generally requires at least five predominantly obstructive events/hour with qualifying symptoms or associated clinical conditions, or at least 15 even without symptoms. Relevant symptoms include unintended sleepiness, unrefreshing sleep, insomnia, and gasping/choking or witnessed pauses. Scoring and coverage rules can differ, so identify the method rather than treating AHI and every reported RDI/REI as identical. Pediatric interpretation does not use these adult thresholds. [AASM diagnostic criteria and testing framework](https://www.aasm.org/resources/clinicalguidelines/diagnostic-testing-osa.pdf)

### Treat OSA and verify that treatment reaches the whole night

Positive airway pressure supports the airway mechanically. AASM recommends PAP for adults with OSA and excessive sleepiness and supports its use for impaired sleep-related quality of life or coexisting hypertension. CPAP provides fixed pressure; APAP adjusts within prescribed limits. Either may suit ongoing uncomplicated adult OSA. Home APAP initiation is an option for selected uncomplicated patients with education and follow-up, not a default for hypoventilation or complex central events. Routine bilevel treatment is not inherently superior. [AASM PAP guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374094/)

Review objective PAP use, leak, residual events, symptoms, and removal during sleep. Address mask/skin problems, congestion, dryness, claustrophobia, pressure intolerance, and supplies with humidification, interface changes, awake practice, or prescribed adjustments. Do not lower pressure merely to conceal a poor seal.

Address electricity, housing, cost, language, travel, and work barriers with early troubleshooting. Insurance adherence thresholds do not establish whole-night protection. Persistent sleepiness warrants reassessing sleep duration, efficacy, medicines, mood, and other sleep disorders before adding wake-promoting treatment. [AASM PAP follow-through](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374094/)

A custom titratable mandibular advancement appliance is an alternative for selected adults who prefer it or cannot tolerate CPAP. Coordinate a qualified dentist and sleep clinician, monitor dental/occlusal effects, and confirm effectiveness with follow-up testing; reduced snoring alone does not prove the obstruction resolved. An over-the-counter mouthpiece is not equivalent to a fitted prescribed appliance. [AASM/AADSM oral-appliance guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4481062/)

Weight management, appropriate positional treatment, and reduction of alcohol or unnecessary sedatives can complement airway therapy. Selected patients may need assessment for upper-airway surgery, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, or bariatric treatment; anatomy, event type, severity, prior treatment, and current device criteria determine suitability. These are not interchangeable referrals. Do not leave clinically important apnea untreated while a patient tries to lose weight.

Tirzepatide under the Zepbound label is an option for **moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity**, alongside reduced-calorie nutrition and physical activity. It is not approved for every cause of snoring or central apnea. Review GI effects, pancreatic/gallbladder risks, interactions, and the contraindication with personal/family medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2. Stop when pregnancy is recognized. Oral hormonal contraception requires a nonoral method or added barrier protection for 4 weeks after initiation and after each dose escalation. Section 04D reviews weight-treatment selection and safety; use the current tirzepatide label for titration. This indication does not mean any weight-loss drug can immediately replace PAP. [Current tirzepatide labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=487cd7e7-434c-4925-99fa-aa80b1cc776b)

Reassess after major weight change, symptom recurrence, or a non-PAP intervention when results would alter management. Routine repeat testing is not required for every stable, asymptomatic patient doing well on PAP. Do not stop therapy merely because weight fell or a partner no longer hears snoring; confirm whether meaningful disease persists. [AASM longitudinal-testing guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8314660/)

### Distinguish central apnea from obesity hypoventilation

Central apnea reflects reduced or absent respiratory effort during an event, rather than continued effort against an obstructed airway. HF, opioids, altitude, neurologic disease, and treatment-emergent events are relevant settings. Mixed mechanisms can coexist. Evaluate the underlying disorder, medicines, gas exchange, and study pattern; a low overnight oxygen trace alone cannot distinguish obstruction, central apnea, and sustained hypoventilation.

Current AASM central-apnea treatment is cause-specific and specialist-directed. CPAP can be appropriate in selected settings; bilevel therapy without a backup rate is not routine CSA treatment. Adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) is a distinct mode, not simply a more comfortable CPAP setting. Current guidance discusses selected use and experienced-center monitoring in reduced-EF HF, but this does not override device contraindications. [AASM central-apnea guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40820608/)

ResMed ASV instructions contraindicate use in chronic symptomatic HF with LVEF at most 45% and moderate/severe predominantly central apnea. Specialist selection must respect the exact device contraindication. [Current ASV device safety information](https://www.resmed.com/en-us/health-professionals/products/cpap/machines/aircurve-11-asv/)

Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS) requires obesity (adult BMI at least 30 kg/m2), sleep-disordered breathing, and awake arterial hypercapnia, generally PaCO2 at least 45 mm Hg, after excluding another cause. Obstructive apnea is common but does not alone explain every case. With low/moderate suspicion, serum bicarbonate below 27 mmol/L makes OHS unlikely; at least 27 prompts arterial CO2 assessment. A raised bicarbonate is not diagnostic, and high suspicion warrants direct assessment rather than reassurance from a screening value. [ATS OHS guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680300/)

Stable ambulatory OHS with severe OSA generally starts with CPAP under the ATS pathway; persistent hypoventilation, inadequate response, or a different phenotype can require NIV. Acute hypercapnic failure needs monitored respiratory care, not a routine home-test order. For a patient hospitalized with respiratory failure and suspected OHS, ATS suggests discharge with NIV pending outpatient diagnostic testing and PAP titration, ideally within 3 months. This interim support does not replace completing the evaluation. Sustained substantial weight reduction can improve OHS, but oxygen alone does not replace ventilation. [ATS OHS recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680300/)

### Diagnose insomnia in the context of a full day

Insomnia involves trouble initiating or maintaining sleep, or waking too early, despite adequate opportunity and circumstances, with meaningful daytime consequences. Chronic insomnia typically occurs at least three nights weekly for at least three months. Shorter episodes can still require care, but do not justify an automatic long-term hypnotic prescription. Insomnia can coexist with depression, pain, menopause, or apnea and may need direct treatment while those problems are addressed. [NHLBI insomnia overview](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia)

Explore rumination, clock watching, long naps, and prolonged wakefulness in bed as perpetuating factors. Assess pain, reflux, dyspnea, substances, withdrawal, and medication timing. Reduced sleep need with increased energy, pressured speech, or impulsivity suggests mania rather than insomnia; assess mood and immediate safety.

Compare time in bed, estimated sleep, and daytime function using a diary; questionnaires track response. Reserve PSG for suspected apnea, movements, parasomnia, or another disorder. Typical insomnia requires neither PSG before treatment nor an expectation of uninterrupted sleep.

### Use CBT-I as an active treatment, not a handout

Multicomponent CBT-I treats conditioned arousal, scheduling, beliefs, and behaviors perpetuating wakefulness. It is the primary adult treatment; sleep-hygiene advice alone is insufficient. Structured brief treatment, telehealth, or evidence-based digital care can extend access when suitable; confirm participation. [AASM behavioral-treatment guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)

| CBT-I element | What it is intended to change |
| --- | --- |
| Stimulus control | Rebuild the link between bed and sleep rather than prolonged wakeful struggle. |
| Individualized time-in-bed adjustment | Consolidate sleep, then adjust the sleep window as sleep becomes more efficient. |
| Cognitive work | Reduce catastrophic predictions and effortful monitoring that maintain arousal. |
| Relaxation and schedule support | Reduce activation and support a workable, consistent sleep-wake pattern. |

Prescribe and monitor time-in-bed restriction, adapting it for hazardous sleepiness, seizures, bipolar illness, pregnancy/postpartum demands, and caregiving. Track daytime function; sleep loss must not create harm or turn unavoidable circumstances into apparent nonadherence. [AASM behavioral-treatment precautions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)

The AASM 2026 combination-treatment recommendations are **conditional, with low-certainty evidence**: starting CBT-I plus medication is favored over medication alone, but not over CBT-I alone. Early symptom priorities, adverse effects, access, and preferences inform a shared decision. This is not a requirement to add a drug to every CBT-I course or withhold behavioral treatment until all medicines have been stopped. [AASM combination-treatment recommendations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41975142/)

### Make any insomnia prescription specific and reviewable

Define the medication's target, expected benefit, duration, review, and stop plan. VA/DoD 2025 supports selected low-dose doxepin, dual orexin antagonists, or nonbenzodiazepine receptor agonists, and advises against routine benzodiazepines, trazodone, diphenhydramine, and antipsychotics for chronic insomnia. [VA/DoD medication framework](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Provider-Summary_final_20250422.pdf)

Review age, falls, cognition, apnea/hypoventilation, substance use, pregnancy/lactation, other sedatives, interactions, and next-day tasks. Use current product instructions; drugs differ in sleep-onset versus maintenance effects, duration, and contraindications. Low-dose insomnia doxepin is not interchangeable with antidepressant-dose tricyclic treatment. Avoid adding multiple nighttime depressants to compensate for untreated apnea or an unrealistic sleep schedule.

For sleep-maintenance difficulty, labeled oral **doxepin tablets are 3-6 mg once nightly**, not a usual antidepressant dose. Start at 3 mg from age 65 or with hepatic impairment; the adult maximum is 6 mg/day. Take within 30 minutes of bed and not within 3 hours of a meal. Do not use with an MAOI or within 2 weeks of one, untreated narrow-angle glaucoma, or severe urinary retention; it is ordinarily not recommended with severe sleep apnea. Avoid alcohol and assess additive sedation. Reassess persistent insomnia after 7-10 days rather than escalating indefinitely. Pregnancy and breastfeeding need separate review; the label advises against breastfeeding. [Doxepin insomnia labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=1bec1223-5239-4eb6-a9e8-62444106d2c0)

Eszopiclone, zaleplon, and zolpidem carry a boxed warning about serious complex sleep behaviors, including activities performed without full awareness. Such behavior requires stopping the implicated medicine and prompt prescriber contact; a prior episode with these drugs is an important contraindication to prescribing them again. Alcohol avoidance does not eliminate the risk. Next-day impairment can occur without a remembered nighttime event. [FDA complex-sleep-behavior warning](https://www.fda.gov/media/123819/download)

Orexin antagonists reduce wake-promoting signaling and are not interchangeable with benzodiazepines. They can still cause next-day impairment, unusual sleep behaviors, or sleep paralysis. Daridorexant is contraindicated in narcolepsy, and respiratory disease and interactions require review. A different mechanism does not make a hypnotic risk-free. Do not abruptly discontinue an established dependence-forming regimen without a supervised taper when indicated. [Daridorexant prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=3a2d1503-b816-40c9-9fac-7e03c5a3bcef)

### Identify restless legs before treating it as insomnia

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) produces an urge to move, often with uncomfortable leg sensations, that begins or worsens at rest, improves with movement, and predominates in the evening/night. Ask what makes it stop. A painful sustained calf cramp, persistent neuropathic burning, joint pain, or generalized drug-related restlessness is not automatically RLS. Diagnosis is clinical; repetitive limb movements on PSG alone do not establish RLS or prove that they cause daytime impairment.

Assess iron status, blood loss, pregnancy, kidney disease, family history, caffeine/alcohol, and aggravating medicines. Antihistaminergic, serotonergic, or antidopaminergic drugs can contribute, but do not abruptly stop essential psychiatric treatment. Review alternatives with the treating clinician. Untreated apnea may worsen the sleep problem and needs attention in parallel. A normal hemoglobin does not exclude an iron-related contribution to RLS.

The AASM 2025 pathway calls for regular ferritin and transferrin-saturation testing in clinically important RLS, preferably in the morning after avoiding iron supplements and iron-containing foods for 24 hours according to collection instructions. Its adult consensus thresholds support oral or IV iron when ferritin is at most 75 ng/mL or transferrin saturation is below 20%; in the 75-100 ng/mL ferritin range, the recommended route is **IV rather than oral**. These are RLS-specific consensus thresholds, not a universal definition of anemia or an instruction to give iron indefinitely. Identify blood loss or another cause, account for inflammation, and repeat studies and symptoms to assess response and avoid overload. Pediatric and dialysis thresholds differ. [AASM RLS guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/)

For example, an adult with convincing RLS has ferritin 92 ng/mL and transferrin saturation 16%, with no anemia. This is not a reason to dismiss iron-related RLS or merely repeat oral iron indefinitely: discuss the IV pathway after assessing the cause, contraindications, and monitoring. IV formulations are not interchangeable; AASM strongly supports ferric carboxymaltose in appropriately selected adults, while evidence for other products differs. [AASM formulation-specific recommendations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39324694/)

Current treatment emphasizes iron assessment and selected gabapentinoids rather than routine dopamine agonists. The AASM recommends against standard use of pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine, and levodopa because of augmentation risk. Earlier onset, spread to other body regions, or a shorter rest-to-symptom interval during dopamine treatment can represent augmentation, not simple undertreatment. Escalating the dose can worsen it; arrange an individualized supervised transition rather than abrupt withdrawal. The current RLS Foundation algorithm likewise emphasizes gabapentinoids and careful augmentation management. [Current RLS Foundation algorithm](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42203073/)

Gabapentin-class treatment requires renal adjustment and assessment of sedation, falls, misuse risk, and breathing. Respiratory depression risk increases with opioids, other depressants, lung disease, and older age. Do not convert a first-line class recommendation into a blanket prescription for a frail hypercapnic patient. Refractory disease, pregnancy, childhood, advanced kidney disease, or a proposed opioid regimen merits specialist-directed selection and monitoring. [FDA gabapentinoid respiratory warning](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/neurontin-gralise-horizant-gabapentin-and-lyrica-lyrica-cr-pregabalin-drug-safety-communication)

### Recognize sleep at the wrong biological time

Circadian disorders misalign internal sleep timing with required activity. Delayed phase permits normal sleep on a later schedule but impairs early sleep/waking; advanced phase reverses this pattern. A diary covering work/school and free days, sometimes with actigraphy, distinguishes phase displacement from insomnia or insufficient opportunity.

Light and melatonin are timing signals as well as influences on sleep. Treatment depends on the desired phase change and actual rhythm; wrong timing can shift the clock in the wrong direction. An individualized schedule, carefully timed light, and selected timed melatonin can be appropriate for specific intrinsic circadian disorders. This does not establish melatonin as routine treatment for all chronic insomnia or justify escalating an arbitrary bedtime dose. [AASM intrinsic circadian-disorder guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4582061)

For shift symptoms, assess protected sleep opportunity and schedule linkage. Plan sleep windows, darkness/quiet, timed light/caffeine, rest, and safe transport. Full adaptation may be impossible with rotation. Coordinate light/melatonin with the next shift and commute; never sedate before driving. Persistent problems warrant sleep/occupational assessment. [NHLBI circadian treatment principles](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/circadian-rhythm-disorders/treatment)

### Evaluate persistent sleepiness and unusual nocturnal behavior

After verifying adequate sleep and treating disruption, persistent unintended sleep can suggest a central hypersomnolence disorder. Narcolepsy may include sleep attacks, paralysis, vivid sleep-transition experiences, and fragmented nighttime sleep. Cataplexy is brief emotion-associated loss of muscle tone with preserved awareness, not a usual syncopal episode. Type 1 narcolepsy is linked to deficient orexin/hypocretin signaling; absence of cataplexy does not exclude other narcolepsy phenotypes. Sleep paralysis alone is not diagnostic. [NIH narcolepsy overview](https://health.nih.gov/health-topics-a-z/narcolepsy)

Specialists may use overnight PSG followed by a multiple sleep latency test (MSLT). AASM preparation includes documenting adequate habitual sleep for about two weeks and ensuring other sleep disorders are effectively treated. Sleep deprivation, shift timing, untreated apnea, and sedating/alerting or REM-modifying substances can invalidate interpretation. A supervised medication plan is essential; sudden withdrawal can alter results or endanger the patient. MSLT assesses sleep propensity, whereas a maintenance of wakefulness test asks a different question. [AASM MSLT/MWT protocols](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8726366/)

Narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia require specialist-guided treatment, safety planning, and school/work accommodations. Selected wake-promoting or oxybate-based treatments have different effects and risks; an agent useful for sleepiness may not treat cataplexy. Modafinil can reduce hormonal contraceptive effectiveness, and reproductive plans, cardiovascular effects, interactions, and misuse/respiratory risks influence selection. Do not use a wake-promoting drug to conceal chronic insufficient sleep or ineffective apnea treatment. [AASM hypersomnolence-treatment guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8636351/)

Newer treatments can act on the deficient signaling itself. FDA has announced oveporexton for adult type 1 narcolepsy; it activates orexin receptors, unlike insomnia medicines that block them. Check current labeling, interactions, scheduling, and availability before prescribing. A type 1 indication does not establish treatment for every cause of sleepiness. [FDA orexin-directed treatment announcement](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-drug-treat-full-range-narcolepsy-type-1-symptoms)

Punching, kicking, or injurious dream enactment suggests RBD, but apnea-related arousals, drugs, seizures, and other parasomnias can mimic it. Arrange assessment, often video PSG; remove dangerous objects and protect the patient and partner from falls/injury. Confirmed isolated RBD warrants neurologic counseling/follow-up, not a presumed existing neurodegenerative diagnosis. Individualize treatment around falls/apnea risks. [AASM RBD guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071384/)

### Adapt the assessment and close the follow-up loop

Children with snoring and disrupted breathing may show hyperactivity, poor school performance, enuresis, or growth concerns instead of adult-type dozing. Use pediatric sleep/airway assessment and criteria; adult HSAT pathways and AHI cutoffs should not be imported. Adenotonsillar, obesity-related, craniofacial, and neuromuscular causes require different care, and treatment does not eliminate the need to reassess residual symptoms. Pediatric home testing is not routinely recommended by the active AASM position. [AASM pediatric home-test position](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5612636/)

Pregnancy can reveal or worsen apnea, insomnia, and RLS; do not dismiss witnessed pauses or dangerous sleepiness as inevitable pregnancy symptoms. Review blood pressure, iron, medication safety, and postpartum follow-through with the relevant teams. In older adults, pain, nocturia, circadian change, polypharmacy, and neurocognitive disease often coexist; prioritize function and safety rather than escalating sedatives to achieve an arbitrary nightly number.

**Clinical Case.** A 39-year-old rotating-shift technician sleeps roughly five hours and nearly crashes driving home. Gasping persists on vacations despite eight hours of sleep. Protect the commute, improve sleep opportunity, and arrange apnea assessment; shift work alone does not explain the presentation.

**Clinical Case.** A 66-year-old widower lies awake increasingly after illness, has evening leg discomfort relieved by walking, and takes a nightly OTC antihistamine. Address insomnia behaviors, possible RLS/iron status, and medication burden. Follow sleep, daytime function, adverse effects, and treatment completion rather than substituting another sedative.
